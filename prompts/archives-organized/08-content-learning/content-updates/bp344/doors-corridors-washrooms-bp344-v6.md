# Doors, Corridors & Washrooms — BP 344 / RA 9442

**Updated:** 2024 Revised IRR (effective August 8, 2024) · Source: Official Gazette Vol. 120 No. 28

**The ALE tests you to** identify specific dimensions, apply them to described building scenarios, and spot violations in accessible washroom floor plans.

---

## Start Here: The One Dimension That Explains Everything

Before any specific rule, you need one mental anchor — the single dimension that drives almost every number in this article.

A standard wheelchair is **800 mm wide** and **1,100 mm long**. To complete a full 180° turn, it needs a diameter of **1,800 mm** — roughly the width of a standard car.

That turning circle is the governing logic behind BP 344. Every major dimension you will memorize traces back to it: the toilet cubicle is 1,800 × 1,800 mm because a wheelchair must complete a full rotation. The corridor turnabout is 1,800 × 1,800 mm for the same reason. The approach space on both sides of a door is 1,800 × 1,800 mm because that is the space needed to maneuver a wheelchair to a door, stop, reach the handle, and pull it open.

When you encounter a number and you aren't sure why it exists, ask: *what movement is this making possible for a person in a 1,800 mm turning circle?* The answer will make the number unforgettable.

**Wheelchair reference dimensions**

| Dimension | Value |
|---|---|
| Chair width | 800 mm |
| Chair length | 1,100 mm |
| Full turning circle diameter | **1,800 mm** |
| Comfortable vertical reach | 700–1,200 mm above floor |
| Comfortable horizontal reach | 500–900 mm |
| Minimum clear width needed | 900 mm |
| Knee/leg clearance under fixtures | 700 mm |

> **Most-tested:** The 1,800 mm turning circle diameter. Every major dimension in the washroom section traces back to it.

## The Three Laws — What Each One Does

Three laws govern accessible design. They work together but test differently. Know what each one is actually for before you memorize anything.

*Batas Pambansa Blg. 344 (1982)* is the technical standard — the one that says **what to build**. It specifies the exact dimensions, materials, and hardware. The **2024 Revised IRR** is its current implementation document. When an ALE question asks about a specific measurement, the answer comes from here.

*Republic Act No. 7277 (1992)*, the *Magna Carta for Disabled Persons*, is the rights framework — the law that establishes **why it's legally required**. It doesn't specify measurements. Think of it as the constitutional basis for BP 344.

*Republic Act No. 9442 (2007)* is the enforcement amendment — the law that specifies **what happens if you don't comply**. It amends RA 7277 by adding criminal liability, and it explicitly names architects as persons liable.

| Law | Role | What the ALE Tests |
|---|---|---|
| BP 344 + 2024 Revised IRR | Technical standard — what to build | Specific dimensions and conditions |
| RA 7277 (1992) | Rights framework — why it's required | Policy basis, coverage |
| RA 9442 (2007) | Enforcement — penalties for non-compliance | Fine ranges, imprisonment terms, liable parties |

### Penalties under RA 9442 — the numbers that get tested

These are criminal penalties, and they appear in ALE questions. Memorize both tiers.

**First violation:** ₱50,000–₱100,000 fine, or 6 months–2 years imprisonment, or both.

**Subsequent violations:** ₱100,000–₱200,000 fine, or 2–6 years imprisonment, or both.

Architects are explicitly named as liable parties. This is a professional liability question — not an abstract legal one.

> **ALE Watch:** Questions about penalties never come from BP 344 itself. They come from **RA 9442**. If an option says "BP 344 prescribes a ₱50,000 fine," it's wrong — that provision lives in RA 9442.

## How This Topic Is Tested

Understanding the three question patterns before you study prevents the most common failure mode: knowing the numbers but not knowing what the exam is actually asking you to do with them.

**Pattern 1 — Direct recall.** "What is the minimum clear opening width of an accessible door?" → **900 mm**. No reasoning required. These questions test the value.

**Pattern 2 — Scenario application.** "A corridor is 1,350 mm wide. A wheelchair user and an ambulant person must pass each other. Is this corridor compliant?" → No. The 2024 IRR requires **1,500 mm** for this condition. These questions test whether you can apply the rule to a described situation.

**Pattern 3 — Violation identification (Area C).** A floor plan of an accessible washroom is shown. One or more elements violate BP 344. You identify them. This is where students who memorized numbers without understanding the spatial logic fail — because a violation only looks like a violation if you know what the correct spatial arrangement looks like.

Pattern 3 is why this article explains the reasoning, not just the values. Read the prose sections. The tables will follow.

## Part 1: Entrances

A wheelchair user reaches the door, the corridor, and the toilet only if the building entrance works. Accessible entrance design is the first link in the chain.

All public entrances must be accessible — including those serving leased spaces, parking structures, and elevated walkways. Where the entrance is not level with the arrival grade, a ramp or lift must be provided.

### The vestibule

A *vestibule* is the transitional space between outside and inside — the small chamber between the exterior door and the interior door. For a wheelchair user, the vestibule presents a specific problem: they must be fully inside, with the outer door closed, before they can reach the inner door. If the vestibule is too small, the wheelchair is caught between two doors simultaneously.

The solution is straightforward: the vestibule floor must be large enough to contain one full wheelchair turning circle. The 2024 IRR requires **1,800 × 1,800 mm** ⟳ of clear, level floor area — the same dimension as the turning circle.

### Glass doors and panels

A frameless glass door is essentially invisible, which is a hazard — particularly for people with visual impairments. BP 344 requires that glass doors and panels carry a horizontal or graphical pattern with a minimum **30% luminance contrast**, positioned between **800 mm and 1,500 mm** above the floor. This band covers both seated eye level (wheelchair users) and standing eye level.

### Weather protection

Every accessible entrance — including all ramps and stairs serving it — must be protected from weather. A canopy or equivalent structure is required.

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Vestibule level area (depth × width) ⟳ | **1,800 × 1,800 mm** | C.1.4 |
| Glass door/panel contrast zone | 800–1,500 mm above floor | C.1.5 |
| Luminance contrast minimum | 30% | A.7.3 |

## Part 2: Doors

A standard interior door is 800–900 mm wide. An accessible door must clear **900 mm**. That measurement is taken from the face of the fully open door at 90° to the door jamb — not from frame to jamb. The distinction matters: a partially-open door is narrower than 900 mm even if the frame opening is 900 mm. The door must be fully open for the measurement to be correct.

### The approach space ⟳

Before passing through the door, a wheelchair user must maneuver up to it, stop, and reach the hardware. That process requires clear, level space on both sides. The 2024 IRR requires **1,800 × 1,800 mm** ⟳ on both sides of every accessible door. This is larger than the old 1,500 × 1,500 mm — a change that affects retrofit projects and new construction alike.

**The one exception:** Where a door opens into — but not from — a corridor (the door swings away from the corridor side), the corridor side may be reduced to a **1,500 mm** minimum. The 1,800 × 1,800 mm requirement still applies on the room side.

### The threshold ⟳

A threshold is the raised strip at the base of a door frame. Even a small threshold can catch a wheelchair caster, trip a cane user, or create a stability risk for ambulant persons. The 2024 IRR sets a hard maximum of **12 mm** ⟳ — reduced from 19 mm in 2013.

The bevel rule: any threshold **above 6 mm** must be beveled at a slope of **1:8** — a gentle taper, not a sharp vertical edge.

### Hardware

The handle of every accessible door must be operable with one closed fist — no twisting, grasping, or fine finger manipulation. This means **lever-type handles only**. Knob-type hardware is prohibited on accessible routes. Handles are centered at **900 mm** above the floor.

For automatic closers: the maximum closing force for an interior accessible door is **2.0 kg** ⟳. This dropped from 4.0 kg in the 2013 IRR. A door that requires significant force to open is effectively inaccessible — even if every other dimension is correct.

The sweep period — the time the door takes to travel from 70° open to approximately 12° before latching — must be a **minimum of 4 seconds**. This gives a wheelchair user enough time to clear the threshold before the door contacts them.

### Additional door hardware

A kick plate protects the lower portion of the door from wheelchair footrest damage. Required height: **300–400 mm** from the bottom of the door.

A secondary peephole — for wheelchair users who cannot reach a standard eye-level peephole — is mounted at **1,000 mm** ⟳ above the floor.

## Part 3: Switches and Controls

A light switch that a wheelchair user cannot reach without leaning dangerously forward is a failure — even if the door next to it is perfectly sized. The 2024 IRR places switches and controls at **900–1,200 mm** ⟳ above the finished floor. The old lower bound was 920 mm; it has been slightly lowered to 900 mm to better accommodate seated reach.

Placement horizontally: switches must be within **200 mm** of the latch side of the door, so a person approaching the door can reach them without repositioning.

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Switch height range ⟳ | **900–1,200 mm** above floor | C.4.1 |
| Max distance from door latch side | **200 mm** | C.4.2 |

## Part 4: Corridors

### Width — the most commonly changed value in the 2024 IRR

A wheelchair user needs **900 mm** of clear width to move unobstructed. An ambulant person walking alongside needs approximately **600 mm**. The sum is roughly 1,500 mm, and the 2024 IRR formalizes this as the minimum corridor width where a wheelchair and a walking person must pass each other: **1,500 mm** ⟳.

This is the biggest single change in the 2024 IRR for everyday building design. The old standard was **1,200 mm** — still shown in most older review materials. If your notes say 1,200 mm for a general accessible corridor, they are wrong.

Where two wheelchair users must pass simultaneously, the minimum is **1,800 mm** — two turning circles, side by side.

### Turnabout spaces ⟳

A wheelchair user in a long corridor must be able to reverse direction without backing up the entire length. Turnabout bays — zones wide enough for a full rotation — are required at maximum **12,000 mm (12 m)** intervals along the corridor, and at every dead end.

Each turnabout must be **1,800 × 1,800 mm** ⟳ — the turning circle, in every direction. The old standard was 1,500 × 1,500 mm.

At dead ends: the turnabout must be within **3,500 mm** of the dead end wall.

### Surface and slope

Corridors must be slip-resistant. The maximum *longitudinal slope* — the slope running in the direction of travel — before the corridor legally becomes a ramp is **1:20 (5%)**. The maximum *cross gradient* — slope perpendicular to travel — is **1:100 (1%)**.

Surface interruptions (cracks, expansion joints, grating gaps) must not create raised edges above **6 mm**. Grating gaps must not exceed **13 mm** center-to-center — wide enough to drain, narrow enough that a cane tip or caster cannot drop in.

|---|---|---|

| Two wheelchairs simultaneous | **1,800 mm** minimum | C.5.2 |
| Turnabout dimensions ⟳ | **1,800 × 1,800 mm** | C.5.2 |
| Max spacing between turnabouts | **12,000 mm** (12 m) | C.5.2 |

> **Most-tested:** The corridor width change from 1,200 mm to **1,500 mm** ⟳. This is the number most likely to appear in scenario questions with a "is this corridor compliant?" framing.

## Part 5: Accessible Washrooms

This is the most heavily tested section of BP 344 in the ALE, and the section most significantly revised by the 2024 IRR. Work through it carefully.

### Before the dimensions: what the toilet must allow

An accessible toilet cubicle must enable a specific sequence of movements: enter the cubicle → close the door → turn to face the toilet → transfer from wheelchair to toilet seat → stabilize using grab bars → reverse the entire sequence to exit.

Every dimension in this section corresponds to one step in that sequence. When you encounter a value that feels arbitrary, map it to a movement: *what is this measurement making possible?*

### How many accessible toilets are required

The 2024 IRR requires at least **one accessible female cubicle and one accessible male cubicle** on every floor with **20 or fewer WCs** total. This is per gender, per floor — not per building.

---

### The Cubicle ⟳

The accessible toilet cubicle is now a **1,800 × 1,800 mm square** in the 2024 IRR. The old asymmetric plan (**1,700 × 1,800 mm**) is superseded. The square reflects the turning circle logic: a wheelchair must be able to rotate fully either inside the stall or immediately outside it.

Outside the cubicle — before the stall door — there must be an additional **1,800 × 1,800 mm** ⟳ of clear turning space. This is the maneuvering space used to approach and enter the stall.

The stall door must swing **outward**, or be sliding or folding. This is non-negotiable, and BP 344 states the reason explicitly: a rescuer must be able to open the door from outside if the occupant has fallen and is lying behind the door. The door lock must be openable from outside using only a coin or a simple flat tool.

> **ALE Watch:** Door swing direction is a frequent Area C question. The answer is always *outward* (or sliding/folding). The reason — rescuer access if the occupant has collapsed — is part of the answer in any written or oral exam format.

---

### The Water Closet

The WC seat height is **450 mm** above finished floor. This is slightly higher than a standard residential toilet — the raised position makes transfer from a wheelchair easier because the height difference between the chair and the seat is reduced.

The WC must be positioned with its centerline **450 mm** ⟳ from the finished face of the side wall. Three values define the precise wall-to-toilet spatial relationship — and all three appear independently in ALE questions:

- Grab bar outer face to wall: **100 mm**
- Grab bar face to WC centerline: **350 mm**
- WC centerline to wall: **450 mm** (= 100 + 350)

These three values are additive. Understanding the addition prevents confusion when the exam presents any one of them and asks for the others.

---

### Grab Bars ⟳

Grab bars are the equipment that allows a person to lower themselves onto the seat, push themselves up from it, and stabilize during use. Two types are required in every accessible cubicle.

**Fixed L-type grab bar** — mounted on the side wall. The horizontal leg runs parallel to the toilet, extending **300 mm** past the front of the WC. The critical height: the top of the grab bar must be exactly **300 mm** above the top of the toilet seat ⟳. In the 2013 IRR this was a 280–300 mm range; the 2024 IRR made it a single exact value. Since the seat is at 450 mm above the floor, the grab bar top is at approximately **750 mm** above finished floor — useful as a secondary reference.

**Flip-up grab bar** — on the open (transfer) side of the toilet. It extends **100 mm** past the front of the WC at the same height as the L-bar. It must support a minimum load of **200 kg**.

Both bars share the same specifications:
- Diameter: **30–40 mm** ⟳ (changed from 38–45 mm in 2013)
- Wall clearance: minimum **50 mm**

> **ALE Watch:** The grab bar height reference point trips up students. The **300 mm** measurement is from the **top of the toilet seat** to the **top of the grab bar** — *not* from the floor. The floor measurement (~750 mm) is a consequence, not the specification. If the exam gives you the floor dimension and asks for the seat-to-bar measurement, the answer is 300 mm regardless.

---

### The Lavatory

The lavatory sits **outside** the 1,800 × 1,800 mm cubicle footprint — it does not compete with the wheelchair's maneuvering space. Its centerline is **450 mm** from the adjacent wall. The rim height is **800 mm** — within comfortable seated reach. Below the rim, there must be clear knee space with a minimum vertical clearance of **650 mm**, so a wheelchair user can pull up directly underneath.

The faucet must be lever-type, push-button, or sensor-activated. Knob-type faucets requiring grip or wrist rotation are prohibited.

The mirror's bottom edge must be at or below **1,000 mm** above the floor — within seated sightline.

### Urinals ⟳

The 2024 IRR made significant changes to urinal requirements. Urinals must be wall-hung with an elongated lip.

The maximum lip height dropped from **480 mm** to **350–400 mm** ⟳ — a drop of 80–130 mm. This brings the control zone within comfortable seated reach. The clear floor space in front changed from the old 750 × 1,200 mm rectangle to a **900 × 900 mm square** ⟳. Grab bars are required on both sides, **600 mm** long, with the top edge at **1,500 mm** above finished floor. A privacy shield of **750 mm minimum** is required.

### Roll-in Showers ⟳

Roll-in showers allow a wheelchair user to bathe while remaining seated. The minimum dimension is **1,800 × 1,800 mm** ⟳ — expanded from 1,500 × 1,500 mm in 2013. The maximum curb height is **12 mm** ⟳ — matching the door threshold maximum. L-shaped grab bars are required inside. The shower spray hose must be at least **1,500 mm** long.

For bathtubs: a clear transfer space of **900 × 1,500 mm** is required alongside the tub.

## Part 6: Signage ⟳

Two values changed significantly in the 2024 IRR.

The door-mounted sign for accessible toilets and showers is now **200 × 200 mm** ⟳ — double the old 100 × 100 mm. Wall and door signage must be mounted with the center of the sign at a maximum of **1,500 mm** above finished floor ⟳ (previously a 1,400–1,600 mm range).

The International Symbol of Access (ISA) — white figure on UN Blue background (#5B92E5) — must face right. When the accessible facility requires directional guidance, a left-facing arrow is added.

All signage requires a minimum **30% luminance contrast** from surrounding surfaces. Braille must be included on labels in public areas. Objects mounted on walls that project more than 100 mm must maintain **2,200 mm** minimum headroom clearance — protecting ambulant persons with visual impairments.

## Common Mistakes: 10 Ways Students Lose Marks

Now that you have the full picture, here are the specific errors that appear in ALE answers. Each one corresponds to something explained above.

**1. Carrying the old corridor width.**
The 2024 IRR raised the single-wheelchair + ambulant minimum from 1,200 mm to **1,500 mm** ⟳. This is the most frequently used wrong value from older review materials. If your notes say 1,200 mm, they're wrong.

**2. Making the cubicle rectangular.**
The 2024 cubicle is a **square — 1,800 × 1,800 mm**. The old 1,700 × 1,800 mm plan no longer applies. Any answer option showing 1,700 mm as a cubicle dimension is testing the superseded standard.

**3. Using the old threshold maximum.**
Students memorize 19 mm (old value). The 2024 maximum is **12 mm**. The 6 mm bevel trigger is unchanged — but the absolute maximum dropped.

**4. Using the old door approach space.**
Students recall 1,500 × 1,500 mm. The 2024 standard is **1,800 × 1,800 mm** ⟳. These are values from two different IRR editions.

**5. Measuring grab bar height from the floor.**
The **300 mm** measurement is from the **top of the toilet seat** to the **top of the grab bar** — not from the floor. The 750 mm floor measurement is a derived value.

**6. Misremembering the WC centerline.**
The WC centerline is **450 mm** from the finished wall ⟳. Some students recall 750 mm from an older ambiguous reference. 450 mm is the explicit 2024 value.

**7. Using the old urinal lip height.**
The old standard was 480 mm. The 2024 standard is **350–400 mm** ⟳. The reduction of 80–130 mm is the largest absolute dimension change in the urinal section.

**8. Getting the door swing reason wrong.**
The door swings outward so that a **rescuer can open the door if someone has collapsed behind it**. Students who memorized "outward" without the reason misapply it in Area C scenarios where they need to justify their answer.

**9. Using the old turnabout dimensions.**
The 2024 turnabout is **1,800 × 1,800 mm** ⟳ — not the old 1,500 × 1,500 mm. The maximum spacing (12 m) and dead-end distance (3,500 mm) did not change.

**10. Misattributing penalties to BP 344.**
Criminal penalties come from **RA 9442**, not BP 344. BP 344 specifies what to build; RA 9442 specifies what happens if you don't.
