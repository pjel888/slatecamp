# Exam Day Playbook & Mental Performance

## Day 1 Playbook — Hour by Hour

**Day before (the evening):**
- 6–8 PM: Light review of 20–30 items from known topics. No new material.
- 8 PM: Prepare physical exam materials. Check list:
  - [ ] Valid government-issued ID
  - [ ] Notice of Admission (NOA)
  - [ ] Ball pens (black ink, 2–3 pens)
  - [ ] Pencils No. 2 (2–3 pencils)
  - [ ] Calculator (non-programmable, allowed functions only)
  - [ ] Printed copy of PD 1096 IRR Rules VII and VIII (for Day 2)
  - [ ] Food in transparent plastic bag
  - [ ] Water in clear bottle
- 9 PM: No more studying. No exam discussion with anxious contacts.
- 10:30 PM: In bed. Non-exam reading or music if needed to wind down.

**Exam morning:**
- Wake up in time for a full meal. No caffeine overload — moderate is fine.
- Arrive at venue by 6:15–6:30 AM. Locate room and seat.
- 6:30–7:30 AM (before exam starts): Sit in your seat. Look at your notes if you brought them (outside). Stop reading by 7:15 AM. At 7:20, close everything and breathe. The preparation is in your head, not on the page.

**Inside the room:**
- When the exam booklet is distributed: write your name and exam details first. Do not open to questions until instructed.
- When permitted to start: scan the first 5 questions quickly to calibrate difficulty. This is orientation, not answering.
- Begin answering at a steady pace.

## The Multiple Choice Mental Protocol

### System for Each Item

**Read** → **Think** → **Eliminate** → **Select** → **Move**

1. **Read** the stem completely. Underline the key action word (which, what, most, least, except).
2. **Think** of the answer before looking at choices. Even a vague answer protects you from being misled by attractive wrong choices.
3. **Eliminate** choices you know are wrong. Even eliminating one changes your odds from 25% to 33%.
4. **Select** from remaining options. The choice that most precisely matches the primary law or code provision wins.
5. **Move** on. Never spend more than 90 seconds on any item during first pass.

### Flagging System

On first pass:
- Answer everything you know confidently
- Flag uncertain items with a light mark
- Skip only if genuinely blank — but write a guess first so you have something

On second pass (after first pass complete):
- Return to flagged items only
- Fresh eyes often resolve uncertainty
- If still uncertain after 60 seconds: your first instinct was probably right

**Critical rule:** Do not change an answer unless you have a specific, concrete reason to change it. Research consistently shows first answers are correct more often than revisions made under uncertainty.

## Area C Day Mindset

The design problem day is different. Multiple choice is recall under time pressure. Area C is creation under time pressure.

**Your activation state before Day 2 should be:**
- Alert and calm — not wired, not sluggish
- Physically warm (sketching muscles need to be loose — bring a jacket if the room is cold)
- Already having thought through the building type if it was pre-announced

**When you receive the brief:**
- One breath. Then read.
- You have done this before. Many times. Trust the reps.
- The protocol is automatic. You do not need to think about the process — execute it.

**When you hit a design problem you do not immediately know how to solve:**
- Revert to first principles: what are the zones? What is the circulation? What are the code constraints?
- Any logically organized building that meets the program and codes will score passing marks
- A creative or inspired design is a bonus — not the baseline

## Mental Performance Techniques

### Pre-Exam Activation Protocol (The 10 Minutes Before the Exam Starts)

Sit in your seat. Close your eyes if comfortable. Run through this sequence:

1. **Three slow breaths.** In for 4 counts, hold for 2, out for 6. This activates the parasympathetic nervous system and reduces cortisol.

2. **One memory.** Think of one session where you studied well — a day you felt sharp and the material clicked. Hold that feeling for 10 seconds. You are capable of that performance. You have proven it.

3. **One statement.** Internal, not spoken: "I prepared for this. I will do my best work with what I know." Not "I will definitely top the board." Just: I am ready to do my best work.

4. **Open your eyes.** Look at the desk in front of you. The exam has not started. You are calm and alert.

This takes 4 minutes. It is not mystical. It is a neurological reset.

---

### When Anxiety Spikes During the Exam

If you read a question and your mind goes blank or anxiety rises:

1. Cover the question with your hand.
2. Look at the desk.
3. One breath — in for 4, out for 6.
4. Uncover the question. Read it again.

Blank-mind moments last approximately 30–60 seconds when not fed. They dissolve when you stop feeding them with "I don't know this" thoughts and redirect to "what do I actually know about this topic?"

---

### On Seeing Questions You Don't Know

This happens to topnotchers too. It happened in the exam where someone topped the board.

Your response to an unfamiliar question: neutral assessment, not panic. "I don't immediately know this. What do I know about this topic? Which code governs this area? What is the most logical answer?"

If you genuinely cannot narrow it down: eliminate the most extreme option (the highest or lowest number, or the most sweeping statement), then choose among the remaining options. Do not leave it blank.

---

### Managing Energy Over a Two-Day Exam

**Day 1 evening:**
- Eat a proper meal. Your brain ran hard today.
- Do not discuss the Day 1 exam with others — you cannot change those answers, and analysis creates anxiety that bleeds into Day 2.
- Light walk or stretching if possible — physical movement releases tension and improves sleep.
- Sleep matters more tonight than any last-minute review.

**Between subjects on Day 1:**
- During breaks, stand up, stretch, move.
- Eat something — even a small snack maintains blood glucose for the next subject.
- Do not analyze completed questions with other examinees during break.

## The Mental Traits of High-Performing LEA Candidates

These are not personality traits you are born with. They are practiced behaviors.

**Equanimity under uncertainty.** The LEA contains questions you will not know. The topnotcher treats these as calculation exercises ("given what I know, what is most likely correct?") rather than emotional events ("I don't know this, I'm going to fail").

**Consistent daily contact.** Not marathon sessions. Not cramming. The topnotchers who spoke about their review described one consistent behavior: they studied something every single day. Baniago: "Just don't let a day go by without studying something." This is the compound interest of knowledge.

**Self-compassion without self-indulgence.** A bad day is a bad day — not evidence that you cannot pass. Adjust and continue. But self-compassion does not mean excusing yourself from the work. It means recovering faster and showing up again tomorrow.

**Curiosity over obligation.** The best reviewers reframed the review as curiosity-driven, not fear-driven. Sendrijas reframed it as becoming a better architect, not surviving an exam. Ko kept asking "why is this the answer?" Ko's examiner-like questioning style — developed by a professor who trained with board-exam-type questions — became a direct competitive advantage.

**Willingness to confront weakness.** Every topnotcher, without exception, identified their weak areas and ran toward them. Not around them. The feeling of discomfort when studying a difficult topic is the signal that learning is happening. Comfortable review sessions are often the least productive.
