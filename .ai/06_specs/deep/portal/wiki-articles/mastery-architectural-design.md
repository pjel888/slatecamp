# Architectural Design and Site Planning — Mastery & Connections

At the mastery level, Architectural Design stops being a subject you study and becomes a lens through which you read everything else. This article maps the connections — how the thirteen subtopics link to each other, and how this subject reaches outward to pull concepts from every corner of the ALE.

If you've covered the overview and done your initial round of practice questions, this is where you build the integrative picture that separates a good score from an excellent one.

## The Internal Logic: How Subtopics Connect

Architectural Design is not a collection of thirteen independent topics. It is one continuous act of thinking, broken into stages. The connections run both forward and backward through the design process.

**Site Analysis → Design Process → Building Type Decisions**

Every building begins with its site. Topography determines drainage strategy and building placement. Solar orientation in the Philippines — with the sun tracking a southern arc — means east-west elongation is a passive cooling strategy for most building types. Prevailing winds from the northeast (amihan) and southwest (habagat) inform natural ventilation strategies.

These site-reading skills do not disappear once you move into the design process phase. They shape every decision: where the main entry goes, how floors are oriented, where mechanical equipment is placed to avoid conflict with prevailing wind patterns.

**Design Process → Anthropometrics → Spatial Adequacy**

The design process produces spaces. Anthropometrics tells you whether those spaces actually work for people. A corridor that looks proportionally fine on a 1:200 plan may be inadequate for two-way wheelchair passage — a fact BP 344 codifies but that anthropometric understanding explains. Room dimensions, door widths, counter heights, clearances at plumbing fixtures — all flow from human body dimensions applied to functional requirements.

**Building Type → Code Application**

Every building type generates a specific regulatory context. You cannot design a school without knowing DEPED space standards. You cannot design a hospital without DOH guidelines. You cannot design any occupied building without PD 1096 occupancy classification driving your setback, height, and floor area calculations. The building type subtopics and the code subtopics are not separate study areas — they are always studied together.

**Code Application → Graphic Communication**

Code requirements ultimately live in drawings. A fire exit stair that meets RA 9514 travel distance requirements must be shown in the correct location on the floor plan. An accessible toilet that meets BP 344 turning radius requirements must be drawn to scale to verify adequacy. Graphic communication is the medium through which code compliance is demonstrated and verified.

## Connections to Every Other ALE Subject

Architectural Design is the only ALE subject that has a documented connection to every other subject in the exam. This is what makes it both the most demanding and the most rewarding subject to master.

### Connection to Architectural Theory, Humanities, and Technology

Design principles — proportion, scale, rhythm, hierarchy, unity — are the intellectual foundation of design process and methodology. The theory subject gives you the vocabulary and the historical context. Architectural Design puts them to work in building-scale decisions. A question about the proportional system governing a civic building's facade is simultaneously a theory question and a design question.

### Connection to Structural Conceptualization

Every design decision has a structural implication, and every structural decision has a design consequence. This relationship runs deep:

- **Column grid selection** determines floor plate geometry, lease depth for commercial buildings, and parking bay efficiency in podium structures.
- **Shear wall placement** constrains open-plan layouts and determines where cores must be located.
- **Long-span systems** enable the column-free spaces essential for gymnasiums, auditoriums, and covered markets — and require designers to understand deflection, vibration, and connection details.
- **Foundation selection** affects ground floor design, basement feasibility, and the relationship between the building and its site.

### Connection to MEPF (Mechanical, Electrical, Plumbing, Fire Protection)

Space planning for building services is one of the most practically tested integration areas. The architect must allocate space for:

- **Mechanical rooms** (AHU rooms, chiller plants) — typically 3–5% of gross floor area for commercial buildings
- **Electrical rooms** (main distribution boards, transformer vaults) — requiring no plumbing overhead and specific clearances
- **Plumbing cores** — wet areas stacked vertically across floors for efficiency
- **Fire protection** (sprinkler heads, standpipe hose cabinets, fire pump rooms) — driven by RA 9514 requirements

A floor plan that looks elegant but has no mechanical room, no electrical room, and no clear riser shaft strategy is not a complete design. The ALE tests whether you understand what those service spaces require and how they should be located relative to the building's functional zones.

<ExamTip>ALE questions frequently present floor plan scenarios and ask you to identify the most appropriate location for a mechanical room, electrical room, or fire escape stair. These are integration questions: they require knowledge of MEPF space requirements (from the MEPF subject), fire egress rules (RA 9514), and architectural planning principles (service zones at the building's perimeter or core) all applied simultaneously.</ExamTip>

### Connection to Philippine Laws and Practice Management

The legal framework is inseparable from design practice. Three laws shape nearly every design decision in Architectural Design:

**PD 1096 (National Building Code)** — occupancy classification governs setbacks, height limits, floor area ratios, and use regulations. A mixed-use building may have different occupancy classifications on different floors, each with its own requirements.

**BP 344 (Accessibility Law)** — accessible design is not a special condition applied to specific buildings. It is a universal requirement for all public buildings, and the ALE tests it extensively. Ramp slopes (1:12 maximum), door widths (900mm minimum clear), accessible parking dimensions (3.7m × 5.0m), tactile guide paths, accessible toilet layouts — these are specific, tested, consequential.

**RA 9514 (Fire Code)** — means of egress is a life safety system. Travel distances, exit widths per occupant load, number of exits required, stair enclosure requirements, exit discharge to public way — all are tested in the Architectural Design context.

### Connection to Site Development and Planning

Site planning is not just a subtopic within Architectural Design — it is a bridge to the Site Development and Planning subject. The skills overlap significantly: reading topographic surveys, calculating cut and fill, understanding easements and rights-of-way, applying setback requirements, designing for stormwater management.

The practical distinction: Site Development focuses on the landscape and infrastructure systems surrounding the building. Architectural Design focuses on the building's response to its site. In the exam, questions that present a site with given conditions and ask for design decisions draw on both subjects.

## The Design-as-Synthesis Mental Model

The most useful mental model for mastering Architectural Design is this: every design question is a constrained optimization problem. You are finding the best solution within a set of constraints — and the constraints come from all of the other subjects.

A question asking you to design a portion of a hospital floor plan is constrained by:
- DOH minimum room dimensions (institutional design)
- BP 344 accessibility requirements (accessibility standards)
- RA 9514 egress requirements (fire safety)
- NSCP structural grid requirements (structural conceptualization)
- Natural ventilation principles (environmental design)
- PD 1096 occupancy classification (national building code)

The student who has studied Architectural Design in isolation will see a design question. The student who has built the integrative mental model will see a multi-subject problem — and will be able to navigate it systematically.

<Callout>This is why Architectural Design is saved for comprehensive review rather than early study. It is the subject where all your other preparation lands. If you haven't covered the codes, the structures, the services, and the laws first, you cannot bring them to bear on design decisions. Build the foundation subjects first, then let Architectural Design become the place where they integrate.</Callout>

## Integration Patterns: What the ALE Actually Tests

**Pattern 1: Code-Constrained Design Selection**
"A two-storey commercial building with 300 occupants on the second floor requires how many exits, and what is the minimum combined exit width?"
This requires: RA 9514 occupant load calculation + exit count rules + exit width formula.

**Pattern 2: Accessibility Compliance Review**
"Which of the following toilet room layouts complies with BP 344 requirements for persons with disabilities?"
This requires: BP 344 turning radius (1.5m clear circle), grab bar placement, door swing direction, fixture heights.

**Pattern 3: Site Response Design**
"A rectangular site in Metro Manila faces north. The building program requires natural ventilation. Where should the primary building mass be positioned, and how should openings be oriented?"
This requires: solar geometry (south sun path in PH) + monsoon wind patterns + site planning principles.

**Pattern 4: Multi-Code Building Type**
"A school building requires how many toilet fixtures per 100 students, what is the minimum corridor width for an educational occupancy, and how should the main exit stair be located relative to classrooms?"
This requires: PD 1096 plumbing fixture ratios + occupancy corridor standards + RA 9514 egress rules.

## Building Your Integration Habit

Three practices accelerate mastery of Architectural Design as an integration subject:

**Review drawing sets, not just individual spaces.** A complete floor plan contains structural grids, service cores, egress paths, accessible routes, and space adjacencies simultaneously. Practice reading full floor plans and identifying all of these systems at once.

**Study codes comparatively.** When you learn a BP 344 ramp requirement, immediately compare it to the RA 9514 requirements for accessible egress. When you learn a PD 1096 setback, ask how it interacts with the site planning decisions you would make on that lot. Codes are not isolated documents — they govern the same building from different angles.

**Work backward from exam scenarios.** Take a practice question and, after answering it, identify every subject it drew upon. A question that seems to be about residential design may require structural knowledge, code knowledge, and anthropometric knowledge simultaneously. Making those connections explicit accelerates the integration.
