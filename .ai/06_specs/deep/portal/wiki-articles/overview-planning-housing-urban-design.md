# Planning, Housing & Urban Design — Overview & Study Strategy

Planning, Housing & Urban Design is where architecture connects to the broader human systems that shape cities, communities, and the built environment at scale. It asks you to think beyond the individual building — to the block, the neighborhood, the region, and the policies that govern how all of it grows, changes, and is inhabited.

For many candidates, this subject feels more abstract than the design subjects. The key to making it concrete is to anchor every concept to the Philippine context you already live in. Zoning laws, housing subdivisions, barangay boundaries, and city development plans are not just exam content — they are the regulatory environment you will practice in.

## What This Subject Actually Covers

The LEA organizes Planning, Housing & Urban Design into seven subtopic areas:

**General Principles and History of Planning** — The intellectual tradition of urban planning: from Haussmann's Paris to Ebenezer Howard's Garden City, from the City Beautiful movement to Jane Jacobs's critique of urban renewal. This subtopic grounds the technical content in a theoretical and historical context.

**Housing and Human Settlements Planning** — Standards for residential development, housing typologies, density calculations, site requirements, and the frameworks for planning communities at various income levels. This is where BP 220 (Economic and Socialized Housing) and PD 957 (Subdivision and Condominium Buyers' Protective Decree) become essential reading.

**Innovation Layer — 21st Century Planning** — Emerging frameworks: smart cities, transit-oriented development (TOD), resilience planning, inclusive design at the urban scale, and climate-adaptive urbanism. The LEA increasingly tests awareness of contemporary approaches alongside traditional planning doctrine.

**Landscape Architecture and Ecology** — The integration of green infrastructure, ecological corridors, stormwater management, and open space planning into urban design. Understanding the relationship between built and natural systems is tested here.

**Planning Agencies, Governance & Philippine Planning Law** — The institutional landscape: which agency does what, which law governs which aspect of planning, how Local Government Units (LGUs) exercise their planning authority under RA 7160 (the Local Government Code). This is highly testable content.

**Site Planning and Environmental Analysis** — The technical tools of site analysis: topography, drainage patterns, solar orientation, prevailing winds, vegetation, access, views, and hazard zones. This subtopic bridges planning and architectural design.

**Urban and Regional Planning** — Larger-scale frameworks: metropolitan planning, regional development, the urban-rural continuum, land use planning theory, and transportation planning.

## The Philippine Context Is the Core

Many planning questions on the LEA have a distinctly Philippine framing. The exam is not testing abstract international planning theory — it is testing whether you can function as a professional architect within the Philippine regulatory environment.

The three legislative pillars you must know cold:

**RA 7160 — Local Government Code of 1991.** This law devolved significant planning authority to LGUs. It mandates the Comprehensive Land Use Plan (CLUP) and Zoning Ordinance for every municipality and city. Understanding what LGUs can and cannot do under RA 7160 is foundational.

**PD 957 — Subdivision and Condominium Buyers' Protective Decree.** Governs the development, sale, and regulation of subdivision lots and condominium units. Sets minimum standards for roads, open spaces, and community facilities in subdivision developments.

**BP 220 — Economic and Socialized Housing.** Sets the minimum design and construction standards for economic and socialized housing projects — standards that are deliberately less stringent than those for open-market housing, in recognition of affordability constraints. Know the minimum lot sizes, road widths, and open space requirements for each housing category.

<ExamTip>The LEA frequently asks about the minimum standards under BP 220 and PD 957. These are specific numbers — minimum lot sizes, minimum road right-of-way widths, minimum open space percentages. Memorize the key figures for each housing category (socialized, economic, low-cost, medium-cost, open market).</ExamTip>

**DHSUD** (Department of Human Settlements and Urban Development, formerly HLURB) is the primary regulatory body for housing and land use. Know its mandate, its relationship to LGUs, and how it interfaces with other agencies.

## Study Strategy

**Start with the legislation.** The agency-and-governance subtopic and the housing-standards subtopic are the most numerically testable — specific figures appear in questions. Get RA 7160, BP 220, and PD 957 into your working memory first. These are your most reliable source of correct answers.

**Then build the conceptual framework.** Once you know the rules, the history and principles of planning give you the "why" behind them. Ebenezer Howard's Garden City explains why Philippine subdivision standards require open space. The City Beautiful movement explains why Philippine civic centers often have a central plaza. These connections make the standards memorable.

**Treat the 21st-century layer as vocabulary acquisition.** Smart cities, TOD, resilience planning — you do not need deep expertise here, but you need to recognize the terms, understand what they advocate, and be able to apply them to a scenario question.

**Use site planning as a bridge to design.** The Site Planning and Environmental Analysis subtopic is the most directly connected to Architectural Design. If you are strong in design, let that pull you into planning. If planning feels abstract, use your design intuition to make it concrete.

<Callout>The Comprehensive Land Use Plan (CLUP) is one of the most important planning instruments in the Philippine system. Every LGU is required to prepare one. It classifies land into zones (residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural, etc.) and is the legal basis for the Zoning Ordinance. Understanding the CLUP process — who prepares it, who approves it, how long it is valid — is regularly tested.</Callout>

## Connection to Laws & Codes

Planning, Housing & Urban Design and Laws & Codes (LEA-LAWS) have substantial overlap. The same legislation appears in both subjects, but Laws & Codes tends to test statutory interpretation and specific provisions, while Planning tests the application of those laws to planning and design scenarios.

If you are studying both subjects simultaneously (which is the recommended approach), use your planning study to build conceptual understanding and your Laws study to nail the specific provisions.

Key overlapping legislation:
- RA 7279 (Urban Development and Housing Act — UDHA): socialized housing, eviction and demolition rules
- RA 9003 (Ecological Solid Waste Management Act): waste facility siting
- PD 1096 (National Building Code): building setbacks, lot coverage — directly connects to site planning
- RA 10587 (Environmental Planning Act): licensure of environmental planners; relevant to understanding the planning profession

## Common Pitfalls

**Confusing HLURB and DHSUD.** HLURB was reorganized into DHSUD by RA 11201 in 2019. Exam questions may still reference HLURB functions — understand that DHSUD absorbed those functions. Do not be thrown by either name.

**Mixing up BP 220 categories.** "Socialized housing," "economic housing," "low-cost housing," and "medium-cost housing" have specific, different standards. A question asking for the minimum lot area for socialized housing has one correct answer; the same question for economic housing has a different answer.

**Treating planning as pure memorization.** Planning questions often present scenarios — a site with specific characteristics, a community with specific needs — and ask you to apply principles. The candidates who do best here are those who understand the *intent* of the standards, not just the numbers.

**Underweighting governance.** Questions about which agency has jurisdiction over what, or which law governs a specific planning action, are among the most reliably correct-or-wrong questions on the exam. There is no partial credit for knowing the right concept but the wrong agency. Invest time in the governance map.
