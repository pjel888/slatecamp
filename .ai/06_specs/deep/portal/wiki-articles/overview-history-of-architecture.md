# History of Architecture — Overview & Study Strategy

History of Architecture is one of the most expansive subjects on the LEA. It spans tens of thousands of years — from the first stone shelters of prehistory to the parametric towers of the 21st century — and it touches every inhabited region of the world. That breadth is both what makes it fascinating and what makes it worth approaching with a clear strategy.

The good news: this subject rewards curious, connected thinking. Once you see the through-lines — how trade routes carried building techniques, how climate shaped form, how each generation reacted to the one before it — the material starts to organize itself.

## What This Subject Actually Covers

The LEA treats History of Architecture as a survey of human building across 11 subtopic areas:

- **Pre-Historic & Ancient** — Megalithic structures, Mesopotamia, Egypt, Greece, Rome
- **Medieval to Baroque** — Early Christian, Byzantine, Romanesque, Gothic, Renaissance, Mannerist, Baroque, Rococo
- **Modern & Contemporary** — Arts & Crafts through Postmodernism, High-Tech, Deconstructivism, and beyond
- **Asian & Pacific Architecture** — India, China, Japan, Southeast Asia, Oceania
- **Islamic Architecture** — The great mosque traditions, Persian influence, Mughal, Ottoman, Moorish
- **Philippine Architecture** — Pre-colonial, Spanish colonial, American period, contemporary Filipino practice
- **African Architecture** — Sub-Saharan traditions, North African vernacular, Great Zimbabwe, contemporary African cities
- **Latin American Architecture** — Spanish colonial urbanism, Baroque churches, 20th-century modernism (Niemeyer, Barragán)
- **Pre-Columbian & Indigenous American** — Maya, Aztec, Inca, Pueblo, and related traditions
- **Australian & New Zealand Architecture** — Indigenous building, colonial period, contemporary practice
- **United States Architecture** — Colonial through the Chicago School, Mid-Century Modern, and postwar urbanism

<Note>Philippine Architecture deserves its own dedicated study pass even though it also appears here. It connects directly to your lived context, and the LEA questions on it often reward specificity — knowing the difference between a *bahay na bato* and a *bahay kubo* matters.</Note>

## Why 35% Matters

Within Area A, History of Architecture carries more weight than any other subject. A strong performance here can anchor your Area A score even if you find Planning or Professional Practice harder to absorb quickly. Conversely, going into the exam thin on History puts you in a position where no other Area A subject can fully compensate.

This does not mean memorizing every building and every date. It means building a reliable mental map — one where you can orient an unfamiliar building within its period and place by reading its visual and contextual cues.

## Study Strategy: Chronological vs. Thematic

Most candidates default to chronological study — reading Western architectural history from cave paintings to curtain walls in sequence. This works, but it has a risk: you can get so absorbed in the European timeline that Asia, Africa, and the Americas feel like afterthoughts crammed in at the end.

A more effective approach is **hybrid**: start with one chronological pass through Western history to establish your temporal anchor, then study the other traditions thematically by asking "what was happening here during the same era?"

For example, while Gothic cathedrals were rising in France (12th–14th century), the Angkor Wat complex was being completed in Cambodia, the Great Zimbabwe was under construction, and the Aztec Empire was consolidating in Mesoamerica. Holding these in the same frame makes each tradition more memorable — and the LEA sometimes tests exactly this kind of cross-cultural awareness.

<ExamTip>LEA questions on History often provide a photograph or description of a building and ask you to identify its period, style, architect, or cultural tradition. Train yourself to read buildings visually: column types, arch forms, roof profiles, ornamental vocabulary, and material palette are all legible clues.</ExamTip>

## Common Pitfalls

**Mixing up periods within the Western timeline.** Romanesque and Gothic are frequently confused — especially in questions that provide images rather than labels. Romanesque: round arches, thick walls, small windows. Gothic: pointed arches, flying buttresses, soaring verticality and light. Commit the structural logic, not just the names.

**Confusing architects with movements.** Le Corbusier is associated with the International Style and Brutalism — but attributing a specific building to him when it was designed by Mies van der Rohe (or vice versa) is a costly error. For the major figures, know at least three canonical works and the decade they were built.

**Treating Philippine Architecture as a separate silo.** Philippine architecture is a story of layered influences — Austronesian, Hindu-Buddhist, Islamic, Spanish, American — and understanding those influences makes the details stick. A *simbahan* built in 1610 tells you a story about Spanish colonial urbanism, earthquake engineering, and labor systems all at once.

**Ignoring non-Western chronology.** Many candidates can recite the Western timeline fluently but freeze when asked about the Heian period in Japan or the Mamluk dynasty in Egypt. Spend at least a few focused sessions mapping non-Western timelines onto the eras you already know.

## Where to Begin

If you are starting fresh, this is a solid entry sequence:

1. Lock in the Greek Orders (Doric, Ionic, Corinthian) and Roman engineering (arch, vault, dome). These references echo through 2,000 years of subsequent architecture.
2. Walk the Western timeline from Early Christian through Baroque. Mark the key transitions and what drove each one.
3. Add a dedicated pass on Philippine Architecture — use your local knowledge as an anchor.
4. Study Islamic, South Asian, and East Asian traditions in one thematic sweep, focusing on the mosque, the temple, and the palace as building types.
5. Cover Modern and Contemporary architecture with an emphasis on movements and manifestos — what each movement was reacting *against* is as important as what it proposed.
6. Fill in Africa, the Americas, and Oceania in shorter focused sessions.

History of Architecture is a subject where genuine curiosity pays dividends. The more you treat these buildings as answers to real human problems — how do you build in a desert? how do you express imperial authority in stone? how do you make a shelter that also reaches toward the sacred? — the more naturally the details will stay with you.
