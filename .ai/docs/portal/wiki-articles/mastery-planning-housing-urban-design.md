# Planning, Housing & Urban Design — Mastery & Connections

The Planning, Housing & Urban Design subject is built like a set of concentric circles. At the center is the individual site — its topography, ecology, and access conditions. Moving outward, that site sits within a neighborhood, which sits within a city, which sits within a region governed by a layered system of law and institutional authority. Mastery of this subject means being able to move fluently between those scales — and to connect what you know here to what you know in Architectural Design, Laws & Codes, and Environmental Design.

This article maps those connections explicitly.

## How the Subtopics Connect: A Scale-Based Mental Model

The most useful organizing principle for this subject is **scale** — from the smallest planning unit (the individual lot) to the largest (the regional and national system).

**Site Planning and Environmental Analysis** operates at the lot and block scale. It is the most architecturally familiar subtopic: solar analysis, prevailing wind, drainage, topographic constraints, access points, setbacks, lot coverage. This is where the architect's design instincts apply most directly.

**Housing and Human Settlements Planning** operates at the subdivision and community scale. It asks: how do you arrange lots, roads, open spaces, and community facilities to create a livable, standards-compliant residential development? The standards in BP 220 and PD 957 define the minimum acceptable answer.

**Landscape Architecture and Ecology** operates at the site-to-neighborhood scale. It asks how the green infrastructure — trees, open spaces, drainage swales, ecological corridors — connects across the development and integrates with the surrounding environment.

**Urban and Regional Planning** operates at the city and regional scale. It brings in transportation networks, land use patterns, density gradients, and the relationship between urban centers and their surrounding territories.

**Planning Agencies, Governance & Philippine Planning Law** is the regulatory framework that sits above all the other scales. It determines who has authority to make decisions at each scale and what rules they must follow.

**General Principles and History of Planning** provides the intellectual genealogy — the ideas and precedents that explain why the current system is structured the way it is.

**Innovation Layer** is the forward-looking overlay — the emerging frameworks that are beginning to reshape practice at every scale.

<Note>When you encounter a planning question on the ALE, your first move should be to identify the scale it is operating at. A question about lot coverage is a site-planning question. A question about CLUP approval is a governance question. A question about TOD is an urban-planning question. Identifying the scale focuses your recall.</Note>

## Site Analysis → Urban Planning: The Upward Chain

One of the most important connections in this subject is the chain from site-level analysis to urban-level planning. These are not separate activities — they are the same analytical process applied at different scales.

At the site scale, you analyze: slope, aspect, drainage patterns, existing vegetation, prevailing winds, solar access, views, adjacent land uses, pedestrian and vehicular access, hazard zones.

At the urban scale, you analyze: topographic patterns across a watershed, green corridors connecting parks and open spaces, prevailing transportation routes, zoning patterns, existing infrastructure capacity, hazard maps.

The tools are the same — maps, analysis, judgment about what the land can support — applied at different resolutions. Candidates who are strong in site planning but struggle with urban planning often have not made this connection explicit. The macro and micro are the same analytical discipline at different scales.

<ExamTip>Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA), required under PD 1586, operates across scales — from the site (direct impacts) to the regional (cumulative and downstream impacts). Understanding EIA as a multi-scale tool connects site planning, environmental design, and governance in one framework.</ExamTip>

## Connections to Architectural Design

The Site Planning and Environmental Analysis subtopic is the most direct bridge between Planning and Architectural Design. The ALE treats site planning as a shared competency — questions about building orientation, setbacks, lot coverage, floor area ratio, and access can appear in either subject area.

Key overlapping concepts:

**Floor Area Ratio (FAR) and Lot Coverage Ratio (LCR).** These are planning controls that directly shape building form. A site with FAR 3.0 and LCR 0.6 allows you to build up to 60% of the lot on any given floor and up to 3x the lot area in total floor space. Understanding these controls is essential for both site planning and architectural design.

**Setbacks.** The National Building Code (PD 1096) sets minimum setbacks; zoning ordinances may set additional ones. Knowing the source of each setback requirement (building code vs. zoning) helps you answer questions about which agency or instrument controls it.

**Parking requirements.** Both planning and design questions may ask about minimum parking provision. The standards differ by land use type and are set by the National Building Code and local zoning ordinances.

<Tip>The connection between planning and design is most visible in the site plan — the drawing that shows a building in its site context, with setbacks, landscaping, parking, access drives, and open space all shown. If you are comfortable reading and drawing site plans, you are already thinking across the planning-design boundary.</Tip>

## Connections to Laws & Codes

Planning, Housing & Urban Design and Laws & Codes share a substantial body of legislation. The key to navigating the overlap is understanding that the same law often has both planning implications (what can be built where) and legal implications (who is liable, what penalties apply, what rights are protected).

**PD 957 — Subdivision and Condominium Buyers' Protective Decree:**
- Planning angle: sets minimum subdivision standards (road widths, open space, community facilities)
- Laws angle: protects buyers, sets developer obligations, defines what triggers government intervention

**BP 220 — Economic and Socialized Housing:**
- Planning angle: sets reduced minimum standards for affordable housing developments
- Laws angle: defines the legal categories of housing (socialized, economic, low-cost) and the regulatory requirements for each

**RA 7279 — Urban Development and Housing Act (UDHA):**
- Planning angle: mandates socialized housing components in commercial developments; governs resettlement
- Laws angle: protects informal settlers' rights; sets eviction and demolition procedures

**RA 7160 — Local Government Code:**
- Planning angle: mandates CLUP and Zoning Ordinance; grants LGUs authority over local land use decisions
- Laws angle: defines LGU powers and limitations; establishes accountability for local officials

## Connections to Environmental Design

Landscape Architecture and Ecology connects directly to the Environmental Design subject area (ALE-ENV). The shared content includes:

- Bioclimatic design principles (passive solar, natural ventilation, daylighting) — tested in Environmental Design as building-scale strategies, and in Planning as urban-scale considerations (street orientation, tree canopy, urban heat island mitigation)
- Stormwater management — tested in Environmental Design as a site drainage challenge, and in Planning as a green infrastructure and low-impact development strategy
- Ecological sensitivity zones — wetlands, flood-prone areas, steep slopes — that constrain both site planning decisions and building design choices

The ALE sometimes presents a scenario where environmental constraints (a mangrove buffer, a landslide hazard zone, a floodway) overlap with planning decisions (where to site roads, buildings, or open spaces). These questions are testing your ability to integrate environmental analysis with planning judgment.

## The SPP of Planning: The CLUP Process

Just as Professional Practice has the SPP document chain, Planning has its own sequential process — the CLUP cycle — that structures how local planning works in the Philippines.

1. **Ecological Profile / Situational Analysis** — Data gathering on the municipality's physical, demographic, economic, and institutional characteristics
2. **Land Use Analysis** — Existing land use mapping; identification of constraints, potentials, and conflicts
3. **Vision-Mission-Goals-Objectives** — The community's aspirations for its future development
4. **Land Use Planning** — Allocation of land to use zones based on analysis and vision
5. **CLUP Document** — The policy document integrating all of the above
6. **Zoning Ordinance** — The implementing instrument enacted by the Sanggunian
7. **Implementation, Monitoring, and Evaluation** — Ongoing review, enforcement, and updating

<ElaborativeInterrogation question="Why does the CLUP need to be updated regularly, and what triggers a mandatory review?" answer="The CLUP is a long-range policy document, typically covering 10 years. DHSUD guidelines generally require LGUs to update their CLUPs every 6 to 10 years, with monitoring and evaluation conducted annually." />

## Integration Patterns

**Planning + Design + Laws:** The most complex ALE questions integrate all three. A scenario presents a development project, asks you to identify the applicable standards (planning), the design implications (architectural design), and the legal requirements (laws). Practice working through scenarios from all three angles simultaneously.

**Planning + History:** The history of planning is not just background — it explains why current Philippine planning instruments are structured the way they are. The influence of American colonial planning (the Burnham Plan for Manila and Baguio, the surveyed grid of American-period subdivisions) is visible in contemporary Philippine urbanism. Knowing this history makes the present more legible.

**Planning + 21st Century Innovation:** Questions about transit-oriented development, smart city frameworks, or resilience planning are increasingly present. These are not abstract — they are being applied in Philippine cities right now. Anchor your understanding of these frameworks to specific Philippine examples (the BGC transit spine, the Metro Manila Greenway Project, the Pasig River corridor) and they become much easier to recall under exam conditions.

The goal of mastery in Planning, Housing & Urban Design is to think like a planner: to see a site or a community and understand the scales of intervention available, the legislation that governs those interventions, and the design strategies that can make them work for the people who will live in them.
