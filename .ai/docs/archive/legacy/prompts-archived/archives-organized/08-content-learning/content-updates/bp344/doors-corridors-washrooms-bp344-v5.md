## Before You Memorize a Single Number

BP 344 exists because of a specific failure: buildings were being designed for people who can walk, and everyone else was excluded. A person in a wheelchair couldn't get through the entrance, couldn't navigate the corridor, couldn't use the toilet. The law's entire purpose — and the purpose of every dimension in this article — is to make ordinary movement possible for a person whose mobility aid takes up space.

That's the lens for this topic. When you encounter a dimension you don't understand, the question to ask is always: *what movement is this making possible?* The answer will make the number unforgettable.

### The one thing to hold in your head

Everything in BP 344 is designed around a wheelchair user who needs **1,800 mm** to complete a full turn. That turning circle is the governing dimension. It drives the toilet cubicle size, the door approach space, the corridor turnabout bay, the vestibule, the maneuvering space outside the stall. Once you see the 1,800mm circle as the "why" behind all of these, the numbers stop feeling arbitrary.

| Wheelchair dimensions | Value |
|---|---|
| Length | 1,100 mm |
| Width | 800 mm |
| **Full turning circle (diameter)** | **1,800 mm** |
| Comfortable vertical reach | 700–1,200 mm above floor |
| Comfortable horizontal reach | 500–900 mm |
| Minimum clear space (wheelchair + occupant width) | 900 mm |
| Knee/leg clearance under tables and fixtures | 700 mm |

These are the source values. Every table in this article follows from them.

### How to use this article

Read it top to bottom the first time — don't jump to the tables. The prose is doing work: it explains why each dimension exists. After that first read, the tables serve as quick reference for review sessions. The drill at the bottom is for final consolidation before exam day.

## What This Topic Covers in the ALE

Accessible design in the ALE is not just about knowing dimensions. It sits at the intersection of three things that are tested independently and together:

**Building codes (Area A and B):** You need to know the specific values, the source section, and how to apply them to a described building scenario.

**Professional practice (Area B):** As the architect of record, you are personally liable for code compliance. The 2024 IRR explicitly names architects as individuals liable for violations.

**Design application (Area C):** A floor plan with accessible elements is presented. You identify which elements violate BP 344 and explain why. This is where students who only memorized numbers fail — because the plan requires spatial understanding, not recall.

The three ALE question patterns for this topic are:

**Pattern 1 — Direct recall:** "What is the minimum clear opening width for an accessible door?" → **900 mm**. No reasoning needed, just the value. These questions reward memorization.

**Pattern 2 — Scenario application:** "A proposed corridor is 1,350 mm wide. Is this compliant for a building where wheelchair users and ambulant persons must pass?" → No. The 2024 IRR requires **1,500 mm**. These questions reward understanding the condition.

**Pattern 3 — Violation identification (Area C):** A floor plan shows an accessible washroom. One or more elements violate BP 344. You must identify them. This rewards knowing both the value *and* the reasoning — because the wrong element may look plausible if you only memorized a number without understanding what it's measuring.

## The Three Laws and How They Relate

Three laws govern this topic. Understand the relationship before exam day so you're never confused about which law applies to which question.

**Batas Pambansa Blg. 344 (1982) — the Accessibility Law.** This is the technical standard. It tells architects exactly what to build — dimensions, materials, hardware specifications. Administered by the DPWH; enforced by city/municipal engineers through the building permit process. The **2024 Revised IRR** is its current implementation document. When a question asks about a specific dimension, the answer comes from here.

**Republic Act No. 7277 (1992) — the Magna Carta for Disabled Persons.** This is the rights framework. It doesn't specify dimensions — it establishes that PWDs have the legal right to accessible buildings, transportation, and public spaces. Think of it as the "why this matters legally" law.

**Republic Act No. 9442 (2007) — the Enforcement Amendment.** This amends RA 7277 by adding criminal liability. First violation: ₱50,000–₱100,000 fine or 6 months–2 years imprisonment. Subsequent violations: ₱100,000–₱200,000 or 2–6 years. Architects are explicitly listed as persons liable. When an ALE question asks about *penalties* for non-compliance, the answer comes from here.

The practical relationship: **BP 344 tells you what to build. RA 7277 says why it's legally required. RA 9442 says what happens if you don't.**

> **Source:** BP 344 (1982); RA 7277 (1992); RA 9442 (2007); BP 344 2024 Revised IRR

## A Note on the 2024 Revised IRR

The 2024 Revised IRR raised multiple dimensions from the earlier 2013 Illustrated IRR. Most review materials and review centers still use 2013 values — which means students who studied from older books or notes are carrying wrong numbers into the exam.

Every value in this article reflects the **2024 Revised IRR**. Where a value changed from 2013, it is marked ⟳. The most consequential changes are:

- Corridor width (single wheelchair + ambulant): **1,200 mm → 1,500 mm**
- Toilet cubicle: **1,700 × 1,800 mm → 1,800 × 1,800 mm** (now a square)
- Door approach space: **1,500 × 1,500 mm → 1,800 × 1,800 mm**
- Threshold maximum: **19 mm → 12 mm**
- Urinal lip: **480 mm → 350–400 mm**

If you see these older values in review materials, notes from professors, or multiple-choice options on a practice set — be aware that they reflect superseded standards.

## Accessible Entrances

Before a wheelchair user reaches a door, corridor, or toilet, they have to enter the building. Accessible entrance design solves for this first step.

All public entrances must be accessible — including those serving leased spaces, parking structures, pedestrian tunnels, and elevated walkways. Where the entrance is not at the same level as the site arrival grade, a ramp, wheelchair lift, or similar mechanism must be provided.

**Vestibule** ⟳: Where an entrance has a vestibule — the transitional space between exterior and interior — it must be large enough for a wheelchair to be fully inside with the outer door closed before the inner door opens. That's why the minimum is **1,800 mm × 1,800 mm** (depth × width). One wheelchair turning circle, in a box.

**Glass doors and panels:** A common safety failure is that frameless transparent glass is invisible. BP 344 requires horizontal or graphical patterns with minimum 30% luminance contrast, positioned between **800 mm and 1,500 mm** above the floor — within the standing and seated eye-level zone.

**Weather protection:** Accessible entrances, including all ramps and stairs serving the entrance, must have full weather protection — a canopy or similar structure — to protect from rain and heat.

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Vestibule level area (depth × width) ⟳ | **1,800 × 1,800 mm** | C.1.4 |
| Glass door/panel contrast zone | 800–1,500 mm above floor | C.1.5 |

> **Source:** BP 344 2024 Revised IRR, Part C, Section 1

## Doors

A standard interior door in the Philippines is typically 800–900 mm wide. An accessible door must clear **900 mm** — measured from the face of the fully open door at 90° to the door jamb, not from the door frame to the opposite jamb. That distinction matters because a partially-open door is a narrower gap than a fully-open one measured correctly.

### The approach space

Before passing through the door, a wheelchair user must approach it, stop, reach the hardware, and open it — while maneuvering their 1,800 mm turning circle in front of it. The code requires **1,800 × 1,800 mm** of clear, level space on both sides of the door ⟳. This increased in 2024 from the previous 1,500 × 1,500 mm.

The exception: where a door opens *onto* but not *into* a corridor (meaning the door swings away from the corridor, not into it), the corridor side may be reduced to a **1,500 mm** minimum corridor width.

### The threshold

A threshold is the strip at the base of a door frame. Even a small raised threshold can catch a wheelchair wheel, tip forward mobility aids, or trip ambulant PWDs. The 2024 IRR sets the maximum at **12 mm** ⟳ (down from 19 mm). If a threshold exceeds **6 mm**, it must be beveled at a gradient of **1:8** — a gentle ramp profile, not a sharp edge.

### Hardware

The handle must be operable with one hand without twisting, grasping, or fine finger manipulation. This means **lever-type only** — knob-type hardware is prohibited on accessible routes. Handles are centered at **900 mm** above the floor. Vertical pull handles are preferred for swing doors.

For door closers: the interior door closer maximum force is **2.0 kg** ⟳ (down from 4.0 kg in 2013). This matters because a door that requires significant force to open is inaccessible in practice even if the width is technically compliant.

|---|---|---|
| Minimum clear door width | **900 mm** | C.2.1 |
| Double-leaf door minimum | **1,800 mm** | C.2.3 |
| Clear level space (both sides) ⟳ | **1,800 × 1,800 mm** | C.2.6 |
| Exception — corridor side ⟳ | **1,500 mm** minimum | C.2.6 |
| Interior door closer max force ⟳ | **2.0 kg** | C.2.4 |
| Hardware height (centered) | **900 mm** | C.2.11 |
| Hardware type | Lever-type — knob type prohibited | C.2.10 |
| Secondary peephole height ⟳ | **1,000 mm** | C.2.14 |
| Kick plate height | 300–400 mm | C.2.13 |
| Threshold maximum height ⟳ | **12 mm** | C.3.1 |
| Threshold bevel trigger | Above **6 mm** → bevel at 1:8 | C.3.1 |

## Switches and Controls

A light switch that a wheelchair user cannot reach without straining is an accessibility failure. The 2024 IRR places manual switches at **900–1,200 mm** above the floor ⟳ (the old lower bound was 920 mm). Switches must also be close enough to the door that a person approaching the door can reach them without repositioning — **200 mm maximum** from the latch side of the door.

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Switch height range ⟳ | **900–1,200 mm** | C.4.1 |
| Max distance from door latch side | **200 mm** | C.4.2 |

## Corridors

### Width

A wheelchair is 800 mm wide. A person walking alongside needs approximately 600 mm. That sum is 1,400 mm, and the 2024 IRR adds a buffer to arrive at **1,500 mm** ⟳ as the minimum corridor width for a wheelchair user and an ambulant person to pass each other. The old standard was 1,200 mm — this is the most consequential change in the 2024 IRR for typical building design, and the most likely to appear in questions.

Where two wheelchair users must pass simultaneously — a higher-traffic scenario — the minimum is **1,800 mm**, the full double-turning-circle dimension.

### Turnabout spaces

A wheelchair user in a long corridor must be able to reverse direction. Turnabout bays — zones wide enough to execute a full turn — are required at regular intervals and at every dead end. Under the 2024 IRR, each turnabout must be **1,800 × 1,800 mm** ⟳, spaced no more than **12,000 mm (12 m)** apart. A dead-end corridor must have a turnabout within **3,500 mm** of the dead end.

### Surface and slope

Corridors must be slip-resistant and level. The maximum longitudinal slope before a corridor becomes a ramp is **1:20 (5%)**. Maximum cross gradient is **1:100 (1%)**. Surface interruptions — cracks, expansion joints, grating gaps — must not create edges above **6 mm**.

| Parameter | Value (2024 IRR) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Width — single wheelchair + ambulant ⟳ | **1,500 mm** | C.5.1 |
| Width — two wheelchairs | **1,800 mm** | C.5.2 |
| Turnabout dimensions ⟳ | **1,800 × 1,800 mm** | C.5.2 |
| Max spacing between turnabouts | **12,000 mm** | C.5.2 |
| Dead-end turnabout distance | Within **3,500 mm** | C.5.3 |
| Maximum longitudinal slope | 1:20 (5%) | B.3.2 |
| Maximum cross gradient | 1:100 (1%) | B.3.3 |
| Maximum surface interruption | 6 mm | B.3.6 |
| Maximum grating gap | 13 mm center-to-center | B.3.5 |

## Accessible Washrooms and Toilets

This is the most tested section of BP 344 in the ALE, and the section most changed by the 2024 IRR. Work through it carefully.

### The problem it's solving

An accessible toilet must allow a wheelchair user to: enter the cubicle, close the door behind them, turn to face the toilet, transfer from the wheelchair to the toilet seat, use the grab bars for support, and reverse the process to exit. Each dimension in this section corresponds to one of those movements.

### How many accessible toilets are required

The 2024 IRR requires at least **one accessible female and one accessible male cubicle** on every floor with 20 or fewer WCs total. This is per gender, per floor.

### The cubicle ⟳

The accessible toilet cubicle is now a **1,800 × 1,800 mm square** in the 2024 IRR. The old asymmetric plan (1,700 × 1,800 mm) is superseded. The square reflects the 1,800 mm full turning circle — a wheelchair must be able to rotate fully inside or just outside the stall.

There must also be **1,800 × 1,800 mm** of clear turning space outside the cubicle ⟳, before the stall door. This is where the wheelchair user maneuvers to approach and enter the stall.

The stall door must swing **outward** — or be sliding or folding. This is non-negotiable, and the code gives an explicit safety reason: so that a rescuer can open the door from outside if the occupant has fallen and is lying behind the door. The lock must be openable from outside with a coin or simple device.

### Water closet position

The WC seat height is **450 mm** above finished floor — slightly higher than a standard residential toilet, designed for easier transfer from a wheelchair.

The WC must be positioned with its centerline **450 mm** from the finished face of the side wall ⟳. The grab bar's outer face is **100 mm** from the wall, and the grab bar's face to the WC centerline is **350 mm**. These three values define the precise spatial relationship between wall, grab bar, and toilet — and all three can appear in exam questions independently.

### Grab bars ⟳

The grab bar is the piece of equipment a person uses to push themselves up from the seat, lower themselves onto it, and stabilize during use. Two types are required in every accessible cubicle.

**Fixed L-type grab bar** — mounted on the side wall. The horizontal leg runs parallel to the toilet seat from the back wall, extending to **300 mm** past the front of the WC. The vertical leg rises from the elbow of the L. The critical height: the top of the grab bar must be exactly **300 mm** above the top of the toilet seat ⟳ — the 2024 IRR made this an exact value, not the 280–300 mm range in the 2013 version. Since the seat is at 450 mm above the floor, the grab bar top is at approximately **750 mm** above finished floor.

**Flip-up grab bar** — on the open side of the toilet, for lateral transfer. It extends **100 mm** past the front of the WC, at the same height (**300 mm** above the seat). It must be capable of supporting **200 kg** minimum.

Both grab bars: diameter **30–40 mm** ⟳ (changed from 38–45 mm), wall clearance **50 mm** minimum.

### Lavatory

The lavatory is positioned **outside** the 1,800 × 1,800 mm cubicle footprint — it does not eat into the maneuvering space. Its centerline is **450 mm** from the adjacent wall. Rim height is **800 mm**. Clear knee space below: minimum **650 mm** vertical clearance. Faucet type must be lever, push, or sensor — nothing that requires gripping or wrist rotation.

### Urinals ⟳

The 2024 IRR made significant changes to urinal requirements. The urinal type is wall-hung with an elongated lip. The maximum lip height dropped from 480 mm to **350–400 mm** ⟳ — bringing the control zone within seated wheelchair reach. Clear floor space in front changed from the old 750 × 1,200 mm rectangle to a **900 × 900 mm square** ⟳. Grab bars are required on both sides, **600 mm** long, with the top edge at **1,500 mm** above finished floor.

### Roll-in shower ⟳

Roll-in showers allow wheelchair users to remain seated during bathing. The minimum dimension is now **1,800 × 1,800 mm** ⟳ (was 1,500 × 1,500 mm). The maximum curb height is **12 mm** ⟳. L-shaped grab bars are required. The shower spray hose must be at least **1,500 mm** long to reach from a seated position.

## Signage ⟳

The ISA (International Symbol of Access) — white figure on UN Blue background (#5B92E5) — must face right. Directional arrows are added when the accessible facility is to the left.

Two significant changes in 2024: Door-mounted signage for accessible toilets and showers is now **200 × 200 mm** ⟳ (doubled from 100 × 100 mm). Wall and door signage must be mounted with the center of the sign at a maximum of **1,500 mm** above finished floor ⟳ (previously expressed as a 1,400–1,600 mm range).

All signage must have minimum 30% luminance contrast from surroundings. Braille must be included on labels for public areas.

| Parameter | Value (2024 IRR) | Source |

| Accessible toilet/shower door sign ⟳ | **200 × 200 mm** | A.7.7 |
| Wall/door sign — max height to center ⟳ | **1,500 mm** | A.7.6 |

## Common Mistakes

Now that you have the context, here are the ten ways students most often lose marks on this topic. Each one maps to something explained above.

**1. Using the old corridor width.** The 2024 IRR raised the single-wheelchair + ambulant minimum from 1,200 mm to **1,500 mm** (C.5.1). Review materials from before 2024 universally show 1,200 mm. If your notes say 1,200 mm for a general accessible corridor, they're wrong.

**2. Making the cubicle rectangular.** The 2024 cubicle is a **square — 1,800 × 1,800 mm**. The old 1,700 × 1,800 mm asymmetric plan no longer applies. Any answer option with 1,700 mm as a cubicle dimension is testing the superseded standard.

**3. Threshold maximum.** Students memorize 19 mm (old value). The 2024 maximum is **12 mm**. The 6 mm bevel trigger did not change — but the absolute maximum did.

**4. Door approach space.** Students expect 1,500 × 1,500 mm and get confused when 1,800 × 1,800 mm appears. These are the same value expressed in two different IRR editions. The 2024 standard is **1,800 × 1,800 mm**.

**5. Grab bar height reference point.** The 300 mm measurement is from the **top of the toilet seat** to the **top of the grab bar** — not from the floor. The floor measurement (~750 mm) is a consequence, not the specification.

**6. WC centerline confusion.** The WC centerline is **450 mm** from the finished wall. Students sometimes recall 750 mm (an old ambiguous reference). 450 mm is the explicit 2024 value.

**7. Urinal lip height.** The old standard was 480 mm. The 2024 standard is **350–400 mm** — a drop of 80–130 mm. This is the largest absolute change in the urinal section.

**8. Door swing.** Accessible toilet stall doors swing **outward**. The reason is specific: a rescuer must be able to open the door if someone has collapsed behind it. Students who just memorized "outward" without the reason misapply it in Area C scenarios where the plan isn't labeled.

**9. Turnabout dimensions.** The 2024 turnabout is **1,800 × 1,800 mm** — not the old 1,500 × 1,500 mm. The maximum spacing (12 m) and dead-end distance (3,500 mm) did not change.

**10. Door closer force.** The 2024 interior door closer maximum is **2.0 kg**, not 4.0 kg. Students who memorized 4.0 kg from older sources are carrying the wrong value.

> **The primary source for this topic is the official document. Please read it.** This wiki article is an explanation and study aid — it does not replace direct engagement with the code text. The Official Gazette publication is publicly available.

| Source | How to use it |
|---|---|
| **BP 344 2024 Revised IRR** — Official Gazette Vol. 120 No. 28, July 8, 2024 (effective August 8, 2024) | **Read this first.** Primary source for all dimensional values. Promulgated by DPWH and DOTr, coordinated with NCDA. Contains diagrams that this article cannot fully replicate |
| **Batas Pambansa Blg. 344 (1982)** | The enabling statute — read Section 1 (Declaration of Policy) to understand the original intent of the law |
| **Republic Act No. 9442 (2007)** | Read Section 46 for the criminal liability provisions — ALE frequently tests penalty ranges |
| **BP 344 Illustrated IRR (2013 Draft)** | Useful for diagrams and spatial references, but dimensional values are superseded by the 2024 IRR. Use with caution |
| **Republic Act No. 7277 (1992)** | Magna Carta for Disabled Persons — rights framework behind BP 344 |

> **⟳ values:** All dimensions marked ⟳ in this article changed between the 2013 Illustrated IRR and the 2024 Revised IRR. If a value in your notes or review book does not match the value in this article, the 2024 IRR takes precedence for exam purposes. Always verify against the current official text.
