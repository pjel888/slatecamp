# FLAR/FAR and BHL Interconnectivity in Philippine Building Law

## Overview

Under the 2004 Revised Implementing Rules and Regulations (RIRR) of Presidential Decree No. 1096 — the National Building Code of the Philippines (NBCP) — the Floor-to-Lot Area Ratio (FLAR, identical to the Floor Area Ratio/FAR) and the Building Height Limit (BHL) are not independent controls. They are part of an interlocking system of Development Controls (DCs) that must be applied **sequentially and collectively** to determine the lawful building bulk on any given lot. Simply put: neither FLAR nor BHL can be used in isolation. Each constrains the other in practice, and the result that is most restrictive governs the final design.[^1][^2]

***

## Defining the Controls

### FLAR / FAR

The **Floor-to-Lot Area Ratio (FLAR)** — also referred to interchangeably as FAR — is a Development Control that limits the total Gross Floor Area (GFA) that can be lawfully generated on a given lot.[^3][^1]

**Formula:**
\[ \text{FLAR} = \frac{\text{GFA}}{\text{TLA}} \]

Where:
- **GFA** = Gross Floor Area (total enclosed usable floor space, excluding parking, driveways, and uncovered areas)
- **TLA** = Total Lot Area

Practically: if a lot is 1,000 sqm with a prescribed FLAR of 3.0, the maximum GFA is 3,000 sqm.[^4][^5]

The NBCP's 2004 RIRR Table VII.G.1 prescribes both **minimum FLAR** (to prevent underdevelopment) and **maximum FLAR** (to prevent overdevelopment). Each zone classification has a specific FLAR range with a corresponding BHL embedded in the designation itself:[^6]

| Zone | FLAR Range | Corresponding BHL |
|------|-----------|-------------------|
| R-1 (Low-density residential) | 1.50 | 3 storeys / 10.00 m |
| R-2 Basic | 1.30–1.50 | 3 storeys / 10.00 m |
| R-2 Maximum | 2.10–3.00 | 5 storeys / 15.00 m |
| R-3 Basic | 1.80–2.10 | 3 storeys / 10.00 m |
| R-3 Maximum | 7.10–8.10 | 12 storeys / 36.00 m |
| R-5 (Condominiums) | 6.00–9.00 | 18 storeys / 54.00 m |
| Commercial 1 | 1.70–3.00 | 5 storeys / 15.00 m |
| Commercial 2 | 3.60–9.00 | 15 storeys / 45.00 m |
| Commercial 3 | 9.00–34.00 | 60 storeys / 180.00 m |

*Source: NBCP 2004 RIRR, Table VII.G.1*[^7][^8]

This table design is deliberate: **FLAR and BHL are bundled together per zone**. A higher FLAR designation inherently implies a higher BHL allowance, because you need more height to accommodate the greater floor area on a fixed footprint.[^9]

### BHL

The **Building Height Limit (BHL)** is the maximum height allowed for a building based on its proposed use/occupancy. It is:[^10][^1]

- Measured from the established grade line to the topmost portion of the building (excluding permitted projections like antennae, masts, and signage)[^11][^10]
- Dependent on zoning classification (Table VII.2 of the NBCP 2004 RIRR)[^12]
- Further constrained by Road Right-of-Way (RROW) width (e.g., a 3m RROW limits the BHL to 2 storeys/6m; a 6–7m RROW limits it to 3 storeys/9m)[^9]
- **Critically: "generally determined after the application of other Development Controls"** — meaning BHL is not the first control applied; FLAR effectively precedes it in the determination sequence[^1][^10]

The BHL is also subject to clearance from the Civil Aviation Authority of the Philippines (CAAP) if the site is near an operating airport. In Metro Manila (near NAIA), CAAP sets an overriding cap of 250 meters.[^13][^1]

***

## The Sequential Application of Development Controls

The NBCP 2004 RIRR explicitly mandates that its Development Controls must **never be applied in isolation**, but rather in a **specific sequential order**. The Architecture Advocacy International Foundation (AAIF) has documented and emphasized this correct sequence extensively:[^2]

### Step-by-Step Sequence (Rule VII and Rule VIII)

1. **Determine zoning classification** — this sets the zone, which governs permissible use/occupancy and the baseline FLAR and BHL ranges[^14]
2. **Apply setbacks and yard requirements** — from Table VIII.2 (residential) or VIII.3 (commercial/institutional/industrial), derive the **Allowable Maximum Building Footprint (AMBF)**[^6][^14]
3. **Confirm Percentage of Site Occupancy (PSO)** — compare the setback-derived PSO against the PSO allowed by Table VII.1; the more restrictive value governs[^15]
4. **Compute AMBF** — the actual ground-level footprint within which the building envelope sits[^14]
5. **Apply FLAR to get GFA** — `GFA = TLA × FLAR` (from Table VII.G.1)[^8][^6]
6. **Compute resultant height** — divide GFA by the per-floor usable area to get the implied number of floors, then multiply by desired floor-to-floor height to get the resultant height[^16]
7. **Check resultant height against BHL** — if the height implied by the FLAR exceeds the prescribed BHL, the BHL prevails and the GFA must be reduced (or basement solutions explored)[^17][^7]
8. **Compute AMVB** — `AMVB = AMBF × BHL (in meters)`, then apply angular plane adjustments from the RROW centerline to finalize the building volume envelope[^8]
9. **Compute TGFA** — `TGFA = BHL (in number of floors) × AMBF`, and confirm it does not exceed the AMVB; if exceeded, TGFA must be reduced because **the AMVB always prevails**[^17]

***

## The Core Interaction: Does a Higher FLAR Adjust the BHL?

This is the crux of the question and the answer is: **yes, they are mutually conditioning, but within prescribed limits — and the AMVB (hence BHL) is the ultimate ceiling**.

### How a Higher FLAR Can Effectively Allow More Height

Within the NBCP framework, FLAR is designated as the **primary or initial determinant of building bulk**. When a higher FLAR is prescribed (or approved through a local zoning ordinance), it necessarily implies a higher GFA demand. Since the footprint (AMBF) is fixed by setbacks and PSO, the only way to accommodate more GFA is to build *taller*. This is why Table VII.G.1 embeds a corresponding BHL alongside each FLAR range:[^7][^8]

> *"A building with a higher FLAR designation may be built higher than one with a lower FLAR designation."*[^9]

For example:
- An R-3 Maximum lot with FLAR 7.10–8.10 has a corresponding BHL of 12 storeys / 36m.
- An R-3 Basic lot with FLAR 1.80–2.10 has a BHL of only 3 storeys / 10m.

The higher FLAR does not *automatically change* the BHL as a free variable — rather, the BHL for a higher FLAR zone is already set higher by the Code to accommodate the additional bulk.

### How a Lower PSO/AMBF Can Push the Building Taller

Conversely, with building volume (AMVB) treated as roughly constant:

> *"The volume/bulk being constant, a building with a lesser PSO or AMBF may be built higher than one with a higher PSO."*[^9]

In other words, if setbacks reduce the usable footprint, the building can be designed taller (up to BHL) to recover the same floor area. A narrow, deep tower on a small AMBF can achieve the same TGFA as a wide, shorter building — as long as BHL is not breached.

### When the BHL Caps and Limits the FLAR in Practice

The more common and practically important scenario is the **reverse**: the BHL acts as a hard ceiling that restricts how much of the prescribed FLAR can actually be realized. This happens when:

- The AMBF is small (due to large setbacks or a narrow lot), and even building to full BHL does not generate enough floor area to reach the maximum FLAR.
- The RROW is narrow, which further restricts BHL independent of zoning (e.g., a 3m RROW caps height at 2 storeys/6m, overriding higher zonal BHL)[^9]
- CAAP clearances apply (especially near airports), imposing an absolute height cap below what the zoning and FLAR would otherwise allow[^13][^1]
- Incremental setback requirements above ground level (per Rule VIII, Section 804.6) progressively reduce the usable floor plate as the building rises, limiting effective TGFA[^18]

The AAIF has documented that the **resultant FLAR arising from correct sequential application of all DCs** often ends up significantly lower than the maximum permissible FLAR listed in Table VII.G.1. This is the gap between what the Code "grants" on paper vs. what physics, setbacks, and height limits actually allow on a specific lot.[^19]

***

## The AMVB: The Binding Three-Dimensional Constraint

The **Allowable Maximum Volume of Building (AMVB)** is the architectural "box" that synthesizes FLAR and BHL into a single three-dimensional limit:

\[ \text{AMVB} = \text{AMBF} \times \text{BHL (in meters)} \]

The angular plane from the RROW further trims this box. Crucially, **the AMVB always prevails**:[^17]

> *"The Allowable Maximum TGFA should not exceed the Allowable Maximum Volume of Building (AMVB). If exceeded, the Allowable Maximum TGFA must be adjusted since the AMVB must always prevail."*[^17]

This is the reconciliation mechanism. If FLAR yields a GFA that, when stacked within the AMBF, would require a building taller than BHL allows, the TGFA is cut back to fit within the AMVB. The FLAR in effect becomes a *right* that can only be exercised to the extent the AMVB permits it.

***

## TGFA: The Two Calculation Methods and Their Reconciliation

The NBCP 2004 RIRR provides **two methods** for computing the maximum TGFA:

| Method | Formula | Source Table |
|--------|---------|--------------|
| BHL-based | `TGFA = BHL (no. of floors) × AMBF` | Table VII.1 (p.84) |
| FLAR-based | `TGFA = GFA × multiplier` (GFA = TLA × FLAR) | Table VII.G.1 (p.119–120) |

*Source: NBCP 2004 RIRR, Rules VII and VIII*[^6][^8]

These two methods can and do yield different values for a given lot. The **lower of the two** — i.e., the more restrictive result — must be adopted. In practice, this means:[^15]

- If the FLAR-based TGFA is higher than the BHL-based TGFA, the BHL is the binding constraint and the actual GFA will be less than `TLA × FLAR`.
- If the BHL-based TGFA is higher than the FLAR-based TGFA, the FLAR is the binding constraint and the building must not go taller even if BHL technically permits it.

***

## Role of Local Zoning Ordinances

The NBCP provides the national baseline, but **LGU zoning ordinances** can — and routinely do — impose different FAR/FLAR values and BHL limits, provided the result is at least as restrictive as the NBCP:[^20][^3]

> *"The BHL shall only be as allowed under this Rule or under the duly approved city zoning ordinance, **whichever is more restrictive**."*[^17]

Local ordinances can also grant **variances** under prescribed conditions (e.g., undue hardship, no impairment of adjoining properties). Some LGUs with Planned Unit Development (PUD) overlays and high-density commercial zones have pushed FLAR values dramatically higher (e.g., C-3 up to FAR 34 at 60 storeys/180m, and some PUD designations up to FAR 18–34), with correspondingly elevated BHL allowances. Conversely, the Batangas City Zoning Ordinance and similar local codes pair FAR and BHL directly in zone-specific tables, confirming the bundled nature of these two controls at the local level.[^21][^20][^8]

***

## Practical Implications for Architects and Developers

The following summarizes the key practical consequences of the FLAR–BHL relationship:

- **FLAR is the starting point, BHL is the ceiling.** Compute GFA from FLAR first, then check if the implied height to stack that GFA within the AMBF exceeds the BHL. If it does, reduce GFA accordingly.[^7][^8]
- **Basement solutions as a workaround.** When above-grade BHL is the binding constraint, the NBCP guidelines suggest exploring sub-grade (basement) solutions to house additional floor area without breaching BHL. Basement levels can add to TGFA without affecting the height of the above-grade structure.[^7]
- **Narrow RROW is a hidden BHL constraint.** Even on a commercially zoned lot with a high FLAR, a narrow RROW in front will cap the building's height independently of the zoning BHL. This alone can prevent the full FLAR from being realized.[^9]
- **Higher FLAR zones have bundled higher BHLs.** Reclassifying or rezoning a lot to a higher-density zone automatically brings a higher BHL allowance, enabling more of the FLAR to be built above grade.[^8][^7]
- **PSO/setbacks determine AMBF, which multiplied by BHL gives AMVB — the hard limit.** All FLAR and GFA calculations are ultimately bounded by this three-dimensional box.[^14][^17]
- **Never apply DCs in isolation.** The correct approach per NBCP 2004 RIRR is always the sequential, integrated application of all Development Controls. Using FLAR or BHL alone will almost certainly give an incorrect (usually overstated) result.[^2]

***

## Summary of the Relationship

The FLAR and BHL are best understood as **two faces of the same building bulk control**. FLAR defines how much total floor area is permissible in terms of lot area; BHL defines the maximum vertical extent of the building. Together, bounded by the AMVB and constrained by PSO/setbacks, they govern what can physically be built on any lot in the Philippines. When FLAR is higher (more floor area is permitted), the corresponding BHL is also set higher to allow it to be achieved vertically. But BHL — especially as reduced by RROW constraints, CAAP clearances, and incremental setbacks — frequently acts as the harder constraint, and the effective realized FLAR ends up lower than the Code's maximum designation. The most restrictive result from sequential application always governs.
