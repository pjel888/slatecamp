## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal

Identify the three distinct voices of Latin American modernism — Costa (theory, analysis), Niemeyer (free-form concrete curves, Brasília), Barragán (color, light, walls, water, Mexico) — and recognize their major works and philosophical contributions.
## Core Concept

Latin American modernism rejected European Bauhaus rigidity in favor of sculptural form, bold color, and integration with landscape and indigenous culture. While European modernists pursued universal rationalism, Latin Americans asked: How does modernism speak to our climate, our political independence, our sense of place?

**Lúcio Costa** (1902–1998) founded the modernist movement in Brazil. He synthesized European functionalism with Portuguese colonial architecture and created a distinctly Brazilian approach: sensuous curves, open planning, integration with gardens and natural light. His philosophy rejected both nostalgic regionalism and foreign imposition.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Note" status="preserved"]

**Oscar Niemeyer** (1907–2012) apprenticed under Costa, then revolutionized concrete form. He abandoned the straight line—"It is not the right angle that attracts me, nor the straight line, hard and inflexible, created by man. What attracts me is the free and sensual curve—the curve that I find in the mountains of my country, in the sinuous course of its rivers, in the body of the beloved woman." His sculptural concrete forms became iconic: sweeping cantilevered roofs, undulating walls, circular ramps, and spaces that feel alive rather than geometric. **Brasília** (designed with urban planner Lúcio Costa, inaugurated April 21, 1960) is his masterwork—a planned capital city where government buildings float on pilotis, where horizontal and vertical scales dwarf the individual, where modernism becomes monumental theater.

**Luis Barragán** (1902–1988, Mexican) pursued an opposite path: intimacy through color, emotion, and spiritual experience. He rejected the white-box international style, instead using vivid walls—magenta, yellow, blue—to create psychological spaces. His **Pritzker Prize citation (1980)** praised his "almost incomparable sense of color" and his resistance to modernism's coldness. His houses are private sanctuaries, not public monuments. His fountains and water channels recall Islamic gardens and Mexican baroque drama. Barragán proved modernism could be warm, sensory, and culturally rooted.

## Key Terms

**Free-Form Concrete** — Sculptural structural system using reinforced concrete; allowed curved, cantileered forms impossible in earlier materials; central to Niemeyer's formal language.

**Pilotis** — Slender columns elevating a building above ground; Le Corbusier's principle; used in Brasília to separate building mass from terrain and allow circulation below.

**Monumental Modernism** — Large-scale, geometrically pure modernist architecture emphasizing collective rather than individual experience; exemplified by Brasília's government axis.

**Emotional Color** — Barragán's use of vivid, non-neutral walls to evoke psychological and spiritual responses; rejects modernism's white-box aesthetic.

**Brasília** — Planned modernist capital city (completed 1960); designed by Costa (urban plan) and Niemeyer (architecture); intended as utopian symbol of Brazilian progress.

**Cantilever** — Horizontal structural element projecting beyond its support; common in Niemeyer's buildings to create drama and emphasize concrete's strength.

**Brazilian Modernism** — Synthesis of European functionalism with Portuguese colonial tradition, tropical climate, and landscape integration; pioneered by Lúcio Costa.

**Fountain as Spiritual Element** — Barragán's integration of water into private gardens; evokes Islamic gardens, Mexican baroque, and personal contemplation rather than public spectacle.
