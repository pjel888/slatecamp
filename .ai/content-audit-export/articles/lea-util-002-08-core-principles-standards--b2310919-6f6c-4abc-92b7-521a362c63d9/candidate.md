## What You Need to Know for the LEA

Elevator traffic analysis is the link between building population, service quality, car size, and the space architects must reserve for vertical transportation. The checked source treats elevator selection as a simultaneous study of service, economics, and architectural integration.

## Selection Criteria

Salvan states that elevator selection for anything beyond the simplest building considers three interdependent concerns:

- Adequate service for the intended building use.
- Economics of elevator selection.
- Architectural integration of lobbies, shafts, and machine rooms.

The source identifies three service-quality criteria: interval and average waiting time, handling capacity, and travel time. In exam items, a correct answer usually weighs all three rather than optimizing one number while ignoring lobby space or machine-room impact.

## Interval and Waiting Time

Interval is the average time between car departures from the lobby. If passenger arrivals are random, average lobby waiting time is commonly treated as a fraction of the interval. The checked source uses about 60 percent of interval as the industry rule of thumb.

The same section gives office-building waiting-time guidance: 15 to 18 seconds is excellent during up-peak conditions, 22 seconds is good, and 26 seconds is borderline. This is why a design can have enough cars physically present and still fail the service-quality target if interval is too long.

## Handling Capacity

Handling capacity depends on the passenger load carried by each car and how often cars leave the lobby. Salvan explains that the number of cars is not the direct measure; car capacity and interval set how many people the system can clear in a period.

The source uses a five-minute rush period as the standard measure. In simplified LEA form, five-minute handling capacity is the 300-second period multiplied by passengers per car and divided by interval. When the interval is 30 seconds, the system can handle about 10 car-loads in five minutes.

## Population Relationship

Handling capacity becomes meaningful only when compared with building population. Salvan relates system capacity to the percentage of building population moved in five minutes. For office buildings, the checked table places center-city investment offices at about 13 to 15 percent and single-purpose office buildings at about 15 to 18 percent.

For residential and hotel uses, the percentages differ because the traffic pattern differs. That is the board-exam point: do not apply one office peak standard to every occupancy.

## Architectural Space

Traffic analysis is not only arithmetic. The checked source also requires the designer to account for pit depth, hoistway dimensions, overhead clearance, penthouse size, support loads, and machine-room space. The machine room contains major equipment and should allow maintenance movement, including an overhead trolley beam where needed.

Elevator selection therefore affects plan, section, structure, electrical service, and equipment access. A source-safe answer connects traffic calculations with the shaft, lobby, pit, and machine-room consequences.

## LEA Focus

When solving an elevator traffic item:

- Identify occupancy and expected traffic pattern.
- Check interval and average waiting time.
- Check five-minute handling capacity against building population.
- Match car capacity and interval before arguing about car count.
- Reserve shaft, pit, penthouse, machine-room, lobby, and structural support space.
- Avoid a one-size-fits-all standard across offices, residences, hotels, and institutional buildings.
