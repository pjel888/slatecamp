## Quasi-Delict and Tort in Architectural Practice — Civil Code Articles 2176–2194

## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- Define quasi-delict and culpa aquiliana under Civil Code Articles 2176–2194.
- Explain how quasi-delict applies to architectural malpractice.
- Distinguish contractual liability (Article 1723) from tort liability (Article 2176).
- Identify the elements of negligence an injured party must prove.

---
### Quasi-Delict Liability: The Foundation of Negligence-Based Claims

Civil Code Articles 2176–2194 establish the regime of _quasi-delict_ (culpa aquiliana), which allows recovery of damages based on negligence _independent of contract_. Article 2176 provides that _whoever by act or omission causes damage to another, there being fault or negligence, is obliged to pay for the damage done_. This principle is broader than Article 1723's strict liability framework; quasi-delict requires proof of negligence but allows recovery for losses that do not rise to the level of structural collapse. For architects, quasi-delict liability applies when professional negligence causes injury or economic loss to clients, third parties, or neighboring property owners. Unlike Article 1723, quasi-delict claims are not limited to structural defects or building failures; they encompass design errors causing project delays, cost overruns, incomplete functionality, aesthetic defects, and environmental damage. The quasi-delict framework operates independently of contract terms and cannot be waived by service agreements.

### Negligence Elements and Professional Standard of Care

To establish quasi-delict liability, a claimant must prove four elements: (1) the architect owed a duty of care to the claimant; (2) the architect breached that duty through negligent act or omission; (3) the breach caused injury or loss; and (4) the claimant suffered quantifiable damages. The duty of care is measured against the _professional standard of care_—the degree of skill, diligence, and knowledge ordinarily exercised by licensed architects in the Philippines under similar circumstances. An architect who deviates from accepted professional practices, violates building codes, fails to consult specialists when necessary, or takes shortcuts in design analysis breaches this standard. Proof of negligence may come from expert testimony comparing the architect's conduct to professional norms, violation of applicable codes, failure to conduct required site investigations, or documented evidence of preventable errors. The burden of proof rests on the claimant, but once negligence is established, damages flow directly from that breach.

### Scope of Negligent Conduct Recoverable Under Quasi-Delict

Quasi-delict liability extends beyond structural failure to encompass: (1) design defects causing functional problems or safety hazards not amounting to collapse; (2) failure to comply with applicable building codes, zoning regulations, or environmental standards; (3) negligent site selection advice or inadequate geotechnical investigation; (4) design errors causing cost overruns or project delays; (5) failure to identify existing site conditions (contamination, hazards, encroachments); (6) inadequate coordination with structural, MEP, or specialty engineers; (7) negligent construction administration causing quality defects; and (8) failure to provide adequate or accurate project documentation. Architects may also face quasi-delict liability to third parties not their clients—neighboring property owners harmed by design defects affecting lateral support, environmental damage, drainage, or vibration transmission have standing to sue under quasi-delict even without privity of contract with the architect.

### Distinction Between Article 1723 and Quasi-Delict Liability

Article 1723 imposes liability for structural collapse within a **15-year window from the completion of the structure**, requiring the plaintiff to show a design or construction defect caused the collapse. By contrast, quasi-delict requires _proof of negligence_. However, quasi-delict is _broader in scope_ than Article 1723, covering defects and losses that do not cause collapse.

Additionally, quasi-delict claims face a different statute of limitations: under **Article 1146**, civil actions upon a quasi-delict must be instituted within _four (4) years_ from the day the quasi-delict is committed or the cause of action accrues. Conversely, actions based upon a written contract (culpa contractual) prescribe in 10 years under **Article 1144**. This four-year quasi-delict period differs from Article 1723, which dictates that actions must be brought within _10 years following the collapse of the building_ (provided the collapse happens within the 15-year liability window). An architect may face both Article 1723 liability (for structural defects) and quasi-delict liability (for negligence-based damages). The two frameworks are complementary, not mutually exclusive.

### Professional Standards and Good Faith Performance

Civil Code Article 2180 provides that architects are _responsible for damages caused by their employees' negligent acts_ performed in the course of work. This vicarious liability requires architects to maintain professional standards in recruiting, training, and supervising staff. Article 2189 holds employers/building owners liable for negligent acts of employees/contractors under their service; architects' liability for subcontracted professionals follows general quasi-delict principles (Art. 2176) and UAP SPP 206. Articles 2191–2194 further define rules for apportionment of liability when multiple parties contribute to injury or damage. These provisions create a framework where architects bear responsibility for the quality of all work delivered under their name, whether performed directly or through contracted professionals. Architects cannot disclaim liability for third-party errors by merely hiring specialists; they remain answerable to the client unless the client assumes the professional risk.
