## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal

Identify the three-part format of a specification section (Part 1: General, Part 2: Products, Part 3: Execution) and explain the type of information required in each part, including references, submittals, quality assurance, materials, and installation procedures.
## Core Concept

Schedules serve as a bridge between graphical drawings and written specifications. A door schedule lists every door in the project (keyed to room numbers), specifying the type (swing vs. sliding), size, frame material, finish, hardware requirements, and fire rating. Rather than annotating each door on floor plans with detailed notes, a schedule centralizes this information for the contractor and fabricator. Schedules are particularly valuable on large projects where repetition would create drawing clutter.

_Door schedules_ organize information by door mark or room identifier. Typical columns include: Door Mark (A, B, C, or room numbers), Quantity, Type (Single Swing, Double Swing, Sliding), Width, Height, Frame Material (aluminum, steel, wood), Face Material (veneer, paint, anodized), Fire Rating (20-minute, 60-minute, 90-minute), Hardware Set (determined by use: residential, commercial, fire-rated), and Remarks. Door schedules must correspond to door symbols on floor plans and elevations. Each door type is keyed to a mark (e.g., "Door Type A") and referenced on the drawings.

_Window schedules_ follow a similar structure: Window Mark, Quantity, Type (Fixed, Casement, Sliding), Width, Height, Frame Material (aluminum, PVC, wood), Glazing (single, double, triple), U-value (thermal performance), SHGC (solar heat gain coefficient), STC rating (sound transmission), and Finish (natural, painted, anodized). Philippine coastal projects must include additional columns for corrosion resistance and wind load rating. Window schedules are essential for coordinating with structural openings and ensuring that specification performance criteria are achievable.

_Room finish schedules_ tabulate finishes for each room: Room Name, Flooring, Baseboard, Walls, Ceiling, Paint Color, Special Features. This schedule prevents field disputes over which room receives which finish. Cross-referencing room numbers on floor plans to room finish schedules ensures consistency. In hospitals, schools, and large office buildings, room finish schedules are essential.

_Hardware schedules_ specify the lock type, hinge size and material, closer requirements, push/pull hardware, and mounting height for each door type. Rather than specifying hardware on each door type, a single hardware schedule lists all required hardware sets (e.g., "Hardware Set A: Institutional Heavy-Duty," "Hardware Set B: Standard Commercial"). Each door schedule entry references the hardware set.

_Lighting schedules_ tabulate fixture type, wattage, quantity per room, mounting height, color temperature (2700K, 4000K), and control method (on/off, dimming, motion sensor). For electrical coordination, lighting schedules are often developed jointly with the mechanical engineer and building systems designer.

## Philippine Construction Context

Philippine architects use schedules extensively in private commercial practice (office buildings, shopping malls, hotels) but less frequently in small residential projects due to simplicity. Government projects (DPWH, DepEd schools, Department of Health hospitals) mandate schedules for transparency and quality control. The Board of Architecture recommends schedules as best practice in the Architects' Code of Ethics: clear, systematic documentation prevents disputes and demonstrates professional competence.

Schedules are essential in the Philippine climate and regulatory context. Fire-rated door and window schedules ensure compliance with NSCP Chapter 8 (Fire Safety) requirements for occupancy types. Typhoon-resistant window schedules specify wind load ratings per NSCP Chapter 1, which is critical for coastal properties. Room finish schedules address mold resistance and humidity durability for tropical climate. Large public projects (hospitals, schools) use room finish and hardware schedules to ensure accessibility compliance (ADA or equivalent Philippine requirements).

In practice, schedules are often developed by interior designers or specification writers in coordination with the architect. The architect verifies that schedules are complete, internally consistent (door marks match floor plan symbols), and aligned with specification requirements. A door schedule specifying "Fire-Rated 60-minute" must correspond to a Division 08 specification stating "60-minute fire rating per ASTM E152."

## How It's Tested

_Pattern 1 — Schedule Completeness:_ The exam presents an incomplete door schedule (missing columns like Hardware Set, Fire Rating, or Remarks) and asks you to identify what information is missing. Answer: A complete door schedule must include quantities, sizes, frame/face materials, fire ratings (where applicable), hardware assignments, and any special requirements (security, accessibility). Incomplete schedules lead to field changes and cost overruns.

_Pattern 2 — Cross-Referencing:_ Given a floor plan with door symbols and a door schedule, the exam asks you to identify mismatches (e.g., the floor plan shows 8 doors marked "Type A," but the schedule lists only 6). You're tested on your ability to recognize and correct inconsistencies between schedules and drawings.

_Pattern 3 — Coordination with Specifications:_ A window schedule specifies "U-value 0.25" and "SHGC 0.22," but the Division 08 specification states "U-value minimum 0.22." The exam asks whether there's a discrepancy and if so, what the resolution should be. Answer: The window schedule (0.25) is worse (higher U-value) than the specification minimum (0.22), which may be intentional (value selection for cost) or an error. The architect must verify that the schedule aligns with the design intent.

_Pattern 4 — Philippine Regulatory Requirements:_ A window schedule for a coastal commercial building omits wind load rating columns. The exam asks what information should be added for NSCP Chapter 1 compliance. Answer: Wind load rating (e.g., Design Wind Pressure of 2.5 kPa for coastal Typhoon Category 4) must be specified to ensure structural adequacy.

_Pattern 5 — Schedule Use in Construction Administration:_ The exam describes a scenario where a contractor installs a door with the wrong hardware or wrong size. The architect should have used a schedule to prevent this. What information in the schedule would have prevented this error? Answer: Clear, complete schedules with photographs or samples referenced in the schedule prevent field substitutions.

<Tip>
Memory aid for schedules: **Completeness, Consistency, Coordination**. Every schedule must be complete (all columns filled), consistent internally (door marks match drawings), and coordinated with specifications and drawings. A missing door mark or hardware set is a red flag. Always verify schedule accuracy before sending to bidding or construction.
</Tip>
