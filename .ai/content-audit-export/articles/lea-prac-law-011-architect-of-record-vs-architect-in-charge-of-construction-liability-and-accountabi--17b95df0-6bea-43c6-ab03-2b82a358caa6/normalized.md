## Architect-of-Record vs. Architect-in-Charge of Construction — Liability and Accountability

- Distinguish Architect-of-Record (AoR) from Architect-in-Charge of Construction (AiCC).
- Explain the respective liabilities of AoR (design) and AiCC (construction supervision).

### Definition and Scope of Architect-of-Record

The _Architect-of-Record_ is defined in RA 9266, Section 3(a) as the architect registered and licensed under RA 9266 who is _directly and professionally responsible for the total design of the project for the client_ and who _assumes the civil liability for the plans, specifications, and contract documents_ that she has signed and sealed. The AoR is the primary design professional who authors or supervises the preparation of all architectural documents and carries professional accountability for design quality, constructability, code compliance, and fitness for purpose. The AoR signs and seals all drawings and specifications, creating a legal certification that the design meets all applicable standards and laws. This role is singular—only one architect may hold AoR status for a project at a given time, ensuring clear accountability for design decisions. The AoR's liability is direct and personal; she cannot disclaim responsibility for the design by designating someone else to review her work.

### Definition and Scope of Architect-in-Charge of Construction

The _Architect-in-Charge of Construction_ (AiCC), also defined in RA 9266, Section 3(b), is the architect registered and licensed who is _directly and professionally responsible and liable for the construction supervision of the project_. The AiCC's role commences when construction begins and extends through substantial completion. The AiCC ensures that the contractor's work conforms to the approved plans, specifications, and contract documents. Responsibilities include site inspections, documentation of contractor compliance, verification of material quality, review of change orders, defect correction, and progress reporting to the client. The AiCC is _not responsible for the overall design_ but is accountable for faithful execution of the design during construction. Unlike the AoR, a single project may have different architects serving as AoR (during design) and AiCC (during construction), though these roles may also be combined when one architect manages both design and supervision.

### Civil Liability of the Architect-of-Record

Under Civil Code Article 1723, the AoR bears _strict liability for defects in the plans and specifications_. This liability is independent of the contractor's performance and is established purely on proof that a design defect caused structural failure. The AoR cannot escape liability by claiming the contractor deviated from plans, because the liability attaches to the design itself. The AoR must ensure all plans are complete, accurate, constructible, and compliant with applicable building codes, zoning regulations, environmental standards, and any contractual specifications from the client. The AoR's seal on documents represents a professional certification that the design is sound. Errors in load calculations, structural configuration, material selection, or environmental analysis constitute design defects. The AoR's professional liability insurance typically covers Article 1723 claims, and the AoR remains liable for the full 15-year exposure period from building completion.

### Civil Liability and Solidary Liability of the Architect-in-Charge of Construction

The AiCC bears _solidary liability with the contractor_ for defects in construction execution or use of inferior materials, under Civil Code Article 1723. This means the AiCC cannot limit responsibility by claiming the contractor acted independently; both parties are jointly and severally liable for construction defects. The AiCC's negligence in supervision—failure to inspect work, oversight of defective materials, or inability to detect deviations from plans—constitutes breach of the AiCC's duty. The AiCC cannot claim ignorance of job-site conditions; the supervising architect must maintain adequate site presence and documentation to fulfill this role competently. The AiCC's liability is measured against a standard of reasonableness: would a competent architect, exercising ordinary professional diligence, have discovered the defect during construction? If the answer is yes, the AiCC bears liability. The AiCC's professional insurance typically covers these construction supervision claims within specified policy limits.

### Distinction in Liability and Practical Accountability

The critical distinction between AoR and AiCC liability is that the AoR is liable for _design defects regardless of how the contractor performed_, while the AiCC is liable for _construction defects and the architect's failure to supervise adequately_. A project may involve all four liability scenarios: (1) design-only failures (AoR liability); (2) contractor execution failures that the AiCC should have caught (solidary AiCC-contractor liability); (3) both design and construction failures (both AoR and solidary AiCC-contractor liability); and (4) contractor failures that the AiCC could not reasonably have detected (potential contractor-only liability). Architects seeking to minimize liability must carefully define their scope—accepting AoR responsibility only for design quality and limiting AiCC responsibility to periods of actual site supervision. Many architectural practices now carry separate professional liability insurance for design and construction supervision phases, with different policy limits reflecting differing risk profiles.
