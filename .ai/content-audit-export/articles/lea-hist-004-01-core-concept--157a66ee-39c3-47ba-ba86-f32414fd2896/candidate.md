# Islamic Architecture

## LEA Point
Islamic architecture is a broad architectural tradition tied to prayer orientation, courtyard planning, surface geometry, and regional adaptation. For the LEA, identify it by mosque vocabulary, qibla orientation, mihrab, sahn, minaret, iwan, muqarnas, arabesque, calligraphy, tilework, and controlled non-figural pattern in sacred settings.

## Source Anchors
The mosque is the safest organizing type. The prayer hall holds the congregation; the qibla wall orients worship toward Mecca; the mihrab marks that direction; the sahn or courtyard supports gathering, light, air, and often purification; and the minaret marks the mosque in the skyline.

UNESCO Damascus source-maps the Great Mosque of the Umayyads as an 8th-century monument and a masterpiece of Umayyad architecture. Use it for early monumental mosque planning and for the way Islamic buildings can absorb earlier urban and sacred sites.

UNESCO Cordoba source-maps the Great Mosque as an outstanding Islamic religious monument and notes reused Roman/Visigoth columns, double arches, and a long sequence of extensions. Use Cordoba for hypostyle space, columns, and Western Islamic influence.

UNESCO Jerusalem source-maps the Dome of the Rock as a 7th-century monument decorated with geometric and floral motifs. Use it carefully: it is a sacred monument with a central plan, not simply a standard congregational mosque.

Muqarnas is the three-dimensional honeycomb or stalactite-like vaulting and transition system. Arabesque and calligraphy enrich surfaces while keeping sacred decoration oriented toward pattern, text, and abstraction.

## Exam Pattern
Islamic architecture questions usually test vocabulary. Mihrab means prayer-direction niche; minaret means tower or call marker; sahn means courtyard; iwan means vaulted recessed hall or portal; muqarnas means prismatic three-dimensional subdivision.

Another pattern compares Islamic and Gothic space. Islamic mosque planning often emphasizes horizontal congregation, courtyard openness, and surface field; Gothic churches emphasize vertical hierarchy, pointed arches, rib vaults, and upward light.

## Guardrails
Do not confuse qibla with mihrab. Qibla is the direction; mihrab is the niche marking that direction.

Do not call muqarnas a flat pattern. It is spatial and three-dimensional, even when used decoratively.

## Real World Scenario
In an image item, a wall niche centered on the prayer direction is a mihrab. A tall skyline marker is a minaret. A honeycomb transition under a dome, vault, or portal is muqarnas. A large open court with arcades and a fountain is a sahn.
