## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal
Apply anthropometric data and ergonomic principles to space planning, determine minimum clearances for various activities, design for universal accessibility per BP 344, and apply human dimension standards for institutional facilities.

Structural system selection is not just an engineering problem — it shapes your entire architectural floor plan. The system you choose determines column spacing, floor-to-floor height, and architectural expression. For the LEA, you must understand how each system affects spatial planning and how it is typically applied in Philippine practice.

## Core Concept

Several structural systems are common in Philippine architecture, each with distinct planning implications. For exam purposes, focus on how they affect span capability, grid planning, floor-to-floor height, and flexibility of interior layouts.

**1. Reinforced Concrete (RC) Frame**  
- Most prevalent system for low- to mid-rise buildings in the Philippines.  
- Column sizes and spacing are set by the structural engineer; many mid-rise buildings use bay spans in the range of about 5–8 m, depending on loads and beam depth.  
- Floor-to-floor height is commonly around 3.0–3.5 m to accommodate slab depth and MEP services.  
- Offers flexible floor plans because partitions are generally non-load-bearing.  
- Advantages: high stiffness, inherent fire resistance, materials and skills widely available locally.  
- Disadvantages: longer construction time, significant formwork and curing time.  
- Architectural impact: Structural grid can be expressed or concealed; coordination with parking modules and unit layouts is crucial.

**2. Steel Frame**  
- Used for high-rise, long-span, and industrial buildings where speed and span are priorities.  
- Column spacing is often in the 6–9 m range for commercial floors, with longer spans possible for special-use or industrial spaces.  
- Floor-to-floor heights can be efficient (about 3.0 m ±) because steel beams and composite decks can be shallower than RC beams for the same span.  
- Composite steel deck with concrete topping is common in commercial projects.  
- Advantages: rapid erection, high strength-to-weight ratio, wide-span capability.  
- Disadvantages: higher material cost, need for fire protection, careful attention to corrosion and thermal bridging.  
- Architectural impact: Slender columns and long spans improve planning flexibility; exposed steel can be an intentional design feature.

**3. Timber / Engineered Wood Frame**  
- Emerging for specialized projects (e.g., sustainable or resort-type developments) rather than mainstream mid-rise structures.  
- Practical spans for conventional framing are shorter (about 3–5 m); engineered timber (e.g., glulam, CLT) can achieve longer spans with proper engineering.  
- Floor-to-floor heights typically around 2.7–3.0 m in building types where timber is used.  
- Advantages: low embodied carbon, potential for prefabrication and fast assembly, warm architectural character.  
- Disadvantages: availability and cost in the Philippines, higher acoustic and fire-protection design requirements, reliance on specialized detailing.  
- Architectural impact: Strong modular logic; exposed wood structure is often a key part of the architectural expression.

**4. Masonry Bearing Wall**  
- Used mainly for low-rise (1–3 storey) residential and small commercial buildings.  
- Load-bearing CHB or masonry walls carry floor and roof loads; typical room spans are more limited, often in the 3–5 m range.  
- Floor-to-floor heights typically around 2.7–3.0 m.  
- Advantages: simple to understand, widely known among local contractors, suitable for basic layouts.  
- Disadvantages: low flexibility — major walls cannot be removed or shifted without structural impact; retrofits are difficult.  
- Architectural impact: Wall locations heavily control room layout and openings; floor layouts between levels must align structurally.

**5. Composite / Hybrid Systems**  
- Examples: RC core with steel perimeter beams; RC columns with precast floor systems; steel frame with RC shear walls.  
- Used where lateral resistance, speed, and span flexibility must be balanced (e.g., mixed-use podium-tower developments).  
- Floor-to-floor heights often similar to RC or steel-only systems (around 3.0–3.5 m, depending on occupancy and services).  
- Architectural impact: Allows differentiation between podium and tower expression; cores and transfer levels strongly influence planning.

## Architectural Planning Impact

**Column and Grid Coordination**  
For RC, steel, and composite systems, the structural grid need not coincide with partitions, but poor early coordination leads to columns cutting through prime spaces (e.g., mid-bay in offices or bedrooms). Align grids with parking modules, unit widths, and facade rhythm as early as schematic design.

**Floor-to-Floor Height**  
Structural depth plus MEP distribution depth determine realistic floor-to-floor height. Overgenerous heights increase envelope cost; undersized heights cause ceiling congestion and service conflicts. In exams, a 3.0 m floor-to-floor is a common assumption unless otherwise specified.

**Facade Expression**  
- RC: columns and floor bands can be emphasized or concealed; infill walls often define fenestration.  
- Steel: long spans and slender members allow larger window modules and more open corners.  
- Masonry: wall thickness and regular openings produce a heavier, more solid character.  
- Timber: structure is often celebrated; bay spacing dictates the façade module.

**MEP Coordination**  
- RC beams and slabs require careful routing of ducts and pipes to avoid excessive slab penetrations.  
- Steel beams and composite decks can allow more straightforward MEP integration between beam lines.  
- Masonry needs planned chases for plumbing and electrical; late changes are disruptive.  
- Timber and engineered wood require attention to penetrations, fire protection, and acoustics.

## Philippine Practice Context

In the Philippines, RC moment frames with CHB infill dominate low- to mid-rise residential, institutional, and commercial projects because of material availability and contractor familiarity. Steel frames are favored for high-bay industrial buildings, long-span roofs, and selected high-rise projects where schedule and weight savings justify higher upfront costs. Masonry bearing wall systems are common in simple low-rise housing but are rarely used for complex multi-storey layouts due to their rigidity. Timber and engineered wood remain niche but are gaining interest in resorts and sustainability-focused developments.

For architects, early collaboration with the structural engineer is essential: grid spacing, core placement, and span decisions must support the intended space planning, parking efficiency, and façade composition. In LEA questions, you are expected to identify the system that best supports the stated functional, economic, and construction constraints — not just the “strongest” system.

## How It's Tested

1. **System Selection Justification**  
   Given a building type (e.g., 10-storey office vs. 2-storey warehouse), you select the appropriate structural system and justify your choice in terms of span, speed, and architectural flexibility.

2. **Planning Constraint Analysis**  
   You analyze how span limits, grid spacing, and floor-to-floor height affect lease spans, unit layouts, or column-free spaces in lobbies and function halls.

3. **Multi-System Comparison**  
   You compare two candidate systems (e.g., RC vs. steel) on cost tendency, schedule, and architectural expression, then recommend one with clear reasoning aligned to the project brief.
