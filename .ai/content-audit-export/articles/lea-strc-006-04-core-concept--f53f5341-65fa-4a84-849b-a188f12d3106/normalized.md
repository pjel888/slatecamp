## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal
By the end of this article, you will be able to:

- Define prestressing and explain why it is used in concrete
- Distinguish between pre-tensioning (strands tensioned before casting) and post-tensioning (tensioned after curing)
- Explain how prestressing creates compressive stress that offsets tensile service stresses
- Identify common applications: bridge girders, prestressed slabs, and beams

Prestressed concrete applies permanent compression to concrete before or during service loads to reduce or eliminate tensile stresses that would otherwise cause cracking. The two methods are fundamentally different in application timing: pre-tensioning applies compression before concrete is cast, while post-tensioning applies compression after concrete has hardened. Understanding this distinction and the resulting structural behaviors is essential for recognizing when each method is appropriate.

**Prestressing** is the application of deliberately induced internal compression to a concrete member to counteract tensile stresses that would develop from external loads. Instead of relying entirely on reinforcing steel to resist tension (as in ordinary reinforced concrete), prestressed concrete uses high-strength steel tendons—either wires or strands—to apply sustained compression. This reduces or eliminates cracks, improves serviceability, and increases the effective load-carrying capacity of the member.

**Pre-tensioning** is the process of tensioning the steel tendons _before_ concrete is placed. The sequence is:
1. Steel tendons (wires or seven-wire strands, typically _F_y ≈ 1,770–1,960 MPa) are stretched to a specified stress (typically 60–75% of breaking strength) and held in tension on a form or frame (the "casting bed").
2. Concrete is cast around the tensioned tendons.
3. After concrete reaches sufficient strength (typically 3–7 days), the tendons are released from the external holding devices.
4. The tendons, now bonded to the concrete, attempt to retract but are prevented by concrete friction and mechanical anchorage. This creates a compressive force on the concrete.
5. The concrete is permanently compressed from the inside by the tendons.

**Characteristics of Pre-tensioning:**
- Tendons are _bonded_ to concrete from casting onward.
- Transfer of stress from tendon to concrete occurs gradually as the tendon tries to shorten (frictional transfer).
- Pre-tensioning is commonly used in factory-controlled environments (precasting plants) because it requires long bed frames and repeated casting/release cycles.
- Typical products: precast I-beams, double-T slabs, hollow core slabs, and box beams.
- Once released, the member immediately carries its own weight (self-weight compression) and remains compressed throughout service.

**Post-tensioning** is the process of tensioning the steel tendons _after_ concrete has hardened. The sequence is:
1. Concrete is cast with ducts (conduits) left empty for later tendon placement.
2. Concrete hardens and reaches full strength (28 days or later).
3. Steel tendons are placed through the ducts.
4. Tendons are tensioned (pulled) by hydraulic jacks at one or both ends, applying force to the concrete.
5. Tendons are anchored at the ends (typically with wedge anchors), locking in the compression.
6. Ducts are grouted (filled) to bond the tendons to concrete and protect them from corrosion.

**Characteristics of Post-tensioning:**
- Tendons may be unbonded (not grouted into the ducts) during the testing phase, but are typically grouted for final service.
- Stress transfer is _instantaneous_ when jacks release (unlike pre-tensioning, which is gradual).
- Post-tensioning is used in cast-in-place construction because it does not require external holding frames or precasting facilities.
- Typical applications: parking structures, bridges, buildings with long spans, and foundation slabs.
- The member carries its own weight _without_ compression (the concrete is uncompressed when the tendons are placed); compression is applied only after the concrete has developed sufficient strength and cured.
- Compression can be applied at multiple stages (e.g., after deck placement in bridge construction) and can be partially or fully locked in.

**Key Differences:**

| Aspect | Pre-tensioning | Post-tensioning |
|---|---|---|
| **Timing of Tension** | Tendons tensioned BEFORE concrete placed | Tendons tensioned AFTER concrete hardened |
| **Bonding** | Bonded from casting | Typically bonded after tensioning (grouted) |
| **Stress Transfer** | Gradual (friction transfer) | Instantaneous (jacks release) |
| **Common Uses** | Precast/precasting plants | Cast-in-place construction, bridges |
| **Tendon Type** | Wires, strands; continuous | Strands in ducts; can be individual tendons |
| **Anchorage** | Mechanical bond to concrete, friction | Wedge anchors at ends |
| **Cost Sensitivity** | Economical for mass production | Economical for long spans, unique shapes |
| **Self-Weight** | Member compressed from release onward | Self-weight resisted by uncompressed concrete until tensioning |

**Tendon Materials:**
High-strength steel is essential to prestressing because:
- High yield strength (_F_y ≈ 1,770–1,960 MPa for strands vs. 414 MPa for ordinary bars) allows large compression forces with small cross-sectional area.
- Modulus of elasticity _E_ = 200,000 MPa (same as ordinary reinforcing steel).
- Relaxation (loss of stress over time under sustained loading) is a concern; prestress is typically applied at 60–75% of breaking strength to minimize long-term relaxation losses.

**Prestress Losses:**
Both pre-tensioned and post-tensioned members experience stress losses over time due to:
- Elastic deformation (instantaneous loss in post-tensioning; gradual in pre-tensioning)
- Concrete creep and shrinkage (time-dependent, referenced in Article LEA-STRC-003-02)
- Steel relaxation (stress loss under sustained load)
- Friction losses in ducts (post-tensioning only)

The initial prestress must be higher than the final required compression because of these losses. Typical final compression is 60–80% of initial prestress.

## Key Provisions

| Parameter | Pre-tensioning | Post-tensioning |
|---|---|---|
| **Steel Yield Strength** | 1,770–1,960 MPa (typical strand) | 1,770–1,960 MPa (typical strand) |
| **Initial Stress** | 60–75% of breaking strength | 60–75% of breaking strength |
| **Bonding Method** | Mechanical grip + friction | Grouted ducts + wedge anchors |
| **Transfer Length** | Requires development (0.5–2 m typical) | Instantaneous at anchors |
| **Duct Grouting** | Not applicable (fully bonded) | Required for final service |
| **Typical Span** | 15–30 m (precast beams) | 20–60+ m (cast-in-place, bridges) |

**Concrete Cover Requirements for Prestressed Concrete (Simplified):**
Cover is measured from the outer surface to the tendon duct or strand surface and varies by exposure:
- Cast against earth: 75 mm
- Exposed to weather (walls/slabs): 25 mm
- Exposed to weather (beams/columns): 40 mm
- Not exposed (slabs/walls): 20 mm
- Not exposed (beams/columns): 40 mm

These values protect tendons from corrosion, especially important in coastal environments and aggressive exposures.

## Philippine Practice Context

In the Philippines, precast prestressed members (pre-tensioned I-beams, double-T slabs, hollow-core slabs) are widely used in housing projects and bridge construction because of high production efficiency and consistent quality. Post-tensioning is less common but is used in large-span bridges (e.g., across major rivers), long-span parking structures, and high-rise buildings with long cantilevers. Coastal regions, where chloride corrosion is a concern, require high-quality grouting of post-tensioned ducts and adequate concrete cover to protect the high-strength tendons. The PNSC Chapter 7 specifies prestress design requirements and cover requirements specific to Philippine construction and exposure classes.

## How It's Tested

1. **Prestress Transfer Test (ASTM E8 / Philippines Custom):** In a precasting plant, strand samples are tested for ultimate tensile strength and stress-strain properties. Tendons must reach the specified yield strength without failure; typical test values are _F_u_ ≈ 1,860 MPa or higher.

2. **Prestress Loss Measurement (ASTM A416 / Technical Tests):** Strain gauges are placed on a sample member to measure prestress loss over time. Losses due to elastic deformation, creep, and shrinkage are recorded at key ages (1 day, 7 days, 28 days, 90 days, 1 year). Engineers use these measurements to refine prestress loss estimates for design.

3. **Duct Grouting Verification (Post-Tensioning Only):** After grouting ducts, cores are extracted to verify complete grouting (no voids) and proper adhesion to the tendon. Poor grouting allows water infiltration and corrosion.

4. **Residual Prestress Verification:** After all curing and service, or after removal of temporary loads (in bridges), the residual compression is verified by ultrasonic methods or other non-destructive testing to confirm that design prestress levels are maintained.
