Lateral force-resisting systems include shear walls, diaphragms, collectors, and boundary elements. Each component has a specific function in transferring seismic or wind forces from the floor to the foundation. Understanding their definitions and roles is essential for lateral design.

## Core Concept

A **shear wall** is a vertical structural element that resists lateral (horizontal) loads through in-plane shear and bending. Shear walls are typically made of reinforced concrete or masonry and are positioned to transfer wind and seismic forces from diaphragms down to the foundation. They act in pairs or groups to form the "skeleton" of lateral resistance.

A **diaphragm** is a horizontal structural element (floor or roof) that collects lateral loads from the vertical structure above and distributes them to shear walls. Diaphragms act like beams spanning between shear walls, transferring inertial forces generated by floor masses during earthquakes or wind events.

A **collector** (or drag strut) is a beam or truss that collects forces from a diaphragm and channels them to shear walls. Collectors are critical where diaphragm geometry does not naturally align loads with shear walls—they prevent "unraveling" of lateral force transfer.

**Boundary elements** are reinforced regions at the edges of shear walls, typically required in tall or heavily loaded walls to carry compression and confinement reinforcement. They ensure that the wall perimeter and corners are sufficiently reinforced against buckling and crushing.

These elements work as an integrated system: inertial forces act on the diaphragm, the diaphragm transfers forces via collectors to shear walls, and shear walls carry forces to the foundation.

## Key Provisions

| Element | Function | Material Typical | Key Design Consideration |
|---------|----------|------------------|--------------------------|
| **Shear Wall** | Resists lateral loads in-plane | RC, masonry | Aspect ratio, opening configuration, coupling beams if openings present |
| **Diaphragm** | Collects and distributes lateral forces horizontally | RC slab, steel deck + concrete | Flexibility determines load distribution; flexible diaphragms distribute to closest shear wall |
| **Collector** | Transfers diaphragm forces to shear walls | Steel, RC beam/truss | Must not yield; designed for tension and compression |
| **Boundary Element** | Provides confinement at shear wall edges | RC with transverse reinforcement | Required at wall ends in SMRF shear walls; prevents premature crushing |

## Philippine Practice Context

In Seismic Zone 2, shear wall systems are standard in mid-rise residential and office buildings because they provide high stiffness and reliable performance. RC shear walls with boundary elements are required when seismic forces are high. Diaphragm action is ensured through monolithic concrete floor slabs or steel deck with composite action. Collectors are often designed as edge beams running perpendicular to shear walls.

## How It's Tested

**Remember-level:** Define shear wall, diaphragm, and collector from a given building description.

**Understand-level:** Explain why a collector is necessary in buildings where shear walls are not symmetrically positioned.

**Apply-level:** Given a floor plan with offset shear walls and a flexible diaphragm, identify where collectors are required and justify the load path.

<Tip>**Memory aid:** Diaphragm **collects**, collectors **carry**, walls **resist**. Think of them as a team: hands → arms → feet.</Tip>
