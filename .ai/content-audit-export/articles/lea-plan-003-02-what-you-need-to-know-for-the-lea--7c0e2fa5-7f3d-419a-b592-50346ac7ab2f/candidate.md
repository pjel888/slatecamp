## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal
Distinguish the four Philippine housing classifications — socialized (BP 220), economic (BP 220), low-cost, and open-market — by affordability thresholds, minimum standards, target income groups, and applicable regulations.

Philippine housing is classified into four legal categories based on affordability, target market, and development approach. Socialized and economic housing are regulated by BP 220; low-cost housing bridges the gap between economic and open-market; and open-market housing serves middle-income and affluent buyers. The classification system does not govern delivery methods (these are determined by policy choices), but it defines the minimum standards each category must meet. Understanding the boundaries and definitions of each housing type is essential for reading housing policies, analyzing development projects, and answering classification questions on the LEA.

### Four Housing Classifications Under Philippine Law

**Socialized Housing**

Socialized housing serves families earning below ₱15,000 per month (verify current DHSUD thresholds as these are periodically adjusted). It is the foundation of the social housing system and the primary target of balanced housing mandates.

Minimum standards (BP 220):
- New subdivision: 72 sq.m. lot, 22 sq.m. floor area
- In-fill/infill: 28 sq.m. lot, 22 sq.m. floor area

Socialized housing is further subdivided into:

1. **Government-Built Socialized Housing** — Produced by the National Housing Authority (NHA) as primary developer
2. **Developer-Built Socialized Housing** — Produced by private developers as part of balanced housing obligations or as standalone projects for Pag-IBIG financing

The distinction between government-built and developer-built is a delivery method choice, not a legal classification change. Both must meet BP 220 standards.

**Economic Housing**

Economic housing serves middle-income families earning ₱15,000–₱40,000 per month (approximate range; verify current thresholds). It bridges the affordability gap between socialized and low-cost housing.

Minimum standards (BP 220):
- Lot area: 100 square meters
- Floor area: 42 square meters

Economic housing is often called "affordable middle-income housing" and is widely financed through Pag-IBIG and commercial bank programs. It includes townhouses, low-rise condominiums, and small detached homes.

**Low-Cost Housing**

Low-cost housing serves families earning ₱40,000–₱60,000 per month (thresholds vary; verify current DHSUD definitions). It is not formally defined in BP 220 as a statutory category, but it is widely recognized as a market segment between economic and open-market housing.

Typical characteristics (not legally mandated, but industry standard):
- Lot area: 100–200 square meters
- Floor area: 60–100 square meters
- Price ceiling: ₱2–4 million (varies by location and market conditions)

Low-cost housing includes townhouse complexes, low-rise residential projects, and small single-family homes in growth corridors. Financing typically comes from Pag-IBIG, SSS, or commercial banks.

**Open-Market Housing**

Open-market housing serves middle-to-high-income buyers with no price ceiling or affordability mandate. It is regulated by PD 957 (developer registration, 30% open space, buyer protections) but not by BP 220 minimum standards.

Characteristics:
- Lot area: Market-determined; typically 150–500+ sq.m.
- Floor area: Market-determined; typically 100–300+ sq.m.
- Price: Market-determined; ₱4 million and up
- Examples: luxury residential subdivisions, high-end condominiums, exclusive golf course communities

Open-market housing has no affordability requirement, no balanced housing obligation (except insofar as RA 7279 requires developers to produce an equivalent value of socialized housing), and is entirely driven by market demand and supply.

### Housing Delivery Methods (Not Classifications)

Housing classifications describe what is built; delivery methods describe who builds it and how it is financed. These are separate decisions.

**On-Site Balanced Housing**

Private developers of open-market or economic projects build socialized units within the same subdivision or condominium project. Example: A 500-unit residential project includes 75 units (15% of cost) built as socialized housing with finish standards equivalent to the market units but sold at socialized prices.

**Off-Site Balanced Housing**

Private developers build socialized housing in a separate project (different location, different land). Example: A developer building a luxury subdivision in Makati fulfills its balanced housing obligation by building a separate 200-unit socialized project in Quezon City.

**Joint Venture with NHA**

Private developers partner with the National Housing Authority to produce socialized housing. NHA provides land or contributes to development costs; the developer provides expertise and infrastructure. The project is marketed as NHA-developer joint venture.

**Bond Contribution to NHA**

If on-site and off-site are infeasible, developers may contribute a bond or cash to NHA's housing fund. NHA then uses the fund to build socialized housing. This option is the least preferred by government because it delays housing production.

**Community Mortgage Program (CMP) via SHFC**

Community organizations (not individual buyers) secure mortgages from the Social Housing Finance Corporation to purchase land they occupy or collectively identify. SHFC finances the land acquisition; residents gradually pay the mortgage through the community organization. CMP is the most successful in-situ (in-place) upgrading program in the Philippines because it preserves community and avoids relocation.

**NHA Direct Production**

The National Housing Authority directly develops and builds socialized and some economic housing using government budget allocations. NHA targets informal settlers, government employees on low salaries, and disaster victims. Beneficiaries are selected through LGU referrals and NHA's own targeting criteria.

**Pag-IBIG Direct Purchase Program**

Pag-IBIG members use their accumulated fund balance to purchase socialized or economic housing from approved developers. Pag-IBIG finances the transaction; the developer receives payment from Pag-IBIG at closing. This is the largest delivery channel for socialized and economic housing in the Philippines.

### Key Distinctions: What Is Classification vs. What Is Delivery?

| Decision | Is It Classification? | Is It Delivery Method? | Notes |
|----------|----------------------|----------------------|-------|
| Socialized vs. Economic vs. Open-Market | YES | NO | Defined by BP 220 minimum standards and income targeting |
| On-site vs. Off-site balanced housing | NO | YES | Both balanced housing options; both must meet BP 220 standards |
| NHA-built vs. Private developer-built socialized | NO | YES | Both are socialized housing; method of delivery differs |
| Pag-IBIG-financed vs. Bank-financed economic | NO | YES | Both are economic housing; financing source differs |
| Government resettlement vs. Private relocation | NO | YES | Delivery method determined by policy, not classification |

### Delivery Methods by Target Classification

**Socialized Housing Delivery Methods**

1. NHA direct development (government budget, disaster victims, informal settler relocation)
2. Private developer on-site (part of balanced housing mandate)
3. Private developer off-site (part of balanced housing mandate)
4. NHA-private joint venture
5. Community Mortgage Program (SHFC) — most innovative for in-situ upgrading
6. Pag-IBIG direct financing of developer projects

**Economic Housing Delivery Methods**

1. Private developers (market-driven)
2. NHA (limited; not NHA's primary mandate)
3. Pag-IBIG financing of developer or builder projects
4. Commercial bank financing

**Low-Cost Housing Delivery Methods**

1. Private builders and developers (primary)
2. Pag-IBIG, SSS, and bank financing

**Open-Market Housing Delivery Methods**

1. Private developers (sole provider)
2. Commercial bank financing or developer payment plans

### Why This Distinction Matters for the LEA

Questions often ask: "Under what delivery method is socialized housing produced?" Students must not confuse the classification (socialized) with the delivery method (on-site, off-site, NHA, CMP, Pag-IBIG, etc.). The classification is fixed by law; the delivery method is a policy choice.

Example:
- **Question:** "A developer builds 300 units of socialized housing using Pag-IBIG financing. What housing classification is this?" 
- **Answer:** Socialized housing (classification is determined by BP 220 standards, not financing source).
- **Not the Answer:** "Pag-IBIG housing" — this incorrectly treats the financing method as a classification.

Another example:
- **Question:** "Which delivery method for balanced housing does NOT require relocation of the target community?"
- **Answer:** Community Mortgage Program (CMP), because CMP enables communities to purchase the land they already occupy, keeping them in place.
- **Not the Answer:** On-site balanced housing, which does not guarantee that the target community stays in place.

### Summary: Housing Taxonomy in the Philippines

The Philippine housing system layers four classifications (socialized, economic, low-cost, open-market) with multiple delivery methods (government, private, CMP, Pag-IBIG, etc.). Understanding this two-dimensional structure — what is built (classification) versus how it is built and financed (delivery) — is the key to housing policy analysis on the LEA. Students who confuse classification with delivery method consistently misread case studies and policy questions.
