## The Signing-Sealing Prohibition — Direct Supervision, Delegation, and Disciplinary Exposure

## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- State the rule under RA 9266 §33: only a registered architect may sign and seal architectural documents.
- Define 'direct supervision' and what it requires of the architect.
- Identify exceptions: delegation to supervised subordinates.
- Explain disciplinary consequences for unauthorized signing and sealing.

---
### Section 23 Violation and Grounds for Discipline

RA 9266 Section 23 provides that signing or sealing architectural plans not prepared by the architect or not prepared under the architect's direct supervision constitutes grounds for suspension or revocation of the architect's license. This is one of the most serious professional violations in architecture practice because it strikes at the core of professional responsibility and public trust. When an architect signs a document, the public, contractors, and government agencies rely on the architect's certification that the document is accurate, complete, and code-compliant. Violating this requirement undermines this essential trust.

The violation can occur through various scenarios: an architect who signs documents prepared entirely by others and assumes no responsibility for their accuracy; an architect who directs staff to prepare drawings but provides insufficient supervision during development and signs the completed product without thorough review; an architect who signs documents months after their preparation without active involvement in the design process; or an architect who signs designs prepared by a client's in-house designers or contractors without independent verification. Each scenario represents a breach of the direct supervision requirement.

### What Constitutes Direct Supervision

Direct supervision requires active, continuous involvement of the architect in the design process from initial concept through completion of construction documents. The architect must be present during design meetings where spatial configurations, aesthetic decisions, code compliance strategies, and technical solutions are developed. The architect must review drawings as they develop, provide feedback and direction to staff, and ensure design decisions are implemented correctly in the drawings. The architect cannot merely assign design work to others and sign the final product.

Direct supervision also requires that the architect personally verify the accuracy and completeness of the documents before signing and sealing. This verification includes checking that drawings are dimensioned consistently, that details are clearly drawn and understandable, that code compliance is demonstrated, and that drawings coordinate with other design disciplines. An architect who signs drawings without having reviewed them against applicable building codes or without checking for obvious errors or omissions has not exercised the care required of a responsible professional and violates the direct supervision requirement.

### Prohibited Delegation Scenarios

Several common scenarios constitute violations of the signing-sealing prohibition. An architect who hires independent contractors to prepare design drawings without maintaining continuous supervision and then signs the documents violates the requirement. An architect who receives drawings from a client, consultant, or contractor, makes minimal edits, and signs them without active involvement in their development violates the requirement. An architect who accepts design drawings prepared in another jurisdiction, translates specifications to local codes, and signs them without re-designing or thoroughly reviewing the work violates the requirement.

An architect who signs documents prepared by unlicensed drafters or technicians without supervision also violates the requirement. While architects may employ drafters to assist in producing drawings, the architect remains responsible for direct supervision of the drafting process. Junior architects, interns, and entry-level staff may prepare drawings under the direct supervision of a licensed architect, but only if the licensed architect is actively involved in the design decisions and drawing development. The licensed architect cannot defer design responsibility to unlicensed staff.

### Professional Discipline and Consequences

Violation of RA 9266 Section 23 is a ground for suspension or revocation of the architect's Certificate of Registration and Professional Identification Card. Suspension means the architect is prohibited from practicing architecture for a specified period, typically ranging from three months to three years depending on the severity of the violation and whether it is a first offense. Revocation means permanent loss of the license; the architect is barred from practicing architecture unless the license is later reinstated through petition to the Board.

The Board of Architecture investigates complaints of signing-sealing violations through administrative proceedings. The Board may subpoena evidence and witnesses, conduct hearings where the architect is afforded the opportunity to defend against the charges, and may request an inspection of the plans at issue and testimony from the individuals who actually prepared the documents. The Board's decision may be appelead to the Professional Regulation Commission and then to the Court of Appeals. The existence of professional liability insurance does not protect against professional discipline; insurance covers civil liability for damages, but professional discipline is an administrative consequence separate from civil liability.

### Liability Exposure Beyond Professional Discipline

Beyond professional discipline, an architect who signs documents not properly prepared under direct supervision faces significant civil and criminal liability. If the documents contain errors or omissions that result in construction defects, cost overruns, safety hazards, or injuries, the architect is liable for damages to the owner, contractors, and third parties. The architect cannot claim the signature was merely clerical; the signature certifies personal professional responsibility.

Criminal liability may arise if the architect knowingly signs false or misleading documents with intent to defraud the owner or public. For example, if an architect signs documents that do not comply with applicable building codes but deliberately conceals this non-compliance, criminal prosecution for fraud may result. If the non-compliant design results in injury or death, criminal negligence charges may be pursued. While criminal prosecution for document violations is rare, it remains a potential consequence of egregious conduct.

### Distinguishing Direct Supervision from Inadequate Involvement

The distinction between adequate direct supervision and inadequate involvement is often determined by examining the depth and timing of the architect's involvement. An architect who participates in conceptual design meetings, develops the design concept through schematic and design development phases, reviews and approves drawing sets at each stage, and provides specific design direction to drafting staff demonstrates direct supervision. An architect who receives substantially complete drawings from consultants or staff, makes minor comments, and signs them days or weeks later without thorough review likely has not maintained direct supervision.

The architect's documentation of involvement also supports claims of direct supervision. Meeting notes, emails directing design decisions, marked-up drawing sets showing the architect's comments, and review schedules demonstrating ongoing involvement establish that the architect maintained active involvement. By contrast, architects who cannot produce documentation of their involvement during drawing development face challenges defending against claims that they signed without adequate supervision.

### Practical Risk Management Strategies

Architects should implement firm policies requiring thorough document review before signing. This review should include: verification that all sheets are included and correctly titled; checking for consistency in dimensions, symbols, and note references; reviewing details to ensure constructability and code compliance; and comparing the drawings against applicable building codes. Many firms use a formal drawing review checklist that must be completed and signed by the reviewing architect before any document is sealed.

Architects should also maintain clear documentation of their design involvement. This includes keeping copies of emails or memoranda directing design work, retaining marked-up drawing sets or comments provided during design development, and documenting design meetings with dated notes of decisions made and direction provided. This documentation protects the architect if later questioned about whether direct supervision was maintained. Additionally, architects should clearly communicate to clients and staff that the architect will not sign documents without having personally reviewed them, to establish expectations and prevent staff from presenting unsigned documents as final products requiring only signature.

### Coordination with Other Professionals

The direct supervision requirement applies distinctly to each professional's scope of work. An architect is responsible for direct supervision of architectural drawings; a structural engineer is responsible for direct supervision of structural drawings; and mechanical, electrical, and sanitary professionals are responsible for their respective drawings. However, coordination between professionals requires that the architect, as the lead designer, verify that specialists' work coordinates with the architectural design and that conflicts are resolved before documents are finalized.

If a specialist presents drawings that conflict with the architect's design, or that the architect believes are inadequate, the architect should refuse to proceed until the conflict is resolved. The architect should not sign architectural documents knowing that coordination problems exist with structural, mechanical, or other drawings. Professional responsibility requires that all members of the design team work collaboratively to produce an integrated, conflict-free design before documents are signed and sealed.
