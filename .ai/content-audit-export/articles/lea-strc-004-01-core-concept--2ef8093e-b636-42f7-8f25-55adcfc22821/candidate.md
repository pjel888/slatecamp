## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal

- Distinguish between magnitude (energy released) and intensity (perceived damage)
- Explain the Richter scale limitation above M 6.5 and the role of moment magnitude

Earthquakes are generated by fault ruptures at depth. The LEA tests three vocabulary items: where they originate (hypocenter, epicenter, focus), how we measure them (magnitude scales), and how we perceive them (intensity scales).

## Core Concept

An _earthquake_ occurs when stress accumulated along a fault reaches the breaking point, causing sudden displacement. The _hypocenter_ (or _focus_) is the exact point where rupture initiates—typically kilometers below the surface. The _epicenter_ is the geographic location on the surface directly above the hypocenter. All three terms appear in LEA questions; they are not interchangeable.

_Magnitude_ measures the _energy released_ by an earthquake using instrumental data. The Richter scale (local magnitude ML) measures amplitude of seismic waves recorded on a Wood-Anderson seismograph; it becomes unreliable above magnitude 6.5 because the amplitude scale saturates. The moment magnitude (Mw) uses the seismic moment (M0 = μ × A × d, where μ is rigidity, A is fault area, d is average displacement) and is the standard used by seismologists for large events. A magnitude 7.0 event releases approximately 32 times more energy than a magnitude 6.0 event (roughly 2^5).

_Intensity_ measures _perceived effects and damage_ at specific locations. It is subjective and varies by distance from the epicenter, soil conditions, and building quality. The Modified Mercalli Intensity Scale (MMI) ranges from I (not felt) to XII (total destruction); it is not a number that can be calculated—it is assigned by surveyors post-event based on eyewitness reports and structural damage observation.

The Philippines lies on the Pacific Ring of Fire. Magnitude 7+ events occur roughly every 10–15 years in Philippine waters. Ground motion maps (hazard curves) define the spectral acceleration (Ss and S1) that engineering codes use for design.

## Key Provisions

| Term | Definition | LEA Context |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| Hypocenter (Focus) | Point where rupture begins, at depth | Defines seismic wave origin; not the design value |
| Epicenter | Surface location above hypocenter | Reference for distance/MMI assignment; not design input |
| Magnitude | Energy released (Richter or moment) | Describes earthquake size; not directly input to design formula |
| Intensity (MMI) | Perceived effects and damage (I–XII scale) | Qualitative; used to validate hazard maps post-event |
| Seismic Moment (M0) | μ × A × d in units of dyne-cm | Basis for moment magnitude Mw = (2/3) × [log10(M0) − 10.7] |

Magnitude-Intensity distinction: A single magnitude 7.0 earthquake produces _many_ intensity values across the affected region (e.g., MMI VIII near the epicenter, MMI V far away). Do not confuse them.

## Philippine Practice Context

The Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology (PHIVOLCS) operates the national seismic network. Major faults include the Philippine Fault (central spine, 25 cm/year slip rate) and the West Valley Fault (threatens Metro Manila). Historical events: 1645 (Makati magnitude 8.1), 1863 (Mindanao magnitude 7.9), 1990 (Luzon magnitude 7.8), 2013 (Bohol magnitude 7.2). The 1990 Luzon earthquake caused significant damage in Metro Manila and neighboring provinces despite being 100+ km away; this illustrates how distance and soil conditions affect damage patterns.

## How It's Tested

**Pattern 1: Definition matching.** "The point where a fault rupture initiates is called the..." → Hypocenter or focus (not epicenter).

**Pattern 2: Scale distinction.** "Which statement correctly distinguishes magnitude and intensity?" → Magnitude is energy (one value per earthquake); intensity is damage (varies by location). A single earthquake has one magnitude but many intensity values.

**Pattern 3: Energy scaling.** "How many times more energy does a magnitude 8.0 earthquake release compared to magnitude 7.0?" → Answer: 32× (or 2^5). For each unit increase in magnitude, energy increases by a factor of roughly 32.

<Tip>Hypocenter and epicenter are like a lightbulb (hypocenter) and the shadow it casts on the ceiling (epicenter). The bulb releases the energy; the shadow shows where to look up.</Tip>
