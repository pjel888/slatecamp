## Core Concept
**Art Nouveau** (roughly 1890–1910) and **Art Deco** (roughly 1920–1940) are often conflated because both emerged in the early 20th century, but they are fundamentally opposed in their aesthetics and philosophical roots.

Art Nouveau was born from a desire to create a unified, modern style free from historical pastiche. It drew inspiration from organic forms—plants, flowers, flowing water, the human body—and applied these curves to every surface: buildings, furniture, metalwork, graphic design. The movement rejected right angles and straight lines. Instead, it celebrated the "**whiplash line**," a violent S-curve that gives Art Nouveau its kinetic energy. **Victor Horta** (Tassel House, Brussels, 1892–1893) pioneered the style: his interiors are unified wholes where iron railings, stained glass, and wallpaper all speak the same curvilinear language. **Hector Guimard** (Paris Métro entrances, 1900–1913) brought Art Nouveau to public infrastructure, making everyday structures into organic sculptures. **Antoni Gaudí** (Sagrada Família, Barcelona, construction begun 1882, Gaudí design director from 1883) pushed organic design further, embedding geometric and natural forms into monumental religious architecture. Art Nouveau was a total design system; it aimed to reform all aspects of the built and designed environment.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0002 component="Tip" status="preserved"]

**Art Deco** emerged in the 1920s as a reaction against Art Nouveau's excess. If Art Nouveau was nature-inspired and chaotic, Art Deco was geometry-inspired and controlled. It embraced bold geometric forms—triangles, zigzags, chevrons, sunburst patterns—layered with luxurious materials (chrome, glass, marble, exotic woods). Art Deco was the style of Jazz Age optimism, technological progress, and streamlined modernity. The **Chrysler Building** (completed **May 27, 1930**, **William Van Lean**, New York) is the quintessential Art Deco skyscraper: it features setback massing (for light and air), a gleaming stainless-steel crown, and geometric ornamentation that celebrates speed and machine-age technology. Art Deco was popular for commercial, residential, and institutional buildings; it conveyed wealth, progress, and cosmopolitan sophistication.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Note" status="preserved"]

The critical distinction: Art Nouveau says "buildings are organisms"; Art Deco says "buildings are machines." Both rejected 19th-century historicism, but they pointed in opposite directions.

## Key Terms

**Art Nouveau**: A design movement (c. 1890–1910) emphasizing organic, plant-inspired curves; rejection of straight lines; unified total design across all disciplines; exemplified by Horta, Guimard, and Gaudí.

**Whiplash Line**: A signature Art Nouveau motif—a violent S-curve that creates dynamic, organic movement in design; seen in railings, wallpaper, and architectural ornament.

**Art Deco**: A design movement (c. 1920–1940) emphasizing geometric forms, bold angular patterns, luxury materials, and machine-age aesthetics; celebrated speed, progress, and modern technology.

**Setback Massing**: An architectural form in which upper stories step back from the lower facade; used in Art Deco skyscrapers to comply with zoning laws, provide light, and create visual drama.

**Organic Forms**: Design elements derived from nature—plants, flowers, flowing water, animal shapes; central to Art Nouveau philosophy.

**Geometric Forms**: Angular, mathematical design elements—triangles, chevrons, zigzags, sunbursts; central to Art Deco aesthetic.

**Decorative Arts**: All aspects of design applied to objects and spaces—furniture, metalwork, textiles, graphic design; both Art Nouveau and Art Deco were total design systems encompassing decorative arts.

**Streamline/Streamlining**: An Art Deco emphasis on smooth, flowing lines that suggest speed and machine efficiency; applied to static buildings to convey modernity.
