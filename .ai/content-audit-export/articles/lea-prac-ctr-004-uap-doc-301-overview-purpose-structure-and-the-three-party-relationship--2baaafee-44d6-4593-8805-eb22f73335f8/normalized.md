# UAP Document 301 Overview: Purpose, Structure, and the Three-Party Relationship

UAP Document 301 is a General Conditions document for the construction contract. It defines the Owner, Architect, Engineer, Project Representative, Contractor, Sub-Contractor, Surety, bond types, bid-related documents, Drawings, Specifications, and other contract documents. Treat it as a construction-contract administration source, not as the full Owner-Architect design-services agreement.

## What UAP Document 301 Covers

UAP Document 301 defines Contract Documents as the Agreement, General Conditions, Special Provisions, Specifications, and Drawings, including additions, deletions, and modifications incorporated before execution. It defines the Owner as the person or entity ordering the project, the Architect as the professional commissioned by the Owner, and the Contractor as the person or firm whose proposal has been accepted and to whom the Contract was awarded.

The document also defines proposal, proposal bond, performance bond, payment bond, guarantee bond, invitation to bid, bid bulletin, instructions to bidders, drawings, specifications, supplementary specifications, and schedule of materials and finishes. For LEA purposes, these definitions often eliminate distractor answers that confuse design services, bidding, and construction administration.

## Contract Document Interpretation

Contract documents are complementary, and all work must conform to them. If drawings and specifications vary, the specifications control; if detailed specification requirements conflict with the general conditions or modifications, the detailed specification requirements control. Discrepancies, site-condition conflicts, errors, or omissions must be reported immediately to the Architect or Engineer, who corrects them after discovery. Work involving unreported discrepancies is at the Contractor's risk.

For drawings and specifications, figures are followed in preference to scaled measurements. Work shown on drawings but not mentioned in specifications, or mentioned in specifications but not shown on drawings, is treated as included when reasonably implied.

## Architect Status During Construction

During construction, UAP Document 301 places the Architect as the Owner's representative. The Architect may act for the Owner only to the extent expressly provided in the Contract Documents or otherwise in writing and shown to the Contractor. The Architect may stop the work when reasonably necessary to ensure proper execution of the Contract, but failure to order stoppage does not relieve the Contractor from complying with the Contract Documents or protecting persons and property.

The Architect decides questions about quality and acceptability of materials and work, performance and progress, interpretation of drawings and specifications, and acceptable fulfillment of the Contract. As interpreter and judge of performance, the Architect must not side with either Owner or Contractor; the Architect uses contract powers to enforce faithful performance by both.

## CIAP Document 102 Cross-Check

CIAP Document 102 is another private construction-contract source. It defines Bid Documents, Bid Bulletin, Instruction to Bidders, Contract Documents, Drawings, Specifications, Performance Bond, Payment Bond, and Guarantee Bond. It also gives interpretation rules: detailed drawings prevail over general drawings; figures written on drawings prevail over the drawings themselves; calculated dimensions prevail over scaled dimensions; and if those rules do not resolve the conflict, the document priority begins with the Agreement and proceeds through instructions, addenda, drawings, specifications, special conditions, general conditions, and other contract documents.

## Real World Scenario

A contractor notices that the reflected ceiling drawing and specification call for different materials. Under UAP Document 301, the safe answer is to report the discrepancy to the Architect or Engineer for correction before proceeding. Under CIAP 102, discrepancy handling and document priority rules are also contract-administration issues, not owner-architect fee rules.
