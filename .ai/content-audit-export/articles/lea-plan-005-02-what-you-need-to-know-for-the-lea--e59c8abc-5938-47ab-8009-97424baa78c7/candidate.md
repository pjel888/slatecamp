## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal
Identify Philippine native species and their ecological roles, distinguish native from exotic/invasive species, apply successional planting and ecological restoration principles, and evaluate biodiversity conservation in landscape design.

Philippine native species form complex ecological systems shaped by tropical biodiversity, endemic endemism, and long human-landscape interaction. Understanding native species identification, ecological systems, and biodiversity conservation is essential for landscape architects, environmental planners, and developers working in the Philippines. 
### Philippine Biodiversity and Native Species Framework

The Philippines is one of the world's 17 mega-diverse countries, with extraordinary species richness and high endemism (species found nowhere else on Earth). Philippine ecosystems include tropical rainforests, mangrove wetlands, coastal seagrass beds, coral reefs, and montane forests. Native species are plants and animals that naturally occur in the Philippines, having evolved here over millennia through ecological processes.

**Native species definition:** A native species is one that naturally occurs within a particular region and has not been introduced by humans. Native species are adapted to local climate, soil, and water conditions through natural selection. They support complex food webs, provide ecosystem services (water filtration, soil stability, pollination), and maintain cultural heritage.

**Endemic species definition:** An endemic species occurs naturally in one geographic area and nowhere else in the world. The Philippines has exceptional endemic diversity: 52% of Philippine flowering plants are endemic; numerous birds, reptiles, and mammals are found only in the Philippines. Endemic species are globally irreplaceable and require protection within their native range.

### Narra as National Tree and Ecological Reference

The **Narra** (_Pterocarpus indicus_) is the national tree of the Philippines, selected for its ecological, economic, and cultural significance. Narra is a large, deciduous hardwood tree native to Southeast Asia and the Pacific region, naturalized in the Philippines.

**Ecological characteristics of Narra:**
- Legume family tree: Narra belongs to Fabaceae but does not fix atmospheric nitrogen through root nodules
- Provides shade for understory plants and animals
- Produces winged seeds dispersed by wind
- Supports diverse fauna through flowers, nectar, and seed resources
- Deep rooting stabilizes soil on slopes

**Uses in Philippine ecological restoration:** Narra is commonly planted in reforestation projects because its nitrogen-fixing capacity improves degraded soils, allowing other species to establish. Mixed with native understory species, Narra creates diverse forest conditions that support wildlife recovery.

### Native Species Identification

Identifying native species requires understanding plant morphology (structure), ecology (habitat preferences), and distribution patterns (where they occur naturally).

**Key identification characteristics:**

**Leaves:** Size, shape, margin (smooth, serrated, lobed), arrangement (opposite, alternate, whorled), texture, and color are primary identification features. Compound leaves (leaflets arranged on a stem) differ from simple leaves (single blade).

**Flowers:** Color, shape, arrangement (solitary, clustered), number of petals, and flowering period are distinctive. Many Philippine trees bloom seasonally—identifying blooming time helps pinpoint species.

**Bark:** Texture, color, and distinctive patterns (smooth, furrowed, peeling) aid identification. Some species have distinctive aromatic bark.

**Fruits and seeds:** Size, shape, color, and seed dispersal method are important. Narra's winged seeds are easily recognized.

**Growth habit:** Whether the plant is a tree (woody stem supporting a crown), shrub (multiple woody stems), herb (soft, non-woody stems), vine, or groundcover affects identification.

**Habitat preference:** Native species occur in specific habitats. Mangrove species occur in tidal zones; montane species occur only in cool, high-elevation forests. Understanding habitat narrows identification possibilities.

### Ecological Systems and Successional Processes

Ecosystems are communities of plants, animals, and microorganisms interacting within their physical environment (soil, water, climate). Philippine ecosystems include:

**Tropical rainforests:** Dense, layered forests with high biodiversity, continuous rainfall, and closed canopy structure. Understory is relatively dark and humid.

**Secondary forests:** Regenerating forests on abandoned agricultural land. Secondary forests establish through ecological succession (the process of gradual species change over time).

**Mangrove ecosystems:** Specialized forests in tidal zones with salt-tolerant tree species, aerial root systems, and support for juvenile fish and crustaceans. Mangroves are nurseries for marine fisheries.

**Coastal dunes and seagrass beds:** Transition zones between sea and land with specialized dune vegetation and underwater seagrass meadows supporting marine biodiversity.

**Montane forests:** Cool, misty forests in upland areas with endemic species found only above certain elevation thresholds.

**Ecological succession** is the predictable process of species change over time in response to environmental conditions. In primary succession, pioneer species colonize bare rock or soil, gradually building soil and creating conditions for later-arriving species. In secondary succession, abandoned agricultural land or disturbed forest rapidly recolonizes with pioneer species (fast-growing, light-demanding trees), then over decades transitions to shade-tolerant species that form mature forest.

2. **Early successional stage:** Mix of pioneers and slower-growing species. Soil improves; shade increases.

3. **Mid-successional stage:** Shade-tolerant species establish; pioneers decline as canopy closes. Understory becomes richer.

4. **Climax forest:** Shade-tolerant, mature species dominate. Species composition stabilizes. Narra and other large hardwoods characterize climax forests.

### Successional Planting and Ecological Restoration

Successional planting intentionally recreates ecological succession to restore degraded land, establish forest, and recover biodiversity. Rather than planting only the "final" forest species, ecological restoration plants a sequence of species that naturally follow each other, faster and more cost-effectively than waiting for natural succession.

**Successional planting strategy:**

**Phase 1 — Pioneer establishment:** Plant fast-growing, nitrogen-fixing pioneer species (_Albizia_, _Acacia_, _Macaranga_) at wide spacing. These species rapidly create shade, improve soil through nitrogen fixation and leaf litter, and establish a canopy structure.

**Phase 2 — Understory introduction:** Introduce shade-tolerant understory species (small trees, shrubs, groundcovers) beneath the pioneer canopy. These species could not survive full sun but thrive under pioneer shade.

**Phase 3 — Climax forest transition:** As pioneers age and decline (30–50 years), mature hardwoods (_Narra_, _Dipterocarp_ spp.) established in Phase 2 become dominant. Mature forest structure develops with diverse canopy, understory, and groundlayer.

**Benefits of successional planting:**
- Faster than passive waiting for natural succession
- Lower cost than planting all mature species at once
- Builds soil through pioneer nitrogen fixation
- Establishes diverse forest structure earlier
- Supports wildlife throughout successional stages

### Native vs. Exotic Species

**Native species** occur naturally in the Philippines and support Philippine ecosystems and wildlife. Native species have co-evolved with Philippine insects, birds, soil microbes, and other organisms. Many native plants have cultural uses—traditional medicines, foods, materials.

**Exotic (or introduced) species** are non-native plants brought to the Philippines by humans. Exotic species are not inherently destructive—many valuable agricultural crops (rice, cacao, mango) are exotic. However, some exotic species become **invasive**—they spread rapidly, outcompete natives, and degrade ecosystems.

**Examples of problematic invasive species in the Philippines:**
- _Chromolaena odorata_ (asaeng gubat, devil weed) — invasive shrub that forms monocultures and prevents native regeneration in disturbed areas
- _Prosopis juliflora_ (algaroba) — thorny tree that spreads rapidly in grasslands and degrades rangelands
- _Salvinia molesta_ (water fern) — floating aquatic plant that clogs waterways
- Calamansi and other naturalized fruit trees — established in Philippine culture as cultivated crops, not classified as invasive

**Preference for natives in ecological contexts:** In ecological restoration, habitat creation, and sustainable landscaping, native species are preferred because they:
- Support native wildlife dependent on them
- Require no fertilizers, pesticides, or supplemental water once established
- Build soils through native microbial partnerships
- Maintain genetic diversity
- Reflect Philippine cultural identity

**Appropriate use of exotics:** Exotic species are appropriate in cultural, agricultural, and economic contexts where their benefits are intentional (fruit orchards, spice gardens, farm systems). However, in ecological restoration and natural area management, native species take priority.

### Biodiversity Conservation and Landscape Implications

Biodiversity conservation protects native species and ecosystems. The Philippines' high endemism means species lost here are lost globally. Conservation strategies include:

**Protected areas:** National parks and protected landscapes (under **RA 7586, NIPAS Act**) preserve habitat for endemic species. Developments near protected areas must consider impact on species dependent on those ecosystems.

**Habitat corridors:** Wildlife need continuous habitat to move, feed, and breed. Landscape designs that fragment habitat with walls, roads, and impermeable barriers harm wildlife. Designing connected greenway corridors supports species movement.

**Community-based conservation:** Indigenous and local communities manage much of the Philippines' forest and coastal resources. Integrating traditional ecological knowledge with scientific conservation improves outcomes.

**Native planting programs:** Urban planting of native species in parks, gardens, and streetscapes expands habitat in developed areas, supporting birds, insects, and small animals in cities.
