## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal
Distinguish urban regeneration (large-scale redevelopment) from placemaking (incremental community activation) and tactical urbanism (low-cost temporary interventions), apply each approach to Philippine urban scenarios, and evaluate gentrification risks.

Urban regeneration and placemaking transform neglected, underutilized, or blighted urban areas into vibrant, economically productive, and culturally significant places. While urban regeneration encompasses large-scale redevelopment and infrastructure investment, placemaking emphasizes incremental, community-driven activation of public spaces and places. Tactical urbanism—low-cost, temporary interventions—serves both strategies as a rapid prototyping and community engagement tool.

## Core Concept

Urban regeneration refers to planned, systematic redevelopment of degraded urban areas to restore economic and social vitality. This may involve infrastructure investment, building rehabilitation, land assembly for new development, or institutional repositioning (e.g., converting a declining industrial area into a cultural or innovation district). Regeneration is not neutral—it always involves questions of who benefits, who is displaced, and whether existing communities are included or priced out.

Placemaking is the process of creating meaningful public spaces and experiences that strengthen community identity and social connection. Placemaking focuses on the human-scale experience: public gathering spaces, pedestrian activation, cultural events, street life. Unlike large-scale redevelopment, placemaking often works with existing conditions and emphasizes community voice.

**Tactical urbanism** encompasses low-cost, temporary interventions that test ideas or activate spaces: temporary parks (parklets), street murals, pop-up retail, community gardens, guerrilla gardening, temporary street closures for events. Tactical interventions serve two functions:

1. **Rapid prototyping** — test design ideas before major capital investment (e.g., a temporary pedestrian plaza reveals whether the location would support permanent plaza development)
2. **Community engagement** — low-risk interventions build trust and demonstrate how spaces can be improved

Adaptive reuse—converting buildings from one use to another (warehouse to lofts, church to restaurant, office to residential)—is both a regeneration strategy and a sustainability practice, preserving existing structures rather than demolishing.

## Philippine Context

Philippine cities have significant areas suitable for regeneration: aging colonial districts (Intramuros, Vigan), declining river corridors (Pasig River, Marikina River), underutilized waterfronts, and deteriorating inner-city neighborhoods. Intramuros regeneration efforts, the Port Area redevelopment in Manila, and river corridor rehabilitation projects demonstrate both the potential and the challenges. Gentrification—displacement of long-term residents and small businesses as property values rise—is a real risk in successful regeneration, particularly in informal settlement areas. Philippine planners must balance regeneration's economic benefits against community displacement through anti-displacement policies and inclusive placemaking.

## How It's Tested

1. **Tactic urbanism identification and application** — "What is tactical urbanism, and provide an example." → Answer: Low-cost, temporary interventions that test design ideas or activate spaces; examples include parklets, street murals, temporary street closures. Tests concept definition and practical understanding.

2. **Adaptive reuse concept** — "A 50-year-old warehouse in a declining district is converted to artist lofts. Is this adaptive reuse, and what planning benefit does it provide?" → Answer: Yes; it preserves the existing building (sustainable), generates economic activity and cultural vitality, and often triggers broader district regeneration. Tests understanding of both adaptive reuse definition and urban regeneration process.

3. **Displacement and community impact** — "A waterfront regeneration project transforms a degraded area into a high-value mixed-use district. What planning risk must be mitigated?" → Answer: Gentrification and displacement of original residents; mitigation requires anti-displacement policies (community land trusts, affordable housing preservation, local hiring). Tests understanding of the social risks of regeneration.

**Exam Tip:** Three key distinctions: (1) Regeneration = large-scale, top-down, capital-intensive. (2) Placemaking = community-driven, incremental, public space-focused. (3) Tactical urbanism = low-cost, temporary, rapid prototyping (pop-up bike lanes, parklets). Know which approach fits which scenario.
