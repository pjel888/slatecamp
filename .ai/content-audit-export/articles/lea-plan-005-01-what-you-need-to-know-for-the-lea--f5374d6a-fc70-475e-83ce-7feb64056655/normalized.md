## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal
Apply landscape design principles — landform, plant composition, water features, hardscape materials — to create functional outdoor spaces, design planting plans for Philippine tropical conditions, and integrate landscape with site drainage and circulation.

Landscape design transforms outdoor spaces through the strategic integration of natural (softscape) and built (hardscape) elements. Mastering landscape principles requires understanding how landforms, plants, water, and human-made features work together to create functional, beautiful, and resilient environments. 
### Landscape Design Fundamentals

Landscape design is the profession and practice of designing outdoor spaces—parks, gardens, streetscapes, campuses, and larger land areas. Unlike architecture, which focuses on buildings, landscape design shapes the land itself and manages the relationship between the built and natural environments. In the Philippines, landscape design responds to tropical climate, diverse ecosystems, and the need for water-efficient design.

**Key distinction:** Landscape design differs from landscape architecture. Landscape design is the conceptual and aesthetic process; landscape architecture is the licensed profession regulated under the scope of practice for architects.

### Hardscape and Softscape Components

Landscape spaces are composed of two fundamental categories:

**Hardscape** consists of permanent, built structures and surfaces:
- Paving materials (concrete, asphalt, pavers, permeable surfaces)
- Walls and fences
- Water features (fountains, ponds, channels)
- Lighting
- Seating and furniture
- Structures (pergolas, pavilions, bridges)

Hardscape provides durability, defines spatial boundaries, and supports program activities.

**Softscape** consists of living and natural elements:
- Turf and groundcovers
- Trees and shrubs
- Herbaceous plants (flowers, perennials)
- Soil and mulch
- Water bodies and wetlands

Softscape provides ecological function, seasonal change, shade, and habitat.

A successful landscape balances hardscape and softscape. Too much hardscape creates hostile, heat-absorbing environments; too much softscape without structure lacks definition and clarity.

### Site Grading and Drainage Fundamentals

Site grading is the process of reshaping the land to establish proper drainage, create level areas for use, and respond to existing topography. Proper grading is essential for site health and safety.

**Key grading principles:**

1. **Surface drainage:** Water must drain away from buildings and activity areas. Grade minimum 2% slope away from structures to prevent water damage.

2. **Drainage swleas and bioretention:** Depressed, vegetated areas collect and filter stormwater runoff. These reduce flooding, improve water infiltration, and recharge groundwater.

3. **Terracing and retaining walls:** On sloped sites, terracing creates level platforms for buildings or plazas. Retaining walls hold soil on slopes.

### Planting Design Principles

Planting design selects plant species and arranges them to achieve functional and aesthetic goals.

**Key planting design concepts:**

**Massing and composition** — Groups of plants create visual rhythm and enclosure. Single plants appear weak; massed plantings of the same species (minimum three of a kind) create visual impact. Odd numbers (3, 5, 7) are more visually dynamic than even groups.

**Layering** — Plant communities have horizontal layers: canopy trees (tallest), understory trees, shrubs, groundcovers, and herbaceous perennials. Layering mimics natural forest structure, increases habitat diversity, and creates visual depth.

**Seasonal interest** — Thoughtful plant selection ensures visual interest year-round through flowering times, foliage color changes, fruit, and winter structure.

**Texture and form** — Plant foliage texture (fine, medium, bold) and branching form (columnar, spreading, weeping) vary spatial experience and visual contrast.

**Maintenance intensity** — Plant selection must match the site's maintenance capacity. Heavily manicured planting requires skilled pruning; naturalistic planting tolerates more variation.

### Xeriscaping and Water-Efficient Landscape Design

Xeriscaping is landscape design that minimizes water use through plant selection and efficient irrigation management. The prefix "xero-" means dry. Xeriscaping is not about creating dry, barren landscapes—it is about creating beautiful landscapes that use water efficiently by choosing appropriate plants and design strategies.

**Core xeriscaping principles:**

1. **Native and adapted species:** Drought-tolerant native plants survive with natural rainfall and need no supplemental watering once established. Native species are ecologically beneficial, requiring no fertilizers or pesticides.

2. **Appropriate plant grouping (hydrozoning):** Group plants by water needs. High-water plants (near permanent irrigation) are clustered separately from drought-tolerant plants that need minimal watering. This avoids over-watering drought-tolerant species.

3. **Soil improvement:** High-quality soil with organic matter retains more water. Mulch (wood chips, bark) over soil reduces evaporation by 50% or more.

4. **Efficient irrigation:** Drip irrigation delivers water directly to plant roots, eliminating waste from sprinkler overspray. Drip systems reduce water use by 30–50% compared to spray irrigation.

5. **Reduced turf areas:** Turf grass is water-intensive. Replacing some turf with native groundcovers, hardscape, or meadow reduces irrigation demand.

6. **Permeable surfaces:** Permeable pavement (permeable pavers, porous asphalt) allows water to infiltrate into soil rather than running off, reducing flooding and recharging groundwater.

**Philippine application:** In the Philippines, xeriscaping using native, drought-tolerant species is increasingly important for resilience to climate variability and water scarcity. Cities adopting water-efficient design reduce stormwater flooding and municipal water demand.

### The Philippine National Tree: Narra

The **Narra** (_Pterocarpus indicus_) is the national tree of the Philippines. Narra is a hardwood tree valued for its timber, nitrogen-fixing properties, and ecological importance. In landscape design, Narra is used in urban reforestation and ecological restoration projects for its site-building capability and shade provision. The tree's cultural significance and ecological role make it a preferred species in Philippine landscape design.
