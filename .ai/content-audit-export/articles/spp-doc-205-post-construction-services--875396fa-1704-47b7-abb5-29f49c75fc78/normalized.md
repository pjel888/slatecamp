## Overview

**SPP Document 205** governs **Post-Construction Services** — the professional architectural services rendered after a building has been constructed and turned over to the owner. It replaces the earlier **1979 UAP Doc. 205** and forms part of the Supplemental Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRR) of **Republic Act No. 9266** (The Architecture Act of 2004).

## Learning Goal

After reading this article, you should be able to:

Identify the scope of SPP Doc 205 Post-Construction Services: building start-up and commissioning, preparation of record (as-built) drawings, post-occupancy evaluation, facility management support, and maintenance and operation manuals (MOMM); distinguish post-construction services from construction administration under SPP 202; apply the per-diem or fixed-fee compensation basis.
---
SPP 205 is strategically placed as the **final document in the core services sequence** (SPP 201 through 205), and together with SPP 206 (Comprehensive Architectural Services), it represents the full lifecycle of architectural involvement from project inception through the productive life of the built structure. Post-construction services are what give full meaning to what the profession calls **Comprehensive Architectural Services** — they close the loop between design intent and long-term building performance.

## Introduction and Policy rationale

### 1.1 Beyond Maintenance and Upkeep

Building administration and management **goes beyond maintenance and upkeep functions**. It requires the services of a **multi-disciplinary professional** who can perform a wide range of functions for the efficient and economical operation and maintenance of a building, facility, or complex. An architect — trained to coordinate structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, interiors, and site systems — is uniquely suited for this role.

### 1.2 Keeping Communications Open

A defining feature of post-construction services is the maintenance of **multi-party communication channels**. Specifically, SPP 205 keeps channels open among:

- Building owners and operators
- Architects and engineers (designers)
- Builders and contractors (constructors)
- Manufacturers and suppliers (material and product vendors)
- Building research groups (technical knowledge sources)
- Utility providers
- Furniture and equipment vendors
- Building administrators and property managers

This continuous communication network ensures that information on building performance and upkeep remains accessible to all stakeholders throughout the building's operational life.

### 1.3 The Architect's Role: Building Administrator and/or Property Manager

The **Architect may be engaged as the Building Administrator and/or Property Manager** of a commercial, industrial, residential, or institutional building, facility, or complex. The primary objective of this engagement is to **maximize the lifespan of the building** in order to **produce the maximum economic return** for the owner. This positions SPP 205 not merely as a maintenance document but as a strategic asset management framework — the architect's mandate is to protect and grow the value of the built asset over time.

***

## Scope of Services

The SPP 205 scope of services is organized into two principal clusters:

1. **Building and Facilities Administration** (Section 2.1) — active, ongoing management of the building's physical and operational condition
2. **Post-Construction Evaluation** (Section 2.2) — retrospective assessment of the building's performance relative to design intent

***

### 2.1 Building and Facilities Administration

Building and Facilities Administration encompasses four sub-services that together constitute the full operational management mandate of the architect as Building Administrator.

#### 2.1.1 Building Maintenance

The Architect shall:

- **See to it that the building and all its parts** — structure, plumbing, electrical, partitions, finishes, etc. — are all in good condition
- **Formulate and enforce rules** for the proper use of the building and facility, particularly in the common areas and the emergency/egress/exit areas
- **Monitor security services** — ensuring adequate security coverage for the property and its occupants
- **Monitor maintenance and upkeep services** — cleanliness of corridors, lobbies, stairs and other common areas, exits, parking areas, and garbage collection

Building Maintenance is the foundational layer of post-construction service — it ensures that the physical asset remains safe, functional, and presentable for occupants and visitors.

#### 2.1.2 Grounds and Landscaping Supervision

The Architect shall:

- **Supervise landscape contractors and gardeners** for the proper watering, pruning, trimming, and maintenance of the landscape (both hardscapes and softscapes)
- **Maintain orderly entrances, exits, and parking areas** — ensuring vehicular and pedestrian circulation is functional and safe
- **Maintain streets and road rights-of-way (RROWs), walkways, and ramps** — keeping all outdoor circulation routes in compliance with accessibility and safety standards

Grounds and Landscaping Supervision reflects the architect's understanding that a building's value is inseparable from the quality of its surrounding environment.

#### 2.1.3 Building Equipment Maintenance

The Architect shall:

- **Assist the proper third parties** in seeing to it that all building equipment — air-conditioning systems, sprinkler systems, generators, transformers, telecommunications equipment, and other installed systems — are **properly maintained and in good working condition**

The use of "assist the proper third parties" is deliberate: the architect does not personally service or repair technical equipment (which requires separate licensed trades), but **coordinates and monitors** the activities of specialist service contractors to ensure that equipment is maintained per manufacturers' specifications and building code requirements.

#### 2.1.4 Business Development and Management

The Architect shall:

- **Innovate schemes to attain maximum building occupancy** — developing and implementing tenant attraction and retention strategies to optimize the building's revenue-generating capacity
- **Bill the tenants for rentals and utilities** — including electricity, water, telephone, cable, gas, and other related dues and maintenance charges

Business Development and Management is the most commercially oriented sub-service under SPP 205. It positions the Architect as a property manager in the full business sense — responsible not only for physical upkeep but also for **financial performance and occupancy optimization** of the building portfolio.

### 2.2 Post-Construction Evaluation

The **Post-Construction Evaluation** cluster is triggered **upon the request of the Owner** — it is not a routine ongoing function like Building and Facilities Administration, but a specific analytical engagement initiated at the owner's direction.

The Architect shall:

**a. Evaluate the initial design program vs. the actual use of the facility**
Assesses whether the spaces, systems, and infrastructure originally designed for the building are being used as intended — identifying programmatic mismatches that may require modifications (e.g., under-utilized spaces, overloaded systems, or informal space repurposing by occupants).

**b. Determine the effectiveness of the various building systems and the materials systems in use**
Evaluates how well the structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and envelope systems are performing relative to design specifications and occupant expectations — including energy performance, maintenance costs, and system reliability.

**c. Assist the proper third parties in evaluating the functional effectiveness of the design and construction process undertaken**
Facilitates multi-party reviews by involving the original designers, engineers, contractors, and specialist consultants in a structured assessment of the entire design and construction process — identifying best practices and lessons learned for future projects.

**d. Study, research, and give solutions to any discovered/emerging/evolving defects and failures**
Addresses defects such as shrinkage, water seepage, spalling, settlement, and other building pathologies that emerge during the building's operational life. This is **explicitly referred to in SPP 205 as "forensic investigation" of the building's systems**, with a cross-reference to **SPP 203.13 (Forensic Architecture)**.

## Manner of Providing Services

As with other SPP documents, the Architect under SPP 205 may enter into contract with the Owner in **two possible ways**:

### 3.1 Dual Capacity: Architect-of-Record + Consulting Architect for Post-Construction Services

The original **Architect-of-Record (Aor)** who designed and supervised the building assumes the additional role of **Building Administrator and/or Property Manager**. This arrangement offers several advantages:

- The Aor has **intimate knowledge of the design intent**, construction details, and building systems — making defect identification and performance evaluation significantly more effective
- Communication between the operational and design phases is seamless, reducing the risk of misinterpretation
- The owner benefits from **continuity of professional relationship** throughout the full project lifecycle

### 3.2 Consulting Architect for Post-Construction Services Only

A **separate Consulting Architect (CA)** is retained solely for post-construction services, independent of the original design team. This may be preferable when:

- The original Aor is no longer available or no longer engaged
- The owner requires an **independent, objective assessment** of the building's performance (especially relevant for post-construction evaluations that may reveal design or construction deficiencies)
- The building was designed by an in-house team or a foreign architect not eligible to practice in the Philippines

### The Incentive Logic of the Percentage Method

The percentage-of-gross-rentals method is notable because it **aligns the Architect's financial interest with the Owner's asset performance**:

- If the Architect successfully innovates schemes to increase building occupancy (2.1.4 Business Development), gross rentals increase — and so does the Architect's fee
- Conversely, poor building administration that drives tenants away reduces rentals and reduces the Architect's fee
- This creates a **performance-based compensation structure** that incentivizes proactive, high-quality building management
- The owner also separately pays **repair and maintenance expenses** — the 4–6% fee covers the Architect's professional service, not the cost of physical works

## Practical Notes for Architects and Clients

**For Architects:**

- The **Architect as Building Administrator** is a distinct professional engagement from the Architect as designer — it requires a separate service agreement, clearly defined scope, and separate compensation. Do not conflate post-construction services with the warranty obligations that flow from the design contract.
- The **forensic investigation cross-reference** to SPP 203 is a practical reminder: when post-construction evaluation reveals systemic defects (especially those suggesting design or construction negligence), a specialist Forensic Architect under SPP 203.13 should be engaged — both to protect the owner's legal position and to protect the building administrator from being perceived as covering up findings.
- Under the **percentage-of-gross-rentals** compensation model, the Architect's business development mandate (2.1.4) is not optional — it is a core deliverable. Architects taking on SPP 205 engagements should have competence in leasing strategy, tenant relations, and facilities financial management, or should partner with qualified property managers.

**For Clients / Building Owners:**

- Engaging the **original Architect-of-Record** under SPP 205 (dual capacity) is generally the most cost-effective option for the first 5–10 years after turnover — the Aor's intimate knowledge of the building's design and construction significantly reduces the time and cost of diagnosing operational issues.
- The **4–6% of gross rentals** fee structure means that for large commercial buildings with high rental income, the monthly Architect's fee can be substantial — owners should evaluate whether the Architect's full Building Administrator scope justifies this rate versus a more limited consulting retainer under PFPE or MDPE.
- Post-construction evaluation (Section 2.2) should be scheduled **systematically** — ideally at 1 year, 3 years, and 5 years post-turnover — rather than only triggered by visible defects. Early evaluations catch system failures before they become costly, and provide structured feedback to the design team for future projects.
