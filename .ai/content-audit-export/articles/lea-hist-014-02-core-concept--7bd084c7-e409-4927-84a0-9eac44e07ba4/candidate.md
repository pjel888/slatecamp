# The Modern Skyscraper

## Board Exam Quick Check

Before choosing a style label, identify the load path. Is the building masonry bearing-wall, hybrid, or metal/steel-frame with a non-load-bearing exterior skin?

## What You Need to Know for the LEA

The modern skyscraper is tested through structure, economics, and expression. The key shift is away from thick load-bearing masonry as the main height limit and toward metal or steel framing, curtain walls, elevators, fireproofing, deep urban land values, and repeated rentable office floors.

Chicago Architecture Center source anchors support the Home Insurance Building as an early skyscraper associated with a metal frame skeleton and non-load-bearing exterior sheathing. The same source family supports the Monadnock as a transitional case from load-bearing masonry to skeleton-frame construction, and the Reliance Building as an early skyscraper with glass and white terra-cotta facade. Chicago History and CAC sources support Burnham & Root as major Chicago tall-building innovators.

## Core Concept

William Le Baron Jenney's Home Insurance Building is the standard exam anchor for the metal-frame skeleton shift. Use careful language: the frame reduced reliance on load-bearing masonry and made height, stability, and flexible walls more feasible. Avoid overstating that every load condition was suddenly solved in one building.

Louis Sullivan gives the skyscraper its famous design principle. Form follows function means that a tall office building should express its vertical stacking, structure, and purpose rather than hide behind arbitrary historical costume. Sullivan did not reject ornament. He rejected ornament that contradicts or conceals the building's structural and functional order.

## Real World Scenario

If a preservation brief says a 1890s office tower has six-foot-thick corner walls, it may be closer to load-bearing or hybrid masonry logic. If the exterior wall is hung on a frame, the conservation problem changes: inspect frame, fireproofing, cladding attachment, windows, and elevator/lobby systems. The LEA clue is not just height; it is how the height is carried.

## How It Is Tested

Expect structural recognition: metal or steel skeleton plus curtain wall points to the skyscraper breakthrough; thick masonry walls point to earlier or transitional systems.

Expect architect identification: Jenney for Home Insurance; Sullivan for form follows function and Wainwright; Burnham & Root for several Chicago tall-building examples.

Expect theory questions: vertical piers, repeated bays, and ornament aligned to structure are Sullivanian expression, not ornament-free minimalism.

## Common Mistakes

Do not overclaim Home Insurance as a perfect modern steel skyscraper by today's standards. Treat it as an early, source-backed metal-frame breakthrough.

Do not say Sullivan rejected ornament. He used ornament when it reinforced the building's order.

Do not call Chicago School purely American in isolation. The safe point is Chicago's systematic union of frame technology, elevators, real-estate economics, and architectural expression.

## Key Terms

**Skeleton Frame** - Columns and beams carry major loads so exterior walls can become thinner and more flexible.

**Curtain Wall** - Non-load-bearing exterior skin hung from or supported by a frame.

**Vertical Expression** - Facade composition that emphasizes height and repeated stacked office floors.

**Form Follows Function** - Sullivan's principle that the exterior expression should grow from the building's purpose and structure.

**Chicago School** - Late nineteenth-century Chicago commercial architecture associated with tall office buildings, frames, elevators, and direct expression.
