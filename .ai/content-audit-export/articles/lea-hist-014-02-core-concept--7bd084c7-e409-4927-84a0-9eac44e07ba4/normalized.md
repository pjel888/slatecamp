## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal

Identify the four-part structural system of the skyscraper — steel frame carries load, curtain wall hangs, elevator enables vertical rise, piers express height — and trace its evolution from the Home Insurance Building (1885) to the Woolworth and Chrysler Buildings.
## Core Concept

The modern skyscraper did not originate in New York or Europe. It emerged in Chicago in the 1880s through a convergence of economic necessity, structural innovation, and architectural ambition. Chicago had burned in 1871; the city rebuilt quickly with new technology. Real estate was expensive; building upward was the only solution. Iron and steel frames could carry weight; curtain walls (thin exterior layers supported by frame) could enclose tall buildings. Electric elevators made upper floors accessible. These technologies converged to make the skyscraper economically viable and structurally possible.

**William Le Baron Jenney** (1832–1907) designed the **Home Insurance Building** (Chicago, 1885)—the first building with a complete iron frame skeleton. It was 10 stories, modest by later standards, but revolutionary in principle: the exterior walls no longer bore weight. The frame carried all loads. This concept defined the skyscraper. Load-bearing walls had been the basis of all architecture for millennia. The iron frame liberated architects from this constraint.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Note" status="preserved"]

**Louis Sullivan** (1856–1924) took Jenney's structural innovation and asked: What should this new building type look like? His answer was "form follows function"—the exterior expression should reflect internal structure. A skyscraper is a vertical stack of stories; express this verticality. The **Wainwright Building** (St. Louis, 1891) demonstrates his logic: a frame of tall piers (expressing verticality) alternates with horizontal spandrels (expressing each floor). The building reads as a unified vertical composition, not a stack of boxes. Sullivan rejected ornamentation that disguised function. Every line of ornament should enhance structural expression, not hide it.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0004 component="Warn" status="preserved"]

The **Chicago School** synthesized innovation: frame structure from Jenney, vertical expression from Sullivan, honest material revelation from both. Architects like **Daniel Burnham**, **John Root**, and **Dankmar Adler** (Sullivan's partner) refined the formula. By 1893, Chicago hosted the World's Columbian Exposition, celebrating American technological and architectural achievement. The skyscraper became America's contribution to world architecture—functional, tall, direct, without European historical reference.

## Key Terms

**Iron Frame Skeleton** — Structural system where columns and beams carry all loads; exterior walls are non-load-bearing curtain walls; pioneered by Jenney at Home Insurance Building.

**Curtain Wall** — Non-load-bearing exterior wall supported by structural frame; thin, economical, allows flexibility in interior planning.

**Form Follows Function** — Sullivan's principle that architectural expression should reflect building's purpose and structural system, not applied historical styles.

**Vertical Expression** — Design principle emphasizing upward movement through repeated piers, spandrels, and proportional relationships; expresses actual vertical stacking of floors.

**Spandrel** — Horizontal panel between windows in successive stories; in Sullivan's work, spandrels are expressed to read as separate from piers, clarifying floor separation.

**Chicago School** — Architectural movement (1880–1910) emphasizing structural innovation, vertical expression, and honest material use; centered on skyscraper design.

**Masonry Curtain Wall** — Thin outer layer of brick or stone supported by frame; provides weather protection and finish; allows rapid construction.

**Electric Elevator** — Mechanical system making upper floors accessible; prerequisite for skyscraper viability; enabled occupation of 15+ story buildings.
