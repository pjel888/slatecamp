# Renaissance Architecture

## LEA Point
Renaissance architecture is the early modern return to measured classical order: proportion, geometry, humanist clarity, and controlled reuse of Roman forms. For the LEA, identify it by calm symmetry, round arches, domes, pilasters, columns, pediments, and plans that read as rational compositions rather than medieval vertical ascent or Baroque theater.

## Source Anchors
Florence anchors the early Renaissance setting. UNESCO describes the city as a symbol of the Renaissance and names Brunelleschi among the masters tied to its historic center. Use Florence for the shift toward perspective, measured space, and structural invention.

Brunelleschi is the safest exam anchor for the Florence Cathedral dome and for clear, modular architectural space. When a question asks who solved a major dome problem at Florence or who made early Renaissance space read with geometric clarity, the answer is Brunelleschi.

Alberti is the theory anchor. His architectural writing and facade practice translate classical proportion, harmony, and humanist order into rules that later architects could study and repeat.

Bramante is the High Renaissance central-plan anchor. The Tempietto and the first St. Peter plan represent ideal geometry, centralized sacred space, and classical restraint.

Palladio is the villa and treatise anchor. UNESCO source-maps Vicenza and the Palladian villas as work based on close study of Roman classical architecture, with influence across Europe and North America. Use Palladio for villa symmetry, temple-front facades, proportional systems, and the Four Books on Architecture.

## Exam Pattern
Renaissance questions often ask for the difference between rediscovery and spectacle. Renaissance design seeks legible order; Baroque design later pushes motion, theatrical light, emotional intensity, and spatial surprise.

Another common pattern is author matching: Brunelleschi for the dome and early clarity, Alberti for theory, Bramante for centralized sacred geometry, and Palladio for villas, proportional codification, and Palladian influence.

## Guardrails
Do not reduce the Renaissance to copied Roman ornament. The source-safe answer is that ancient forms were interpreted through humanist proportion, Christian patronage, new urban ambitions, and practical building problems.

Avoid exact numeric claims about dome dimensions unless the question itself supplies the number and the local source map supports it.

## Real World Scenario
On a board exam image item, a compact villa with a central domed hall, four temple-front facades, and strict bilateral symmetry should point you toward Palladio and the Palladian villa tradition. A small circular chapel with a Doric colonnade should point you toward Bramante and centralized High Renaissance geometry.
