## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal

Identify the key Renaissance architects (Brunelleschi, Alberti, Bramante, Michelangelo, Palladio) and their major works, and explain how classical orders, linear perspective, humanist proportion, and centralized plans defined Renaissance architectural theory.
## Core Concept

**Renaissance architecture** (14th–16th centuries, centered in Florence and Italy) represents a conscious *revival and reinvention* of Classical Antiquity coupled with new mathematical and humanistic thinking. Medieval Gothic gives way to rational proportions, geometric perfection, and human-scaled spaces inspired by Roman temples and Italian Renaissance humanism.

The period divides into **Early Renaissance** (15th century, Florence) and **High Renaissance** (early 16th century, Rome). Early Renaissance rediscovered Classical orders (Doric, Ionic, Corinthian), geometric proportion, and linear perspective in architecture. High Renaissance synthesized these discoveries into monumental, spatially integrated designs.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0002 component="Tip" status="preserved"]

**Filippo Brunelleschi** (1377–1446) launched the Renaissance. His **Florence Cathedral dome** (construction 1420–1436, Florence) solved a 100-year-old engineering problem: how to vault the cathedral's 42-meter opening without a wooden centering (lost to earlier wars). Brunelleschi used a double-shell dome with internal ribbing (24 meridian ribs and 10 parallel ribs connecting the shells), creating the engineering marvel that announced the Renaissance to the world. He also studied Roman ruins, mastered mathematics, and established linear perspective in architecture (the Ospedlea degli Innocenti, 1419, demonstrates perfect geometric clarity).

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Note" status="preserved"]

**Leon Battista Alberti** (1404–1472) was architecture's first theorist. His *De re aedificatoria* (*On the Art of Building in Ten Books*, written 1443–1452, first printed 1485) codified Renaissance principles: proportion, harmony, human measure, and Classical orders as the basis for all design. His buildings (Palazzo Rucellai, Florence, begun 1446–completed c. 1460s; Santa Maria Novella facade, Florence, 1470; Sant'Andrea, Mantua, begun 1472–completed 1731) translate theory into practice—rational facades using Classical proportions, geometric clarity, harmonic relationships derived from musical intervals.

**Donato Bramante** (1444–1514) pioneered the **centralized Renaissance church**—a perfect geometric form (usually circular, square, or Greek cross) symbolizing divine harmony. His **Tempietto** (1502, Rome, S. Pietro in Montorio) is the Renaissance ideal distilled: a miniature circular temple with a colonnade of Doric columns, golden proportions, and Classical simplicity. Later, he designed the initial plan for **St. Peter's Basilica** (begun 1506, Rome) as a Greek cross with a central dome, establishing the model High Renaissance churches followed.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0004 component="Warn" status="preserved"]

**Michelangelo** (1475–1564) dominated High Renaissance and pioneered Mannerism. After Bramante's death, he became chief architect of St. Peter's (1547), redesigning it into a more compact Greek cross emphasizing the dome. His dome (1547–1564, completed posthumously) becomes the architectural icon of the Renaissance—a culmination of engineering (double shell like Brunelleschi's), proportion, and humanistic ambition. Michelangelo's late work shows Mannerist traits: tension, distortion of Classical rules, dramatic light-and-shadow contrasts.

**Andrea Palladio** (1508–1580) codified Renaissance villa architecture and proportion systems. His *Four Books on Architecture* (*I Quattro Libri*, 1570) systematized orders, proportions, and designs, becoming the most influential architectural treatise after Vitruvius. His villas (Villa Rotonda, Vicenza, begun 1567–completed 1590s; Villa Emo, Fanzolo, 1558–1559) use Classical temple fronts with colonnades, central rotundas with domes, and perfect geometric proportions. These designs synthesize rural functionality with Classical grandeur, establishing a model for manor houses across Europe and America for centuries.

**Renaissance principles:**
- **Human measure**: Proportion based on human body and Classical ratios
- **Geometric perfection**: Circle, square, and Greek cross as ideal forms
- **Classical orders**: Systematic use of Doric, Ionic, Corinthian columns and entablatures
- **Harmonic proportion**: Proportions derived from musical intervals (ratios like 1:1, 2:3, 3:4)
- **Centralized plans**: Domes and rotundas emphasizing vertical unity rather than longitudinal procession
- **Rational clarity**: Elimination of Medieval complexity; emphasis on legible structure
- **Integration of theory and practice**: Architects as learned designers, not mere builders

## Key Terms

- **Dome**: A hemispherical or near-hemispherical vault; Renaissance version often uses double shells and Classical proportions
- **Double-shell dome**: Two concentric domes with internal ribbing and space between, as in Brunelleschi's Florence Cathedral dome
- **Classical orders**: Systematic column types—Doric (sturdy), Ionic (graceful), Corinthian (ornate)—derived from Roman architecture
- **Entablature**: The horizontal structure above columns, consisting of architrave, frieze, and cornice
- **Harmonic proportion**: Proportional relationships derived from musical intervals (ratios like 2:3, 3:4)
- **Centralized plan**: A church or building organized around a central vertical axis (dome or rotunda) rather than longitudinal procession
- **Greek cross**: A cross with four equal arms; often used in centralized Renaissance churches
- **Pediment**: A triangular gable above a portico or facade, derived from Classical temples
- **Portico**: A covered entrance or porch with columns, derived from Classical temple design
- **Mannerism**: A late Renaissance style emphasizing tension, distortion of Classical rules, and dramatic effects (Michelangelo's later work)
