# Value Engineering as an Architectural Service: Concept, Process, and Practice Ethics

## Exam scope

Value Engineering is a disciplined review process for improving value while protecting required project functions. The professional boundary is the key exam point: VE must optimize required function and total value, not disguise quality loss as savings.

## What Value Engineering means

The source handbook defines Value Engineering as a systematic, multidisciplined approach designed to optimize the value of each dollar spent. The process identifies, analyzes, and establishes a value for the function of an item or system. Its objective is to satisfy the required functions of the project at the lowest initial total cost and cost over the life of the project.

The same source qualifies the objective: VE must remain consistent with performance, reliability, maintainability, safety, and aesthetics. That qualification is the ethical guardrail. A recommendation that lowers first cost by undermining required function, safety, durability, or design intent is not source-safe VE.

## When to perform VE

VE should be performed early in the design process before major decisions have been completely incorporated into the design. The source identifies the preferred timing as at or near the end of preliminary design or before 30 percent design.

This timing matters in architectural practice. Early VE can compare systems, layouts, constructability, material strategies, maintenance implications, and life-cycle cost before drawings and specifications are locked. Late VE often becomes deletion or substitution pressure, which is more likely to create scope, quality, or coordination risk.

## Who participates

The VE session should be coordinated by the project manager or design manager and performed by a multidisciplined team preferably not part of the design team. The source lists possible participants from electrical, mechanical, civil/structural, construction engineering, architecture, cost estimation, construction management, and operations and maintenance. The designer of record provides support to the VE team.

## How recommendations are handled

After project-manager review, the design team determines whether to adopt or reject each VE proposal and finalizes the VE response report. The report should summarize accepted VE proposals with revised capital and implementation costs and list rejected proposals with reasons for rejection.

This record is the professional control point. It prevents "value" from becoming an undocumented instruction to reduce quality. It also lets the architect explain why a lower-cost proposal was rejected when it conflicts with required function, performance, safety, maintainability, or design intent.

## Link to design reviews and service ethics

The source connects VE to design reviews. Preliminary engineering review should confirm that selected approaches, major design concepts, and VE recommendations are resolved before final design proceeds. Later constructability reviews verify that the technical specifications, drawings, and bid documents can be built.

For LEA practice, answer VE questions by asking four source-grounded checks: What required function is being protected? What total initial and life-cycle cost is affected? What performance, reliability, maintainability, safety, or aesthetic requirement is at risk? Is the accept/reject decision documented with reasons?

## Real World Scenario

During preliminary design, the owner asks the team to reduce cost without weakening required performance. A source-safe VE response starts by identifying the required function, life-cycle cost effect, and risks to performance, reliability, maintainability, safety, and aesthetics. The VE team may propose alternatives, but after project-manager review the design team must decide which proposals to adopt or reject. The final response report should list accepted proposals with revised costs and rejected proposals with reasons, so cost savings do not become undocumented quality reduction.
