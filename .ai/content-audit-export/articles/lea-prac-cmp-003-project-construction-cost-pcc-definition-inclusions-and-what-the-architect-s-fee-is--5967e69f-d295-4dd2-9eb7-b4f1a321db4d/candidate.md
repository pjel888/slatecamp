## Project Construction Cost (PCC) — Definition, Inclusions, and What the Architect's Fee Is Based On

## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- Define Project Construction Cost (PCC) and its inclusions.
- Identify what is included in PCC: materials, labor, equipment, contractor overhead.
- Explain what is excluded: land, architect's fee, owner's administrative costs.
- Calculate the architect's basic fee using PCC × percentage.

---
### Definition of Project Construction Cost

Project Construction Cost is the total estimated construction cost of the building and all site work directly related to the building's construction. PCC includes all labor, materials, equipment, and contractor overhead costs necessary to complete the project as designed. PCC does not include the architect's fee, consultant fees, land acquisition, site acquisition costs, financing costs, owner's contingency reserves for budget overruns, or post-construction operations costs.

### Components Included in PCC

PCC encompasses all building structural elements, exterior envelope (roof, walls, windows, doors, exterior finishes), interior construction (partition walls, interior doors, floor and ceiling finishes), all mechanical and electrical systems (HVAC, plumbing, electrical distribution, fire protection, special systems), site work including grading, drainage, paving, landscaping, and utility connections to the property line, and all contractor overhead and profit. The cost reflects the contractor's estimates or current market rates for labor and materials.

### Components Explicitly Excluded from PCC

Several cost categories fall outside PCC calculation. Architect fees (whether percentage-of-cost, MDPE, lump sum, or hourly) are excluded. Consultant fees for structural engineering, MEP design, sustainability certification, cost estimating, or specialized consulting are excluded. Per SPP 202 Section 7.17, PCC explicitly excludes fees for the Architect, Engineer, Specialist Consultants, and construction inspector salaries. Land and site acquisition costs, environmental remediation costs, and pre-construction site surveys fall outside PCC. Existing building demolition costs should be clarified in the contract, as they may be considered part of site work if necessary for the new construction. Owner contingency reserves, change order budgets, and post-construction owner reserves are excluded. Financing costs, construction period insurance, and owner's project management costs are excluded. Moving and relocation costs, furnishings (beyond built-in casework), and equipment (beyond permanently installed systems) are excluded.

### PCC Determination Timing and Methodology

PCC is first submitted during the Schematic Design phase as a Statement of Probable Project Construction Cost (SPPCC) based on current cost parameters. It is refined during Design Development as more detailed information becomes available. The architect obtains cost estimates based on available project information at each phase—initial estimates during Schematic Design based on conceptual plans, and refined estimates during Design Development based on more detailed design documents. If the project proceeds directly to Contract Documents without a distinct Design Development phase, PCC is determined when documents are sufficiently complete for cost estimation.

<LawCallout code="ra9266">RA 9266, Section 22 — The architect's compensation shall be based on a percentage of the construction cost or other mutually agreed basis as provided in the contract. UAP SPP Documents provide the standard fee schedule.</LawCallout>

During Construction Documents preparation, PCC may be refined if significant design changes occur. The PCC used for fee calculation purposes should be clearly defined in the architect-client contract, typically established at a specific project milestone (such as end of Design Development or Contract Documents completion). If the final bid or actual construction cost materially differs from the PCC estimate, the contract terms govern whether fee adjustment occurs; typically, the original PCC-based fee applies unless the contract specifically reserves adjustment rights.

### Cost Estimation Standards

Cost estimating typically follows one of three approaches: parametric estimation (cost per unit—cost per square meter of building area, per bed, per classroom), detailed line-item estimation (material and labor costs for specific building elements), or general contractor bidding (contractor provides lump sum or detailed estimate based on plans and specifications). Parametric estimation provides speed but less precision; detailed estimation provides accuracy but requires more data. For fee calculation, the most reliable approach is cost estimating based on plans and specifications by qualified estimators.

### Variations in PCC by Project Phase

During preliminary phases (Schematic Design), PCC estimates may have accuracy ranges of ±25–35%. By Design Development, accuracy typically improves to ±15–20%. By Contract Documents completion, estimates should approach ±10% accuracy. These accuracy ranges reflect the normal uncertainty in construction cost estimation. The architect and client should establish clear expectations regarding PCC estimation methodology and accuracy levels in the contract.

### Contingency Reserves and PCC

<ExamTip>**LEA Pattern:** Given a project budget, determine the architect's fee. Common scenario: PCC = Php 10M × 12% fee = Php 1.2M. Watch for distractors that include land cost or architect's fee in the PCC calculation.</ExamTip>
Owner contingency reserves for construction cost overruns are excluded from PCC. These reserves protect the owner against cost escalation during construction but do not represent costs the architect anticipated during design. Contingency reserves typically range from 5–15% of the estimated construction cost and remain separate from PCC for fee calculation purposes.

### PCC and Fee Recalculation Provisions

The contract should clearly establish whether the PCC fee calculation is fixed based on the Design Development estimate or subject to adjustment if final costs differ materially. In Design-Bid-Build delivery, the lowest responsive bid typically establishes actual construction cost; if the bid significantly exceeds the PCC estimate, disputes may arise regarding fee adjustment. Most contracts establish the fee based on the PCC estimate at Design Development and do not adjust if final costs differ unless the contract explicitly reserves that right.

### Multi-Building and Phased Projects

For developments with multiple buildings or phased delivery, PCC calculation addresses each building or phase separately unless the contract specifies aggregate calculation. Some projects may justify single-fee structures based on total project cost; others may require individual building calculations based on each building's characteristics. The contract should specify whether PCC is calculated building-by-building, phase-by-phase, or in aggregate.

### Special Considerations for Specialized Systems

Projects incorporating specialized systems—laboratory equipment, theater systems, specialized HVAC, food service equipment—should ensure these costs are included in the cost estimator's scope. Some cost estimating methodologies underestimate specialized system costs; the architect should verify that cost estimates comprehensively include all project scope.
