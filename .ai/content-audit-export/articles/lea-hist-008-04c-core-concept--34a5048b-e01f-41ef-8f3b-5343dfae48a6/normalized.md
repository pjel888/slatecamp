## Learning Goal

Identify the three defining characteristics of De Stijl — primary colors (red, yellow, blue), orthogonal geometry (right angles only), and asymmetrical balance — and explain how the Schröder House (Rietveld, 1924) applied Neoplasticist theory to three-dimensional space.
## Core Concept
**De Stijl** (Dutch for "The Style"), **Constructivism**, and **Expressionism** all emerged between 1917 and 1925 as radical breaks from academic architecture. Yet they pursued fundamentally different visions.

**De Stijl**, founded in the Netherlands by **Theo van Doesburg**, **Piet Mondrian**, and **Gerrit Rietveld**, reduced form to elemental components: primary colors (red, yellow, blue), orthogonal geometry (right angles only), and white space. The movement believed that this rigorous abstraction expressed universal harmony beneath surface complexity. **Gerrit Rietveld's Schröder House** (1924, Utrecht) is the movement's masterpiece: a spatial composition where walls shift, slide, and dematerialize; primary colors accent structural lines; and the interior transforms based on movable partitions. Every element serves the reduction of form to its essence. De Stijl rejected the European tradition of solid, bounded spaces in favor of interpenetrating planes and voids.

**Constructivism** emerged from Soviet Russia with revolutionary fervor. **Vladimir Tatlin**, **Konstantin Melnikov**, and others sought to express the dynamism and technological power of the communist state. The **Tatlin Tower** (1919–1920, unbuilt) exemplifies this: a spiraling, asymmetrical iron structure tilted at an angle, never perfectly vertical, evoking movement and political thrust. **Melnikov House** (1927–1929, Moscow) showcases his style with its twin cylinders, minimal fenestration, and sculptural solidity—a dwelling as a revolutionary gesture. Constructivism embraced geometric abstraction but favored boldness, asymmetry, and technological apparatus over De Stijl's serene order.

**Expressionism** sought to evoke emotional and psychological response through exaggerated, often organic or sculptural forms. **Erich Mendelsohn's Einstein Tower** (1919–1924, Potsdam, Germany) appears to flow and wave; its curves suggest cosmic dynamism befitting its use as an astrophysics laboratory. Rather than grid and right angle, Expressionism deployed curves, tilts, and sculptural massing to create buildings that feel alive. Where De Stijl and Constructivism abstracted form rationally, Expressionism aimed to make abstraction felt viscerally.

## Key Terms
- **De Stijl**: Dutch movement (1917–1931) reducing art and design to primary colors, orthogonal geometry, and white space to express universal harmony.
- **Orthogonal geometry**: Architecture composed exclusively of right angles and straight lines, avoiding curves or diagonals.
- **Primary colors**: Red, yellow, and blue; used by De Stijl as universal visual principles.
- **Constructivism**: Soviet avant-garde movement emphasizing technological apparatus, asymmetrical dynamism, and revolutionary ideology in form.
- **Asymmetrical design**: Composition lacking bilateral symmetry; favored by Constructivism to suggest movement and instability.
- **Expressionism**: Movement pursuing emotional and psychological response through exaggerated, sculptural, or distorted abstract forms.
- **Sculptural form**: Architecture designed to evoke three-dimensional art; emphasizing volume and surface modulation over planar abstraction.
- **Interpenetrating planes**: Overlapping, sliding surfaces and spaces that dissolve traditional boundaries between inside and outside.
- **Technological apparatus**: Visible expression of mechanical systems, materials, and industrial processes as design elements.
- **Dematerialization of walls**: The visual dissolution of solid barriers through transparency, rotation, and open planning.
