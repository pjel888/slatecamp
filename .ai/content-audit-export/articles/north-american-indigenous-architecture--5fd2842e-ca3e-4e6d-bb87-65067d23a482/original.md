# North American Indigenous Architecture

## The Real-World Scenario (The Hook)

You are designing a flood-resilient community evacuation center in a dense, low-income barangay in Manila. The program requires high thermal mass for passive cooling, a communal hall accommodating 200 displaced families, and rapidly deployable emergency shelter for open ground. Three distinct North American indigenous building traditions — Pueblos, Longhouses, and Tipis — each solve one of these problems using locally available materials and zero mechanical systems.

## Core Concepts and Memory Aid

**Memory Aid — "P-L-T" (Pueblos, Longhouses, Tipis)**

Think: *"People Live Together"* — all three traditions are fundamentally about community-sclea shelter, not individual houses.

- **Pueblos (Ancestral Puebloans / Modern Pueblo peoples):** Multi-story adobe and stone apartment complexes in the arid American Southwest, continuously inhabited for over 1,000 years. Sun-dried adobe bricks (clay, sand, straw) laid over stone foundations create massive thermal mass that absorbs daytime desert heat and radiates it at night, maintaining stable interior temperatures. Small window openings, flat roofs, and subterranean ceremonial chambers (*kivas*) define the form. Key sites include Taos Pueblo (late 13th–early 14th century, still inhabited, UNESCO World Heritage Site), Mesa Verde cliff dwellings (c. 600–1300 CE, Ancestral Puebloan), and Chaco Canyon (c. 850–1150 CE, with *great houses* up to five stories).
- **Longhouses (Haudenosaunee / Iroquois Confederacy):** Elongated timber-framed communal dwellings of the Northeastern woodlands, averaging approximately 24 m long by 5.5 m wide by 5.5 m high (80 × 18 × 18 ft), though some reached 75 m (246 ft) or more. Structural frameworks of fire-hardened sapling poles were clad in bark sheets (elm or cedar). A central corridor lined with family compartments and shared hearths housed extended matrilineal families — typically 20 or more families per longhouse.
- **Tipis (Plains nations, e.g., Lakota, Cheyenne, Blackfoot):** Portable conical structures made of 14–20 lodgepole pine or red cedar poles covered with bison hides (later canvas). The tipi became widespread after the introduction of the horse to the Great Plains (post-1700s), enabling nomadic peoples to transport larger structures. Adjustable smoke flaps at the apex and a raised base perimeter provide controlled ventilation — the interior acts as a chimney, drawing fresh air from below and exhausting smoke above. The low aerodynamic profile withstands sustained high winds.

**Philippine Link:** Pueblo thermal mass = passive cooling for tropical heat without air conditioning; Longhouse communal layout = high-density evacuation shelter planning; Tipi ventilation and portability = rapid-deployment emergency structures for typhoon response.

## LEA Exam Traps and Common Mistakes

- **Conflating Ancestral Puebloans with Plains tribes:** These are entirely different cultures in different environments with different building traditions. Pueblos are permanent, multi-story, stone-and-adobe complexes in the arid Southwest. Tipis are portable, hide-covered conical structures of the Great Plains. The LEA tests precise cultural attribution — never describe Pueblo peoples as nomadic or Plains peoples as sedentary adobe builders.
- **Assuming indigenous structures are "primitive" and code-irrelevant:** Pueblo adobe construction has survived for over 700 years at Taos. The underlying principles — thermal mass, passive ventilation, compact massing, communal space planning — are the same principles codified in modern green building standards (LEED, BERDE). The LEA may ask you to extract transferable design principles, not to dismiss them.
- **Overstating longhouse dimensions:** While some exceptional structures exceeded 75 m, the typical Haudenosaunee longhouse averaged about 24 m long. The LEA may test whether you know the standard dimensions versus the maximum documented lengths — do not confuse the two.

## Active Recall Questions

**Question 1.** What is the primary passive-cooling mechanism of Pueblo adobe construction in the American Southwest?

A. Cross-ventilation through large window openings on opposite walls  
B. Evaporative cooling ponds integrated into flat roof surfaces  
C. Thermal mass — thick adobe walls absorb daytime heat and release it slowly at night, stabilizing interior temperatures across extreme diurnal swings  
D. Underground mechanical air-cooling systems connected to subterranean kivas  

> **Correct Answer: C.** Pueblo adobe walls (typically 30–60 cm thick) function as thermal batteries. In the desert Southwest, daytime temperatures can exceed 40°C while nighttime temperatures drop below 10°C. The massive earthen walls absorb heat during the day (keeping interiors cool) and radiate stored heat at night (keeping interiors warm). This thermal flywheel effect is the defining climate adaptation of Pueblo architecture. Kivas are ceremonial/community spaces, not mechanical systems.

**Question 2.** A typical Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) longhouse measured approximately:

A. 8 × 8 × 4 m (a compact square plan for single-family use)  
B. 24 × 5.5 × 5.5 m (an elongated communal hall with central hearths for multiple families)  
C. 100 × 30 × 15 m (a monumental stone assembly hall comparable to European cathedrals)  
D. 6 × 3 × 3 m (a small portable shelter for seasonal hunting camps)  

> **Correct Answer: B.** The standard Haudenosaunee longhouse averaged approximately 24 m (80 ft) long × 5.5 m (18 ft) wide × 5.5 m (18 ft) high, housing around 20 or more families in compartments along a central corridor with shared hearths. While some exceptional longhouses exceeded 75 m in length, the LEA typically tests the standard dimensions.

**Question 3.** Which feature of tipi construction provides its primary ventilation mechanism?

A. Fixed glass skylights in the hide covering  
B. Adjustable smoke flaps at the apex of the cone, creating a chimney effect that draws fresh air from the base perimeter and exhausts smoke upward  
C. Mechanically operated louvered vents powered by wind turbines  
D. Large rectangular doorways on all four cardinal orientations  

> **Correct Answer: B.** The tipi's conical form creates a natural chimney effect. Adjustable flaps at the apex (controlled by exterior poles) regulate smoke exhaust from the central hearth, while fresh air enters through the base perimeter and through the gap between the outer cover and an interior liner. This passive stack-ventilation system is effective in both calm and windy conditions — a principle applicable to natural ventilation design in Philippine tropical architecture.
