## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal
By the end of this article, you will be able to:

- Identify the components of concrete: cement, aggregates, water, and admixtures
- Explain how the water-cement ratio (w/c) controls strength and durability
- Define slump and its relationship to workability
- State standard concrete unit weight (2,400 kg/m³) and common compressive strengths
- Describe proper curing procedures and their importance

Concrete is a composite material made from cement, aggregates (fine and coarse), water, and optional admixtures. Understanding these components and their proportions—especially the water-cement ratio—determines concrete workability, strength, durability, and long-term performance in reinforced concrete structures.

## Core Concept

Concrete is created by mixing Portland cement with fine aggregate (sand), coarse aggregate (gravel or crushed stone), water, and sometimes chemical or mineral admixtures. The cement chemically reacts with water in a process called hydration, binding the aggregates into a solid mass.

**Portland Cement** is the primary binder. When mixed with water, it undergoes hydration reactions that develop strength over time. The strength gain is most rapid in the first 28 days (the standard curing period) but continues for years.

**Aggregates** comprise 70–80% of concrete volume. Fine aggregate (sand) fills spaces and improves workability; coarse aggregate (gravel, typically 10–40 mm) provides bulk and stiffness. Well-graded aggregates—with proper particle size distribution—minimize voids and produce stronger, denser concrete.

**Water-Cement Ratio (w/c)** is the mass ratio of water to cement. It is the single most critical factor controlling concrete strength and durability. A lower w/c ratio (typically 0.40–0.55 for structural concrete) produces stronger, denser, less permeable concrete but reduces workability. A higher w/c ratio improves workability but reduces strength and increases long-term creep and shrinkage.

**Slump** measures concrete workability—the distance a concrete cone subsides vertically after the cone is lifted. Higher slump (e.g., 100–150 mm) indicates more flow and easier placement; lower slump (e.g., 25–50 mm) indicates stiffer, denser concrete. Slump varies by application: thin slabs and heavily reinforced sections need higher slump; massive sections may use lower slump.

**Curing** is the process of maintaining moisture and temperature after placement to allow hydration to continue. Proper curing—typically 7 days moist or covered, or 28 days in air—is essential for reaching design strength and durability. Early thermal stresses and drying shrinkage during poor curing reduce long-term performance.

**Admixtures** are optional additives that modify concrete properties. Water reducers lower the water needed for a given slump, allowing lower w/c without loss of workability. Air entrainers create tiny air bubbles (3–7% by volume) to improve freeze-thaw durability. Retarders slow hydration for longer working time in hot weather. Pozzolans (fly ash, silica fume) react with cement hydration products, improving long-term strength, reducing permeability, and reducing heat of hydration.

**Concrete Unit Weight** for normal-weight concrete is approximately 2,400 kg/m³. Lightweight concrete weighs 1,600–1,840 kg/m³ and uses expanded clay or slate aggregates; heavyweight concrete (2,800–3,200 kg/m³) uses iron ore or barites for radiation shielding.

**Concrete Properties at 28 Days:**
- Compressive strength: _f'_c (cylinder strength) is the standard design parameter. Common values: residential 21 MPa, commercial 24–28 MPa, high-rise columns 35–41 MPa.
- Modulus of elasticity: _E_c = 4,700√_f'_c MPa (normal-weight concrete).
- Flexural strength (modulus of rupture): _f_r = 0.62λ√_f'_c MPa (λ = 1.0 for normal weight).
- Strain at crushing: ε_u = 0.003 (3 parts per thousand). This is the assumed ultimate compressive strain for reinforced concrete design.

Creep—the long-term increase in strain under sustained load—is a time-dependent property controlled by w/c, curing, and humidity. Higher w/c and poor curing increase creep; low humidity reduces creep compared to continuous wet conditions.

**Water-Cement Ratio Effect on Durability (Philippine Practice):**
For exposure to seawater or aggressive environments (common in coastal regions), w/c ≤ 0.50 and higher cement content (380–420 kg/m³) are required. For non-aggressive environments (inland), w/c ≤ 0.55 is typical.

## Philippine Practice Context

The Philippine National Structural Code (PNSC) requires concrete specifications based on exposure class. Coastal projects and structures in acidic or saline environments must use low w/c ratios and pozzolanic admixtures (fly ash is locally available). Tropical curing conditions—high temperature and humidity—accelerate early hydration but also increase drying shrinkage. Proper curing (keeping concrete wet for at least 7 days) is critical in the tropical climate to prevent early cracking and ensure durability in the humid, corrosive environment.

## How It's Tested

1. **Compressive Strength (ASTM C39):** Standard 150 × 300 mm cylinders are cast, cured, and tested at 28 days. Three cylinders per test date are common. Strength is reported as the average of the three; any specimen more than 15% below average is rejected unless the average of remaining two is at least 85% of the specified _f'_c.

2. **Slump Test (ASTM C143):** A 300 mm tall, 100 mm top/200 mm bottom diameter cone is lifted vertically after being filled with fresh concrete. The vertical distance from the original cone height to the highest point of subsided concrete is the slump. Measured immediately after placement in mm.

3. **Unit Weight and Yield (ASTM C138):** A standard container of known volume is filled with fresh concrete. The mass of concrete is divided by container volume to determine unit weight. Yield (m³ per batch) is calculated to verify mix design proportions.

<Tip>The water-cement ratio is your strongest lever to control concrete strength and durability. Every project in the Philippines should specify w/c ≤ 0.50 for coastal or aggressive environments and w/c ≤ 0.55 for normal exposure. Proper 7-day curing is non-negotiable in tropical climates.</Tip>
