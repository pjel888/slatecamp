# Architectural Design and Site Planning — Overview & Study Strategy

If Structural Conceptualization is the spine of Area B, Architectural Design and Site Planning is the brain of the entire LEA. It is the one subject that does not stay in its lane — it reaches into every other subject area, borrows concepts from all of them, and asks you to put them together into something that works for people.

It also carries more weight than any single subject in the exam. As the sole subject in Area C, it accounts for the entirety of that area's contribution to your total LEA score. Understanding what that means for your study strategy is the first step.

## What This Subject Actually Covers

Architectural Design and Site Planning spans thirteen subtopics that together describe the full scope of architectural practice — from the first pencil mark on trace paper to the final permit drawing set.

* **Design Process and Methodology** — how architects move from brief to concept to schematic to design development; programming, precedent analysis, concept generation, and design iteration.
* **Site Planning and Analysis** — reading the land before designing on it; topography, solar orientation, prevailing winds, drainage, vegetation, views, access, and how site conditions shape building placement.
* **Residential Design** — single-family homes, multi-family housing, and socialized housing; room relationships, privacy gradients, circulation, natural light, and ventilation.
* **Commercial, Business, and Mixed-Use Design** — offices, retail, hotels, and mixed-use developments; tenant flexibility, floor plate efficiency, vertical circulation, and ground-floor activation.
* **Institutional and Public Design** — schools, hospitals, government buildings, and civic facilities; specialized functional requirements, public accessibility, and life safety egress.
* **Industrial, Transportation, and Facilities Design** — factories, warehouses, airports, terminals, and bus depots; operational logic, vehicular circulation, clearance requirements, and industrial processes.
* **Environmental Design** — passive solar strategies, natural ventilation, daylighting, thermal comfort, and biophilic design; how the building structurally and spatially responds to its climate.
* **Fire Safety, Egress, and Life Safety Systems** — means of egress, exit widths, travel distances, exit stairwells, and fire compartmentalization; primarily governed by RA 9514 (Revised Fire Code of the Philippines) together with PD 1096.
* **Parking Design and Vehicular Circulation** — stall dimensions, drive aisle widths, ramp geometry, turning radii, and pedestrian-vehicle conflict reduction.
* **Graphic Presentation and Architectural Drawings** — plans, sections, elevations, details, schedules, drawing conventions, and presentation techniques.
* **Accessibility Standards (BP 344 / RA 7277 as amended by RA 9442)** — ramp slopes, door widths, accessible toilet requirements, parking stalls, signage, and tactile guidance systems.
* **Anthropometrics and Human Dimensions** — body dimensions, reach zones, clearances, and how human sclea drives spatial adequacy.
* **National Building Code Application (PD 1096)** — setbacks, floor area ratios, building height limits, occupancy classifications, and use and occupancy regulations.

## Why This Subject Is Uniquely Important

Most LEA subjects share Area A or B with three or four peers, diluting their individual weight. Architectural Design and Site Planning stands alone in Area C. This is not a coincidence — it reflects the PRC's understanding that architectural design synthesis is the defining skill of a licensed architect.

The practical consequence for your preparation: even if you achieve high scores across all of Area B, a weak performance in Architectural Design will significantly drag your total. Conversely, a strong Area C score provides a meaningful cushion.

<Callout>This is the most integrated subject in the LEA. Every other subject you study — structures, MEPF, materials, construction, planning, laws — contributes concepts that appear in Architectural Design questions. Treating it as a standalone subject is a strategic mistake. Treat it as the arena where everything else converges.</Callout>

## The NBCP Is Not the Only Code Here

Unlike Structural Conceptualization, which centers heavily on a single governing code, Architectural Design draws from a portfolio of Philippine legislation and standards:

* **PD 1096** — National Building Code of the Philippines (NBCP); occupancy classification, setbacks, height limits, floor area ratios, building permits.
* **BP 344** — Accessibility Law; design requirements for persons with disability in the built environment.
* **RA 7277 as amended by RA 9442** — Magna Carta for Persons with Disability, which strengthens and expands rights and privileges and reinforces accessibility obligations alongside BP 344.
* **RA 9514** — Revised Fire Code of the Philippines; egress requirements, fire ratings, sprinkler requirements, and related fire-protection provisions.
* **RA 9003** — Ecological Solid Waste Management Act of 2000, alongside DENR regulations covering environmental design considerations such as solid waste handling and materials recovery facilities.

<ExamTip>LEA questions on Architectural Design frequently cite code provisions without naming the code. "The minimum clear width of an accessible ramp is ___" is a BP 344 question. "The minimum number and size of accessible parking slots is ___" links BP 344 with RA 7277/RA 9442. "The maximum travel distance to an exit in a non-sprinklered building is ___" is an RA 9514/NBCP question. Knowing which code governs which design decision is as important as knowing the number itself.</ExamTip>

## Recommended Study Sequence

The thirteen subtopics of Architectural Design are not equal in complexity or in how they connect to each other. The following sequence builds your understanding progressively and mirrors how real projects unfold.

### Phase 1 — The Design Backbone (Weeks 1–2)

Start with Design Process and Methodology. This gives you the conceptual framework into which everything else fits: understand programming (translating client needs into spatial requirements), design concept generation, and the iterative nature of architectural design.

Then cover Anthropometrics and Human Dimensions. Room sizes, door widths, counter heights, clearances — these numbers underpin every building type you will study; learn them as internalized knowledge, not as a last-minute lookup table.

### Phase 2 — Site and Environment (Weeks 3–4)

Cover Site Planning and Analysis and Environmental Design together. The site is always the first design constraint, and environmental response is how the building relates to its site over time.

Solar angles in the Philippines, prevailing northeast and southwest monsoon winds, and passive cooling strategies for a tropical climate are baseline design moves that separate generic buildings from contextually responsive ones. Integrate these with zoning, easements, and buildable envelope limits as defined in PD 1096 and related local ordinances.

### Phase 3 — Building Types (Weeks 5–7)

Work through Residential, Commercial/Mixed-Use, Institutional, and Industrial/Transportation design in sequence. For each building type, study:

* Primary functional requirements.
* Typical space relationships (adjacency diagrams).
* Critical dimensional standards.
* Code requirements specific to that occupancy (PD 1096, BP 220, specialized agency standards).

<Tip>The most tested building types in the Philippine LEA are residential (including BP 220 Economic and Socialized Housing), educational (schools and universities), and healthcare (hospitals, clinics). Invest proportionally more time in these three before moving to industrial and transportation.</Tip>

### Phase 4 — Codes and Safety (Weeks 8–9)

Fire Safety and Egress, Accessibility Standards, Parking Design, and the National Building Code (PD 1096) are often studied last but should not be rushed. These subtopics generate a high volume of specific, testable questions.

A solid week of focused code study — with practice questions using scenarios — can meaningfully improve your score and sharpen your instincts on what is compliant versus merely plausible.

### Phase 5 — Graphic Communication (Week 10)

Graphic Presentation and Architectural Drawings is sometimes dismissed as straightforward. It is not.

The LEA tests your ability to read and interpret drawings — plans, sections, elevations, and details — and to understand what graphic conventions communicate about structure, materials, and systems. Practice reading unfamiliar drawing sets, not just producing your own plates.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

* **Ignoring code application in favor of design theory.** Many architecture graduates find the design process content intuitive — they have been doing it for five years. What trips them up is precise code application: BP 344 ramp slopes, PD 1096 setbacks, RA 9514 travel distances, and parking ratios. These are specific, tested, and not forgiving of approximation.
* **Weak graphic communication.** The ability to read and interpret architectural drawings is tested directly, especially in situation-type questions that present partial plans or sections. Students who have worked primarily on 3D-modeling platforms and rarely read traditional drawing sets sometimes struggle with plan-reading questions; spend time with actual working drawing sets, not just perspectives and renders.
* **Studying building types without studying the codes that govern them.** Residential design is inseparable from BP 220 and PD 1096; hospital design is inseparable from DOH (Department of Health) guidelines; schools are inseparable from DepEd (Department of Education) standards. Study the building type and its governing requirements together.
* **Neglecting site planning.** Site Planning and Analysis feels like a soft subtopic, but it generates substantial exam content — setbacks, easements, solar orientation, drainage, and buildable area calculations. Do not treat it as a brief introductory section.

<Warn>Do not skip the parking design subtopic because it seems mundane. Philippine LEA questions on parking are highly specific: minimum stall dimensions (2.5 m × 5.0 m standard, 3.7 m × 5.0 m accessible), drive aisle widths for 90-degree parking (6.0 m minimum for two-way), ramp slopes, and the formula for computing required parking slots by building use under PD 1096. These are reliable exam questions that reward preparation.</Warn>
