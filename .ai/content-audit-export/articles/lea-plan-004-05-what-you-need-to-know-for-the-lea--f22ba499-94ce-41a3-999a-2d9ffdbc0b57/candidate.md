## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal
Apply lifecycle cost analysis to site elements, specify durable materials and maintainable systems, develop maintenance schedules for landscapes and infrastructure, and evaluate the long-term cost implications of design decisions.

Maintenance considerations address how a site's physical elements—landscapes, utilities, buildings, and public spaces—are kept functional and safe over time. This is a distinct topic from ecological management (004-04) and focuses on the lifecycle and durability of constructed elements. Maintenance is the lowest-frequency tested topic in the site planning curriculum, but it is still part of the LEA.

### Lifecycle Cost Analysis

A site element's true cost is not its initial capital cost but its total cost over its useful life, including maintenance and eventual replacement.

**Lifecycle Cost Formula**

Total Cost = Initial Capital Cost + (Annual Maintenance Cost × Useful Life in Years) + End-of-Life Replacement Cost

**Example: Paving Material Choice**

| Paving Material | Initial Cost per sq.m. | Annual Maintenance | Useful Life | Total 20-Year Cost |
|-----------------|------------------------|-------------------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Concrete** | ₱1,200 | ₱30/yr (sealing, minor repair) | 20 years | 1,200 + (600) + 0 = ₱1,800 |
| **Asphalt** | ₱800 | ₱100/yr (patching, resealing) | 10 years, then replace | 800 + (2,000) + 800 = ₱3,600 |
| **Permeable pavers** | ₱2,500 | ₱150/yr (weed control, sand refill) | 15 years, then replace | 2,500 + (3,000) + 2,500 = ₱8,000 |

Concrete has higher initial cost but lower lifecycle cost. Asphalt is cheaper initially but requires more frequent replacement. Permeable pavers are ecologically better but expensive over time.

**Implications for Site Planning**

Choose durable, low-maintenance materials when possible. Trade off initial cost for long-term performance. Understand that "cheapest" is rarely the best decision over 20+ years.

### Durability and Material Selection

Material durability—how long a material lasts before deterioration—varies dramatically by climate, use, and maintenance.

**Philippine Climate Challenges**

1. **Humidity and salt spray** — Coastal areas corrode metal, damage concrete, and encourage mold growth
2. **Typhoon exposure** — High winds damage fixtures; rain and flooding stress drainage systems
3. **Heavy monsoon rainfall** — Intense rain erodes soil, overwhelms drainage, and causes flooding
4. **Tropical temperature swings** — Heat expansion and contraction stress materials; UV radiation degrades plastics and paint
5. **Biological growth** — Algae, mold, and fungi thrive in wet tropical climates; they stain and damage surfaces

**Material Durability Rankings** (in Philippine tropical climate)

| Element | Most Durable | Moderate Durability | Least Durable |
|---------|--------------|-------------------|---------------|
| **Paving** | Concrete (25+ yrs) | Interlocking pavers (15–20 yrs) | Asphalt (8–10 yrs) |
| **Structures** | Steel/reinforced concrete (50+ yrs) | Brick/stone (30+ yrs) | Wood (5–15 yrs without treatment) |
| **Fencing** | Concrete posts with chain-link (20+ yrs) | Wooden posts with wire (10–15 yrs) | Bamboo (5 yrs) |
| **Landscape edging** | Concrete curb (20+ yrs) | Metal edging (10–15 yrs) | Wooden edge (3–5 yrs) |
| **Drainage pipes** | PVC or concrete (25+ yrs) | Galvanized steel (15–20 yrs, coastal: 5–10 yrs) | Uncoated steel (3–5 yrs) |

**Design Principle: Choose for Durability**

In tropical climates, over-design slightly for durability. A concrete drainage system costs more upfront but saves maintenance and replacement costs over 20 years.

### Maintenance Access and Inspection

Elements that are difficult to access, inspect, or repair will be neglected.

**Design for Maintainability**

1. **Accessibility** — Valves, meters, and mechanical equipment should be accessible without removing landscape or obstacles
2. **Visibility** — Maintenance workers should be able to see the condition of surfaces, vegetation, and systems; hidden damage goes unrepaired
3. **Safety** — Maintenance workers should not need to climb, wade, or enter confined spaces to perform routine tasks
4. **Documentation** — Site maps showing utilities, maintenance schedules, and responsible parties prevent neglect due to confusion

**Common Maintenance Failures**

- **Buried utilities** — If a water valve is under soil or mulch, workers will not find it; it remains unmaintained
- **Confined spaces** — Narrow gaps under decks or dense vegetation hide drainage damage
- **Inaccessible mechanical systems** — Roof equipment or underground systems require specialized access; routine inspection is skipped
- **Unclear responsibility** — "Someone will maintain it" results in neglect

### Flood and Storm Resilience in Maintenance

Sites in flood-prone or typhoon-prone areas require materials and maintenance strategies that withstand these hazards.

**Flood-Resilient Maintenance Strategies**

1. **Use corrosion-resistant materials** — Stainless steel, coated copper, or plastic rather than uncoated steel
2. **Elevate mechanical systems** — Pumps, valves, and electrical equipment above the 25-year flood level
3. **Plan for rapid drainage** — Ensure drainage systems are sized and maintained for maximum rainfall rates
4. **Use flood-tolerant paving** — Permeable paving that allows infiltration rather than creating ponding
5. **Pre-position repair materials** — Stock sandbags, pumps, and cleaning supplies for post-flood cleanup

**Typhoon-Resilient Maintenance**

1. **Inspect and prune vegetation regularly** — Remove dead branches, weak trees, and hazardous growth that will fail in high winds
2. **Secure loose elements** — Signage, fixtures, and equipment should be bolted down
3. **Design for wind flow** — Avoid pockets where wind eddies and accelerates; design landscape to channel wind safely
4. **Use impact-resistant materials** — Avoid glass where possible; use reinforced materials in high-impact areas

### Utility Maintenance and Asset Management

Site infrastructure (water, power, sewage, storm drainage) requires organized maintenance.

**Asset Management Approach**

1. **Inventory assets** — Map all utilities, their age, material, capacity, and condition
2. **Establish maintenance schedule** — When to inspect, clean, repair, or replace each system
3. **Assign responsibility** — Who owns and maintains each system? (LGU, private developer, barangay, user cooperative)
4. **Budget maintenance** — Allocate annual funds proportional to asset age and deterioration rate
5. **Document maintenance** — Record all work performed; use records to predict failure and optimize replacement timing

**Example: Storm Drainage System**

- **Inventory:** 50 catch basins, 500 meters of PVC pipe, 3 outlet structures (concrete chambers)
- **Maintenance schedule:** Clean catch basins quarterly; inspect pipes after heavy typhoons; repair any blockages within 48 hours
- **Responsibility:** Barangay Public Works Department
- **Budget:** ₱50,000/year for cleaning, materials, and labor
- **Documentation:** Photo record of baseline; maintenance log updated monthly

### Deferred Maintenance and Deterioration

When maintenance is not performed, sites deteriorate rapidly. Deferred maintenance compounds over time, becoming expensive to address.

**Deterioration Rates** (approximations)

- **Unmaintained drainage** — Becomes blocked within 1–2 years; overflow damage occurs; repair costs 10× cleaning cost
- **Unpruned vegetation** — Weak branches fail in wind; tree becomes hazardous; removal costs ₱5,000–₱10,000 vs. annual pruning cost ₱500
- **Unrepaired paving** — Small cracks expand within 1–2 years; infiltration weakens base; failure spreads; reconstruction costs 3–5× timely repair
- **Neglected drainage systems** — Corrosion and clogging; system failure; emergency replacement costs 10× planned maintenance

**Prevention Strategy**

A modest annual maintenance budget (1–2% of initial capital cost) prevents expensive emergency repairs and system failure. The return on maintenance investment is high.

### Commissioning and Handover

When a site is constructed, commissioning (testing and verification) and handover (transfer to management) are critical to ensure long-term success.

**Commissioning Tasks**

1. **Test all systems** — Water supply, drainage, electrical, landscaping irrigation; ensure each works as designed
2. **Verify capacities** — Check that systems can handle design flows (storm water, wastewater, pedestrian traffic)
3. **Document baseline condition** — Photograph all elements; create as-built drawings
4. **Train maintenance staff** — Ensure operators understand how systems work and what to maintain

**Handover Process**

1. **Prepare operations manual** — Document all systems, maintenance procedures, emergency contacts, spare parts sources
2. **Transfer responsibility clearly** — In writing, specify who owns and maintains each system
3. **Establish monitoring schedule** — When and how often each system is inspected
4. **Secure maintenance funding** — Budget or revenue stream allocated before operations begin
5. **Transition period** — Ideally, developer/contractor provides support for first 6–12 months while operators gain experience

**Common Failure**: Handover without commissioning. Site is "finished" but systems are not tested or trained; problems discovered after occupancy become the operator's problem.

### Maintenance Planning Checklist

<Tip>When planning for long-term maintenance during site design:

1. Conduct lifecycle cost analysis for major material choices; favor durable materials over cheap initial cost
2. Select materials suited to Philippine tropical climate, humidity, rainfall, and typhoon exposure
3. Design sites for maintenance accessibility—valves, drains, and equipment should be reachable
4. Plan for flood and typhoon resilience; use flood-resistant materials and elevate critical systems
5. Establish clear asset inventory and maintenance responsibility before opening
6. Budget 1–2% of initial capital cost annually for routine maintenance
7. Create operations manual and train staff before handover
8. Document baseline condition with photos and as-built drawings
9. Establish monitoring schedule to catch problems before they become emergencies
10. Plan for commissioning and testing before site occupation

Maintenance planning begins in design; neglect at this stage leads to expensive failures later.</Tip>
