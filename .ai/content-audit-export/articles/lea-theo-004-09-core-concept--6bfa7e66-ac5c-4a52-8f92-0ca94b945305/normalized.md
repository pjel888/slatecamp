### Driving Forces for Natural Ventilation

Natural ventilation operates through two primary mechanisms: **pressure-driven flow** (wind) and **buoyancy-driven flow** (stack effect). Both are perpetual in the Philippine climate; their interaction determines achievable ventilation rates.

#### Wind-Driven Ventilation

**Wind pressure** creates pressure differentials across a building facade. On the windward side, wind stagnates and creates positive pressure; on the leeward side, wind accelerates and creates negative pressure (Venturi effect). This pressure difference drives air from high-pressure (windward) to low-pressure (leeward) zones.

**Wind speed variation with height** follows a power law: wind speed increases dramatically above ground level. A building's ventilation potential improves with height, elevation above surrounding terrain, and orientation perpendicular to prevailing wind.

For the Philippines:
- **Coronas Type I regions** (Western Luzon, Western Visayas): SW monsoon dominates May–Oct (3–5 m/s average; 8–12 m/s peak). NE trades Nov–Apr (2–3 m/s). **Wind availability is seasonal; mechanical backup needed in calm seasons.**

- **Coronas Type II regions** (Eastern Luzon, Eastern Visayas): SW monsoon in reverse brings heavy rain but lower wind speeds (2–3 m/s). NE monsoon more pronounced (4–6 m/s). **Wet/wind misalignment challenges ventilation design.**

- **Tropical urban areas** (Manila CBD, Cebu): Local terrain and building height variations reduce regional wind availability by 40–50%; roughness coefficient increases, amplifying friction losses.

#### Stack Effect (Buoyancy-Driven Flow)

**Stack effect** leverages temperature difference between interior and exterior air. Hot interior air is less dense; it rises and exits through high-level openings. This pressure difference draws cooler exterior air through low-level openings.

**Achievable pressure difference**:
ΔP = ρg × h × (ΔT / T_ext)

Where:
- ρ = air density (1.2 kg/m³ at sea level)
- g = gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/s²)
- h = vertical distance between inlet and outlet (meters)
- ΔT = temperature difference (exterior to interior)
- T_ext = exterior absolute temperature (Kelvin)

**Philippine example** (Manila, Coronas Type I):
- h = 4 m (ground floor to roof opening)
- ΔT = 2.5 K (exterior 35°C, interior 37.5°C—achievable with passive cooling)
- T_ext = 308 K

ΔP = 1.2 × 9.81 × 4 × (2.5 / 308) = **0.38 Pa**

### Cross-Ventilation Strategy

**Cross-ventilation** occurs when wind pressure drives air through a building from windward to leeward openings. This is the most effective natural ventilation mode in tropical buildings.

**Requirements for effective cross-ventilation**:
1. **Windward opening** of sufficient size (≥20% of floor area facade)
2. **Leeward opening** of equal or greater size (prevents backflow)
3. **Clear flow path** between openings (minimal partitions)
4. **Orientation perpendicular** to prevailing wind direction (90° ideally; 60–90° acceptable)

**Ventilation effectiveness** is measured as **ventilation efficiency** (VE):
VE = Volume of fresh air reaching breathing zone / Total ventilation rate

In the Philippines:
- **Well-designed cross-ventilation**: VE = 0.7–0.85 (70–85% of air changes reach occupied spaces)
- **Poorly configured layouts**: VE = 0.3–0.5 (high air volume but little occupant benefit)

#### Tropical Optimization

For **Coronas Type I climates** with seasonal wind reversal, buildings must accommodate bidirectional flow:
- Windows and doors positioned at multiple compass orientations (not just two)
- Interior doors between rooms remain open (enables multi-zone cross-ventilation)
- Stack effect operates regardless of wind direction, providing year-round backup

The **bahay kubo exemplifies optimal cross-ventilation**:
- Large windows on all four sides (enables flow from any wind direction)
- Open floor plan (minimal partitions, clear flow path)
- Elevated on stilts (enhances stack effect; ground-level air cools before entering)
- Low interior surface area (less air-side friction loss)

### Stack Effect and Vertical Integration

**Vertical stack effect** becomes critical during day-night transitions and calm periods. The mechanism:

1. **Daytime heating**: Interior air warms above exterior temperature by 2–5 K
2. **Rising hot air**: Warm air rises through interior vertical shafts (stairwells, atria)
3. **Roof-level exhaust**: Hot air escapes through high-level openings (roof monitors, clerestories)
4. **Low-level makeup air**: Cooler ground-level air enters through windows/doors

**Design strategy**: Maximize vertical height difference between intake and exhaust. A 6-meter vertical shaft (ground floor to roof) produces 50% greater pressure differential than a 3-meter shaft—critical during low-wind periods.

#### Atrium Stack Effect

Large atriums amplify stack effect by concentrating vertical rise. In a Philippine hospital atrium (h = 20 m):
- ΔT = 3 K achievable in dry climate (Coronas Type I)
- ΔP = 1.2 × 9.81 × 20 × (3 / 308) = **0.23 Pa per 20 m height**

This pressure is modest but cumulative; in a 40-meter atrium with 5 K ΔT, ΔP can exceed 0.5 Pa, driving 6–8 ACH without fans.

### Monsoon Wind and Orientation Conflict

The Philippines faces a critical design paradox: **optimal solar orientation (north-south elongation) conflicts with optimal wind orientation** in many Coronas zones.

- **Coronas Type I**: SW monsoon (May–Oct) flow is primarily from 225°. Ideal window orientation is perpendicular (45° azimuth) for maximum cross-ventilation, but this conflicts with north-south solar optimization.

**Solution**: Prioritize solar orientation; provide **supplementary stack effect and wind-catcher devices** to compensate for non-ideal wind exposure. Secondary facades (east-west) become critical ventilation inlets despite solar liability.

### Wind Catcher (Windtower) Technology

A **wind catcher** (or windtower) is a passive ventilation device that captures air at height and channels it down into a building, exploiting both wind pressure and the Venturi effect.

**How it works**:
1. Upwind scoop captures air at 3–8 meter height (above street-level turbulence)
2. Air descends through a vertical shaft (stack effect amplification)
3. Downwind side creates suction (Venturi effect acceleration)
4. Combined effects drive air into building at rates 2–3× greater than window-level cross-ventilation

**Effectiveness in Philippine tropical climates**:
- **Coronas Type I**: Excellent during monsoon season (May–Oct); less effective in calm dry season
- **Coronas Type II**: Year-round low-wind conditions limit effectiveness; requires Venturi-effect enhancement
- **Coronas IV**: Modest but reliable year-round winds justify installation

**Design considerations**:
- Minimum height: 5 meters above surrounding buildings
- Orientation: Windward side opens toward prevailing wind (±45° tolerance)
- Duct cross-section: 0.5–1.0 m² typical for residential; larger for commercial
- Thermal mass: Metal or ceramic ducts should be insulated to prevent heating incoming air

**Tropical adaptation**: Wind catchers are traditionally Middle Eastern (Iran, Gulf states), designed for arid heat-transfer. Philippine adaptation adds **humidification chambers** or **evaporative cooling pads** to cool incoming air before interior delivery.

### Venturi Effect and Roof Geometry

The **Venturi effect** describes how fluid accelerates as it passes through a constricted area, creating local pressure drop. Architects use this principle in roof geometry to enhance exhaust flow.

A **Venturi-shaped roof** (widest at bottom, narrowest at top) creates a constriction that:
1. Accelerates air exiting the building
2. Creates suction that pulls interior air upward
3. Enhances stack effect, complementing natural buoyancy

Research in tropical climates shows:
- Venturi roofs increase ventilation rates by 15–25% compared to flat roofs
- Effectiveness peaks when roof angle = 45–60° (minimum constriction)
- In calm wind conditions (&lt;1 m/s), Venturi effect accounts for only 10–15% additional flow; wind-driven flow dominates under favorable conditions

### Integration with Thermal Comfort Standards

**ASHRAE 55 Adaptive Comfort Model** recognizes that naturally ventilated buildings can maintain occupant comfort at higher indoor temperatures (26–29°C) than fully air-conditioned spaces (23–25°C) because:
1. Occupants in naturally ventilated spaces adapt psychologically and physiologically
2. Variable air movement and thermal conditions increase engagement
3. Personal control (opening windows, adjusting clothing) enhances tolerance

For **Philippine Coronas Type I climates** (dry season peak 42–45°C exterior):
- Air-conditioned buildings: 24–25°C indoor setpoint requires 8–12 kW/ton cooling
- Naturally ventilated buildings: 27–28°C interior (achievable with 6–8 ACH) allows ASHRAE 55 compliance while reducing cooling energy by 60–80%

This aligns with Philippine Green Building Code emphasis on **climate-responsive design** and BERDE certification for natural ventilation-focused buildings.

## Key Terms

**Cross-Ventilation** — Wind-driven airflow through a building from windward to leeward openings; dominates in low-latitude tropical zones with consistent seasonal winds.

**Stack Effect** — Buoyancy-driven airflow created by temperature differential between interior and exterior; warm air rises and exits high-level openings, drawing makeup air at low level.

**Ventilation Efficiency (VE)** — Ratio of fresh air reaching occupied breathing zone to total ventilation rate; ranges 0.3–0.85 depending on layout quality.

**Air Changes Per Hour (ACH)** — Volume of air circulating through a space per hour, expressed as multiple of room volume; 6–8 ACH typical for tropical natural ventilation.

**Wind Pressure Coefficient (Cp)** — Dimensionless measure of wind pressure on a facade; positive on windward side (pressure), negative on leeward side (suction).

**Venturi Effect** — Fluid acceleration through constricted area, creating local pressure drop; used in roof geometry to enhance exhaust flow.

**Wind Catcher (Windtower)** — Passive ventilation device that captures above-roofline wind and channels it into building; enhances pressure differential driving indoor airflow.

**Pressure Differential (ΔP)** — Net pressure forcing air through building (Pa); created by wind pressure or stack effect; required for any airflow rate.

**Monsoon Wind Dominance** — Coronas Type I regions experience 6-month seasonal wind reversal (SW May–Oct, NE Nov–Apr); building orientation must accommodate bidirectional flow.

**Thermal Buoyancy** — Density difference between warm interior and cool exterior air; creates gravitational force driving upward (stack) flow.

**Coronas Type Ventilation Adaptation** — Natural ventilation strategy modified for Type I (seasonal wind compensation), Type II (year-round weak wind + stack effect), Type III/IV (moderate wind + thermal buoyancy blend).
