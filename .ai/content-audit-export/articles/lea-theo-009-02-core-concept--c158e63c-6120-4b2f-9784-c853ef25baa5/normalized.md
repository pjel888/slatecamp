## Board Exam Quick Check

When an item mentions a bubble diagram, first ask whether it is still showing program relationships or whether it has crossed into a floor plan. The source-safe test is simple: relationship logic belongs to programming; walls, dimensions, finishes, and construction choices belong to later design documentation.

## What You Need to Know for the LEA

The local Design Process source does not support memorized symbol systems for bubble diagrams. What it does support is the programming use of diagrams and words to express relationships, concepts, and translation guidelines before design is fixed.

A source-safe bubble diagram is therefore treated as a program relationship diagram: it helps communicate which spaces, users, activities, or concepts must relate to each other. It should not be confused with a final plan.

## Learning Goal

- Use diagrammatic programming tools to express relationships without over-specifying form.
- Identify when a diagram remains a program concept and when it becomes a design plan.
- Connect relationship diagrams to translation guidelines and later design decisions.

## Core Concept

The Design Process source defines a concept as an ideal set of relationships among elements under the architect's control, including form, material, texture, color, and adjacency. It also states that a concept statement is made up of a single diagram and a few words.

For LEA purposes, this means the safe role of a bubble diagram is to communicate relationship logic. It can show that certain functions should be near, separated, grouped, or connected. It should remain abstract enough that multiple designs could satisfy it.

Translation guidelines are the next step. The same source shows guidelines that convert goals and performance requirements into clearer design constraints, such as entrance spacing, corridor width, door relationships, kiosk area, lab ratios, floor-level relationships, and exhibit allocation. These are not random design decorations; they come from performance requirements.

## Key Facts and Testable Items

| Exam cue | Source-safe answer |
| --- | --- |
| Diagram plus a few words | A concept statement in the checked Design Process source. |
| Adjacency | A relationship among elements under the architect's control. |
| Bubble diagram with walls and fixed room dimensions | Likely no longer only a programming diagram. |
| Translation guideline | A performance or quality standard derived from requirements. |
| RA 9266 IRR space planning | Professional scope that supports programming and layouting decisions. |

## How It's Tested on the LEA

You may be shown a diagram and asked whether it is a programming tool or a design plan. If it only communicates relationships and concepts, treat it as programming. If it fixes rooms, walls, materials, or construction details, it has moved past programming.

You may also be asked what happens after a relationship diagram is accepted. The source-safe answer is not immediate construction. The diagram must be translated into guidelines and then into design decisions that satisfy the program.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating every bubble diagram as a scaled floor plan.
- Adding final wall layouts to a program diagram too early.
- Memorizing unsupported E/D/X notations as if they were in the local source.
- Forgetting that concepts express relationships, not final construction details.

## Key Terms

**Program Relationship Diagram** - A source-safe way to describe a bubble diagram when it communicates relationships among program elements before design is fixed.

**Concept Statement** - A diagram and a few words expressing relationships among controlled design elements.

**Adjacency** - A relationship between spaces or functions considered during concept and programming work.

**Translation Guideline** - A program-derived standard that makes a goal or performance requirement actionable for later design.
