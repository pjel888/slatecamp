## The Architect's Authority During Construction — Powers, Limits, and the Line With the Contractor
## Learning Goal

After reading this article, you should be able to:

- Identify the scope and limits of the Architect's authority during construction as defined by the Contract Documents and Doc 301.
- Distinguish between the Architect's right to issue directives (remove defective work, require testing) and the Contractor's control over means and methods.
- Explain the Architect's authority to issue Change Orders and the thresholds requiring Owner authorization.
- Apply the limits on Architect authority to LEA scenarios involving disputes over work methods, payment certification, and contract interpretation.

The Architect's authority during construction is defined and limited by the Contract Documents and Doc 301. The Architect cannot modify the Contractor's scope, methods, or obligations beyond what the contract permits.

### Scope of Architect's Authority

The Architect's authority derives exclusively from the Contract Documents, primarily Doc 301 and the Agreement. The Architect may issue written directives requiring the Contractor to remove defective work, correct non-conforming materials or equipment, and remedy deviations from specifications or drawings. The Architect may request inspection and testing of materials and equipment. The Architect issues Certificates of Payment, certifying that work completed conforms to the contract and that the Contractor is entitled to progress payment (less retainage).

The Architect may also issue Change Orders authorizing changes to the work, adjusting the Contract Price and Completion Time. However, the Architect's authority to order changes is limited; the Architect cannot unilaterally order changes that exceed a specified threshold (typically 25% of the original contract price or a single line item value) without Owner authorization. Changes within the threshold may be issued by the Architect as a directed change, with the Contractor obligated to proceed despite disagreement over cost or feasibility, subject to a later claim for adjustment.

### Limits on Architect Authority

The Architect does not have authority to direct the Contractor's work methods, labor practices, equipment, or sequencing unless specified in the Contract Documents. The Contractor retains control over means and methods, provided the end result complies with the specifications and drawings. If the Architect observes an unsafe work method, the Architect may require correction for safety reasons but does not direct how the Contractor accomplishes the work.

The Architect does not have authority to extend the Completion Time or adjust the Contract Price unilaterally. These adjustments require the Owner's approval and execution of a Change Order. The Architect may recommend a time extension or price adjustment to the Owner but cannot bind the Owner to such adjustment. If the Architect exceeds this authority and purports to grant a time extension or price increase without Owner authorization, the commitment is not binding on the Owner.

### Inspection and Certification

The Architect conducts inspections to verify that completed work conforms to the Contract Documents. These inspections are not supervision of daily construction activities but periodic examination of substantive progress and quality. The Architect does not provide full-time inspection and does not assume responsibility for the Contractor's quality control, safety measures, or schedule adherence. The Contractor remains responsible for all aspects of work execution.

Upon inspection, if the Architect discovers defective or non-conforming work, the Architect issues a written directive requiring correction. The Contractor must comply with the directive without additional compensation unless a Change Order is issued. If the Architect fails to discover defective work during inspection, this does not constitute approval or waiver of the defect; discovery and correction may be required even months later if the defect is subsequently identified.

The Architect certifies progress payments by issuing a Certificate of Payment, confirming that work completed meets the contract requirements and that payment is due to the Contractor. This certification does not waive the Contractor's obligation to correct latent defects discovered later. The certification is based on visible, completed work; hidden or covered work is conditionally approved, subject to rejection if subsequent inspection reveals non-conformity.

### Change Order Authority and Limitations

The Architect may issue a Change Order for changes ordered by the Owner or for changes necessitated by subsurface conditions, changes in building codes, or other conditions discovered during construction. The Architect's authority to issue a Change Order is limited to the threshold established in the Contract (typically 25% of the original contract price or a single line item). Changes within this threshold may be issued as directed changes; changes exceeding the threshold require Owner authorization before issuance.

The value of a Change Order is determined by the method specified in the Contract Documents or by agreement between Owner and Contractor. Methods include agreed lump sum, unit prices from the original Contract, actual direct cost plus overhead and profit (typically 15%), or other method. If the parties cannot agree on value within a specified period, the Architect may recommend a value to the Owner, but the Owner's decision is final. The Contractor must proceed with the change even if the parties dispute the value, with adjustment determined later.

### Interaction With the Contractor's Representative

The Architect communicates with the Contractor's Project Manager or designated representative and does not direct individual workers or supervise labor. All directives, change orders, and correspondence are issued to the Contractor through the official representative. The Architect does not have authority to hire, fire, or discipline the Contractor's workers. If the Architect observes an incompetent or unsuitable worker, the Architect notifies the Contractor and requests removal; the Contractor may comply or object, and the Owner makes the final decision.

The Architect does not direct the sequence of work unless the Contract Documents specify a particular sequence. If the Contractor's proposed sequence creates conflicts or delays, the Architect may recommend resequencing to the Contractor and Owner but does not have unilateral authority to impose a different sequence. If sequence changes are necessary, they are documented through a Change Order adjusting Contract Price and Completion Time if appropriate.

### Clarification and Interpretation Authority

When the Contractor requests clarification of ambiguous contract provisions, the Architect issues an interpretation or directive. The interpretation is binding on the Contractor, who must proceed in accordance with the Architect's direction. However, if the interpretation imposes unexpected cost or delay, the Contractor may file a claim for adjustment of Contract Price or Completion Time, which is evaluated after contract completion. This structure allows the work to proceed despite disagreement, with financial resolution deferred.

The Architect does not have authority to waive contract requirements or excuse the Contractor's performance obligations. If the Contractor requests waiver of a specification requirement (e.g., using a substitute material or eliminating a procedure), the Architect may recommend the waiver to the Owner but cannot grant it unilaterally. The Owner's written authorization is required for any waiver or modification of contract requirements.

### Distinction Between Authority and Responsibility

The Architect's authority to direct removal of defective work does not eliminate the Architect's responsibility for design defects. If the defect is caused by the Architect's incomplete or incorrect specifications (e.g., omitting a required component or specifying incompatible materials), the Architect may direct the Contractor to correct the defect, but the Architect remains liable to the Owner for the design error. The Contractor's correction of a design defect does not transfer liability to the Contractor; the Contractor may seek indemnification from the Architect for the cost of correction if the defect is design-caused.

Similarly, the Architect's authority to interpret the contract does not extend to changing the contract scope or requirements. Interpretations clarify existing requirements; they do not create new requirements or obligations. If the Owner desires to change the contract, a formal Change Order must be issued; an interpretation cannot substitute for a Change Order.
