## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal

Identify the five testable markers of Baroque architecture — curved walls, hidden light sources, ornamented plans, trompe-l'oeil painting, sculptural facades — and recognize key works (St. Peter's, San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane, Versailles) by their dynamic spatial and visual effects.
## Core Concept

**Baroque architecture** (early 17th–18th centuries) emerged from the Counter-Reformation—the Catholic Church's response to Protestant challenge. Baroque churches aim to inspire awe, emotion, and spiritual surrender through theatrical light, dramatic curves, ornamental abundance, and illusionistic spatial effects. The vocabulary includes:
- **Curved and undulating walls** replacing Renaissance rational geometry
- **Complex spatial interlocking** of volumes (ovals, circles, ellipses)
- **Dramatic light manipulation** through concealed windows, reflective surfaces, and stained glass
- **Ornamental exuberance** with sculpture, gilt, marble, and decorative moldings
- **Theatrical illusionism** blurring boundaries between real space and painted/sculpted illusion

**Gian Lorenzo Bernini** (1598–1680) is Baroque's supreme architect and sculptor. His **Sant'Andrea al Quirinlea** (1658–1670, Rome) exemplifies Baroque dynamism: an oval church with undulating walls, a concave portico, dramatic light pouring from a hidden window behind the altarpiece, and sculptural decoration emphasizing movement and emotion. His **Baldachin** in **St. Peter's Basilica** (1623–1634, Rome) uses Solomonic twisted columns, gilding, and dynamic curves to create a throne of spiritual authority.

**Francesco Borromini** (1599–1667) was Bernini's rival, favoring geometry and rational complexity over sculptural drama. His **San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane** (begun 1638, consecrated 1646, facade ~1677, Rome) is impossibly small yet spatially complex: an oval nave with a coffered dome creating illusionistic height, curved walls, and geometric precision so extreme that the space feels larger than its actual dimensions. Borromini's influence emphasizes the **geometric skeleton** underlying Baroque curves.

**Baroque regional variations:**
- **Italian Baroque**: Churches and civic architecture centered on Rome; sculptural, dramatic light
- **Iberian Baroque**: Facade emphasis with elaborate surface ornament (Churrigueresque style, Spain)
- **Northern European Baroque**: In Austria, Bohemia, and Southern Germany, church interiors become overwhelming white-and-gold stuccoed spaces with illusionistic ceiling frescoes (Vierzehnheiligen, Bavaria)
- **French Baroque**: More restrained; aligned with Classical order; Versailles as political rather than spiritual architecture

**Church of the Gesù** (begun 1568, consecrated 1584, Rome, **Giacomo Barozzi da Vignola** and **Giacomo della Porta**) is the influential proto-Baroque prototype for Jesuit churches. Its single wide nave with side chapels prioritizes preaching and altar visibility, establishing a model for Counter-Reformation churches worldwide.

**Versailles** (begun 1661, **Louis XIV**, architects **Louis Le Vau** [initial expansion from 1661] and **Jules Hardouin-Mansart** [expanded from 1678, including the Hall of Mirrors], landscape **André Le Nôtre**) represents Baroque in service of absolutist power. The palace sprawls across vast gardens, employs mirrors and gilt to create overwhelming gilded interiors (Hall of Mirrors), and uses rational Baroque (geometry + grandeur) to assert the Sun King's divine authority. Versailles is Baroque as *political theater*.

**Rococo** (1730–1760s) emerged in France as a reaction against Baroque heaviness. The term derives from French *rocaille* (shell-like ornamentation). Rococo aesthetics emphasize:
- **Asymmetry and irregularity** replacing Baroque symmetry
- **Pastel palettes** (plea blues, pinks, creams) replacing gilding and dark drama
- **Lightness and delicacy** rather than weight and spectacle
- **Intimate interior decoration** (boiseries, mirrors, stucco) replacing monumental churches
- **Playfulness and pleasure** (salons, hunting lodges, pleasure pavilions) rather than Counter-Reformation intensity
- **Japanese and Chinese motifs** reflecting Rococo's fascination with exoticism

Rococo is fundamentally a domestic and aristocratic *interior style*, not a structural system. Its influence appears primarily in palace interiors, salons, and small pavilions—**Wieskirche** (Bavaria, 1745–1754) being a rare full church expression with white-and-gold stuccoed walls, illusionistic ceiling frescoes, and Rococo lightness.

## Key Terms

- **Baroque**: An architectural and decorative style (early 17th–18th c.) emphasizing curves, drama, light manipulation, ornamental abundance, and illusionistic spatial effects; emerged from Counter-Reformation
- **Oval/elliptical church**: A church with an oval or elliptical (non-circular) plan, common in Baroque for spatial complexity
- **Illusionism**: Architectural or sculptural devices creating perceptual distortion (ceiling frescoes appearing as sky, spaces appearing larger/higher than they are)
- **Baldachin**: A canopy over an altar or throne; Bernini's at St. Peter's uses twisted Solomonic columns and gilt
- **Solomonic column**: A twisted or spiral column, derived from the Temple of Solomon; used dramatically in Baroque
- **Rococo**: A decorative style (1730–1760s) emphasizing asymmetry, pastel palettes, lightness, and intimate interiors; reaction against Baroque heaviness
- **Boiserie**: Elaborate wood paneling and carved decoration on interior walls; common in Rococo
- **Rocaille**: Shell-like or wave-like ornamental motif; basis for term "Rococo"
- **Stucco**: Plaster or stucco work used for ornamental interior surfaces and ceiling illusions
- **Frescoed ceiling**: A painted ceiling technique creating illusionistic spatial effects; common in Northern Baroque
