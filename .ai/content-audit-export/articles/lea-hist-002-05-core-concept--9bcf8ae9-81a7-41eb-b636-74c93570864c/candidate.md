# Baroque Architecture

## LEA Point
Baroque architecture turns classical vocabulary into movement, theater, and persuasion. For the LEA, identify it by curved surfaces, dramatic light, sculptural integration, spatial surprise, and interiors designed to move the viewer emotionally.

## Source Anchors
In church architecture, Baroque design often supports Counter-Reformation communication: clear focus on the altar, emotionally charged imagery, and architecture that draws the worshipper into a staged sacred experience.

Bernini is the safest anchor for theatrical and sculptural Baroque. Vatican News source-maps the St. Peter Baldacchino as Bernini's monumental work over the papal altar, with twisted columns, bronze, drapery logic, and a scale meant to hold the vast basilica space.

Borromini is the anchor for geometric complexity and spatial compression. Use him for San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane, where a small site becomes visually active through curved walls, an oval logic, and a dome that intensifies the perception of space.

The Church of the Gesu is the proto-Baroque Jesuit anchor: a wide nave, side chapels, and clear altar focus support preaching, visibility, and directed attention.

Versailles is the political Baroque anchor. UNESCO source-maps the palace and park as an exceptional royal complex. In exam logic, Versailles uses order, gardens, mirrors, axis, and gilding for court power rather than for church persuasion.

Rococo is the later lightened reaction: more intimate, aristocratic, asymmetrical, delicate, and playful. It is not simply bad decoration; it reflects a different social setting and aesthetic purpose.

## Exam Pattern
Baroque questions often compare Bernini and Borromini. Bernini tends toward sculptural drama, emotional clarity, and theatrical focus; Borromini tends toward geometric invention, elastic surfaces, and spatial ambiguity.

Another common pattern separates Baroque from Renaissance. Renaissance order is calm and rational; Baroque order is activated to persuade, move, and overwhelm.

## Guardrails
Do not describe Rococo as chronologically before Baroque. Do not use the same answer for Versailles and Counter-Reformation churches: both can be Baroque, but their purposes differ.

## Real World Scenario
In an image item, a twisted-column canopy over a major altar points to Bernini's Baldacchino. An oval, compressed Roman church with curved wall movement points toward Borromini. A vast palace-garden axis used to stage monarchy points toward Versailles.
