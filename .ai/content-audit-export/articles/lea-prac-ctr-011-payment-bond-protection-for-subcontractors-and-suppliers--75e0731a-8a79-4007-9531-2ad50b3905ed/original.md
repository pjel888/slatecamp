## Payment Bond — Protection for Subcontractors and Suppliers


The payment bond protects subcontractors and material suppliers by guaranteeing they will receive payment for work performed and materials supplied, even if the contractor becomes insolvent.


### Purpose and Function of Payment Bonds

A payment bond is security furnished by the contractor and surety to guarantee that all obligations arising from the contract will be paid. Unlike the performance bond which protects the owner against incomplete work, the payment bond protects subcontractors, suppliers, and laborers against non-payment. It creates a safety net ensuring that even if the contractor defaults financially, third parties who provide labor and materials are protected.

The payment bond operates as a two-party guarantee involving the obligee (owner) and the surety, with beneficiary protection extending to subcontractors and suppliers. When the contractor fails to pay subcontractors or suppliers as required, these parties can file claims directly against the surety. The surety then becomes obligated to pay the legitimate claims, effectively stepping into the contractor's payment obligation.

### Typical Amount and Standard Percentage

Payment bonds are typically required in amounts equal to the full contract price or a specified percentage thereof, much like performance bonds. Standard practice requires payment bonds at 50% to 100% of the contract value, with 100% being the most common requirement. The contract documents establish the exact percentage before bidding to ensure all parties understand the surety's liability exposure.

The bond amount should remain adequate to cover all potential payment obligations. This includes not only the contractor's fee and direct material and labor costs, but also all subcontractor and supplier costs that accumulate throughout the project. If the contract includes change orders that increase the scope and cost, the payment bond amount may need adjustment to maintain protective coverage.

### Protection for Subcontractors and Suppliers

Subcontractors and suppliers benefit significantly from payment bond protection. If the contractor fails to pay for work performed or materials delivered, these third parties can file claims directly with the surety without being parties to the original contract. This bypasses the need for the subcontractor or supplier to pursue collection action against an insolvent contractor.

To claim against the payment bond, the claimant typically must follow specific procedures outlined in the bond documents or applicable law. Claimants must provide notice to the surety within a specified timeframe—often 60 to 90 days from last furnishing of labor or materials—and present evidence of the unpaid obligation. The surety then investigates the claim and determines whether payment is due based on the bond terms and the evidence provided.

### Documentation and Payment Bond Claims

The owner and architect should maintain clear records of all work performed and materials supplied. When the contractor fails to make payment, documentation becomes crucial for subcontractors and suppliers presenting claims to the surety. The architect's certificates of payment and project records can support claimants' evidence of work completion and rightful entitlement to payment.

The surety's liability under the payment bond is limited to the bond amount. If total valid claims exceed the bond amount, the surety must distribute available funds proportionally among the claimants. The surety is not liable for amounts beyond the bond limit. This cap protects the surety but also underscores why the payment bond amount should be set at an adequate level to cover anticipated costs.

### Distinction Between Payment and Performance Bonds

While both payment and performance bonds may be required in the same contract, they serve distinct purposes. The performance bond guarantees work completion and quality; the payment bond guarantees payment of obligations. A contractor may breach one bond without breaching the other. The owner may call the performance bond for failure to complete work while subcontractors simultaneously file payment bond claims for non-payment, creating parallel remedies for different types of default.
