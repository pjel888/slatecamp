## Design-Build by Administration vs. Design-Build by Guaranteed Maximum Cost: The Critical Distinction

## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- Distinguish design-build by administration from design-build by guaranteed maximum cost.
- Explain how the architect's role differs in each design-build variant.
- Identify the risk allocation differences between the two methods.
- Apply design-build variant selection to project scenarios.

|---
### Design-Build by Administration

In Design-Build by Administration, the architect provides comprehensive services including all regular design phases and construction management. The architect organizes and hires construction personnel, negotiates with contractors, procures materials and equipment, maintains accounts, and manages all business transactions related to project construction.

This model places responsibility for technical excellence and professional coordination entirely on the architect.

Design-Build by Administration works well when project scope and requirements are relatively clear and when the architect and client share a collaborative relationship based on mutual trust. The client benefits from the architect's direct involvement in construction decisions and value management, but assumes cost risk inherent in actual construction expenditures.

### Design-Build by Guaranteed Maximum Cost

Design-Build by Guaranteed Maximum Cost (GMC) adds a critical contractual element: the architect provides a guaranteed maximum cost estimate for the project. The client receives cost certainty knowing that the project construction cost will not exceed this maximum by more than ten percent.

The guaranteed maximum structure places financial risk on the architect, incentivizing efficient design and constructive cost management. When actual costs remain below the estimated maximum, _savings are divided equally_ between client and architect. This arrangement aligns both parties' interests toward cost efficiency and value optimization.

### Cost Control Mechanisms

In the Guaranteed Maximum Cost model, the architect bears liability for cost overruns. However, liability limitations apply. The architect is liable for overruns only up to the amount of the administration fee. Additional costs beyond this threshold are not the architect's responsibility.

Legitimate exceptions to the cost guarantee exist. Change orders (CVOs) initiated by the client, extra work orders (EWOs), and legitimate cost escalation for materials or labor are borne by the client. Substantial escalation must be evidenced by data from nationally recognized agencies such as NEDA. Any costs resulting from causes not attributable to the architect's fault remain the client's responsibility.

### Risk Allocation and Professional Responsibility

The distinction in risk allocation between these models affects how architects approach design decisions. In Design-Build by Administration, risk sharing is less defined; the architect coordinates construction and management but does not bear explicit cost guarantees.

In Design-Build by Guaranteed Maximum Cost, the architect assumes explicit financial responsibility for cost predictability. This incentivizes aggressive value engineering, early cost estimating, and constructive collaboration with building teams to identify cost-saving opportunities without compromising design quality.

Both models require the architect to assume professional responsibility and civil liability for design quality and construction execution. The cost guarantee in the GMC model adds financial accountability for cost outcomes.

### Selection Criteria for Each Model

Design-Build by Administration suits projects where scope and requirements are well-defined, where client and architect relationships are established, and where collaborative cost management is preferred over guaranteed costs. These projects typically feature engaged clients willing to review cost developments actively.

Design-Build by Guaranteed Maximum Cost suits projects where cost certainty is paramount, where the client requires protection from cost escalation, or where the architect's confidence in cost estimating and value management is strong. These projects benefit from the financial alignment that equal savings-sharing creates.

### Contract Documentation and Clarity

Both models require clear contractual definition of scope, responsibilities, change order procedures, and cost controls. Design-Build by Administration contracts specify the architect's management role and fee structure. Design-Build by Guaranteed Maximum Cost contracts define the guaranteed maximum, cost-sharing formulas, and conditions for legitimate cost increases.

Successful Design-Build relationships, regardless of model, depend on transparency about cost factors, clear communication about design implications, and shared commitment to project success. The contractual structure provides the framework, but professional integrity and collaborative problem-solving determine actual outcomes.
