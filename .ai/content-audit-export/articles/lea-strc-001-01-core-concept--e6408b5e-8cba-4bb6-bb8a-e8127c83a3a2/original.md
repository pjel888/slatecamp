## What You Need to Know for the LEA

This topic covers the three fundamental properties that completely describe any force applied to a structure. The LEA tests whether you can identify, define, and distinguish these properties in force diagrams and loading scenarios.

## Core Concept

A **force** is the action of one body on another and is the fundamental cause of deformation and motion in structures. Every force is uniquely defined by exactly three properties:

1. **Magnitude:** The size or intensity of the force, always expressed as a positive value with units (newtons, kilonewtons, pounds, etc.). Magnitude tells you _how much_ force is being applied.

2. **Direction:** The line along which the force acts, expressed as an angle or alignment relative to a reference frame (horizontal, vertical, or a specific coordinate axis). Direction tells you _along which line_ the force operates.

3. **Sense (or Line of Action):** The orientation of the force along its line of direction—whether it acts toward or away from a point, upward or downward, left or right. Sense indicates _which way_ along that line the force points. In vector notation, sense is captured by the sign (positive or negative) or the arrowhead direction on a force diagram.

Together, these three properties completely specify a force's effect on a structure. Two forces with identical magnitude and direction but opposite sense will produce opposite effects—one causing tension, the other compression, for example. In vector terms, magnitude + direction + sense = the **vector representation** of that force.

## Key Provisions

| Property | Definition | Example | Units |
|----------|-----------|---------|-------|
| **Magnitude** | Intensity or size of the force | 50 kN | kN, N, kgf |
| **Direction** | Line along which force acts | 45° from horizontal | Degrees or compass |
| **Sense** | Orientation along the direction (+ or −) | Downward; Tension | Arrow direction on diagram |

## Philippine Practice Context

In Philippine structural design, forces are documented in free-body diagrams and load path sketches. Engineers must communicate magnitude, direction, and sense clearly in drawings and calculations submitted to DPWH or local building officials. Load arrows on structural drawings must always show unambiguous sense—ambiguity in sense has caused misinterpretation of intended load paths on site.

## How It's Tested

1. **Identification:** "Which of the following is NOT a property of a force?" — tests whether you know the exact three properties.
2. **Distinction:** "A 10 kN force acting downward differs from a 10 kN force acting upward in which property?" — tests sense vs. direction.
3. **Application:** "On a free-body diagram, an upward arrow at a support is best described as having magnitude ___, direction ___, and sense ___." — tests ability to read diagrams correctly.

<Tip>**Memory anchor:** **MDS = Magnitude, Direction, Sense.** Every force question will ask you to identify or apply one or more of these. If you can label all three on a diagram, you understand the force completely.</Tip>
