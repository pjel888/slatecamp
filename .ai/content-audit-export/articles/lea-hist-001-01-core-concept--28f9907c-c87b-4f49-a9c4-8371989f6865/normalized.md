## Core Concept

Megalithic architecture emerged in the Neolithic and Bronze Age periods (roughly 4500–1000 BCE) across Europe, the Mediterranean, and parts of Asia. The term "megalithic" refers to structures built from large, undressed or roughly dressed stone blocks, arranged without mortar. Unlike later civilizations that developed sophisticated masonry and vaulting, megalithic cultures relied entirely on the **post-and-lintel** system: vertical stones (posts) supporting horizontal stones (lintels) above them. This simple but structurally sound method remained unchanged across centuries and cultures.

The primary function of megalithic structures was ceremonial or funerary. **Dolmens** (chambers formed by vertical stones capped with a flat stone) served as burial chambers, often covered by earthen mounds. **Menhirs** were single standing stones, frequently arranged in patterns for ceremonial or territorial purposes. **Stone circles** and **alignments** (like Stonehenge and Carnac Stones) suggest astronomical or calendrical functions, though exact purposes remain debated.

Construction methods were labor-intensive but straightforward. Stones were quarried, dragged overland using wooden sledges and ropes, and positioned by communal effort. No evidence suggests mechanical advantage systems; these monuments relied on sheer manpower and persistence. The quarry marks and unfinished stones at various sites show that methods were crude but effective.

The post-and-lintel system has inherent limitations: horizontal stones cannot span great distances without collapsing, and the weight of the lintel creates stress concentration at the points of contact. This is why megalithic interiors tend to be narrow and chamber-like. Despite these constraints, the system proved remarkably durable. Many megalithic structures survive intact today because the basic engineering—weight distribution through vertical support—works without refinement.

Different regions developed local variations. **Passage graves** in Ireland and Scotland featured long corridors leading to burial chambers, some with sophisticated corbelling. **Temple complexes** in Malta employed multiple megaliths to create roofed sanctuaries. **Menhir alignments**, particularly at Carnac in France, stretched for kilometers, suggesting ritual procession routes or astronomical sight lines.

## Key Terms

**Post-and-lintel:** A structural system using vertical stones (posts) supporting horizontal stones (lintels); requires direct vertical alignment to work.

**Dolmen:** A megalithic chamber formed by vertical stones capped with a flat stone; typically used for burial.

**Menhir:** A single, large standing stone, often arranged with others for ceremonial or astronomical purposes.

**Passage grave:** A megalithic tomb with a narrow corridor leading to a burial chamber, often corbelled and covered by an earthen mound.

**Corbelling:** A cantilever building technique where each course of stone projects slightly beyond the one below, creating a false arch or vault.

**Stone circle:** A ritual arrangement of standing stones in a circular or near-circular plan; examples include Stonehenge and Callanish Stones.

**Menhir alignment:** Multiple standing stones arranged in rows or processional patterns, as at Carnac.

**Trilithon:** A post-and-lintel unit consisting of two vertical stones supporting one horizontal stone; famously used at Stonehenge.

**Chambered cairn:** A stone mound covering a burial chamber, constructed from stacked megaliths.

**Sarsen stone:** A type of hard sandstone used in megalithic construction, quarried in southern England and used prominently at Stonehenge.
