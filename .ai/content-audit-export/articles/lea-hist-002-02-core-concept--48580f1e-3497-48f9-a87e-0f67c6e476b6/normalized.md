## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal

Identify Romanesque architectural characteristics — barrel vaults, groin vaults, thick walls, small windows, cruciform plans — and recognize key examples (Pisa Cathedral, Durham Cathedral, Speyer Cathedral) by their structural and regional distinctions.
## Core Concept

**Romanesque architecture** (11th–12th centuries) emerged in Western Europe as builders learned to construct massive stone vaults and arches without relying on wooden roofs that burned. This period marks the first truly Medieval architectural language, distinct from both Roman revival and subsequent Gothic sophistication.

The structural foundation is the **barrel vault**—a continuous semicircular vault spanning the entire nave. Unlike the Roman equivalents it echoes, Romanesque barrel vaults require massive supporting walls to contain their outward thrust. Builders compensated by thickening walls, reducing window size, and concentrating weight downward. The interior is therefore darker, more massive, and more cavern-like than Early Christian basilicas.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0002 component="Tip" status="preserved"]

The **round arch** appears everywhere: arcades, windows, entrance portals, vault openings. It creates a rhythmic, almost primitive aesthetic compared to the soaring verticality of later Gothic. The structure remains fundamentally *horizontal* in emphasis—long nave leading toward the altar—rather than the vertical thrust of pointed arches.

**Cross vaults** (groin vaults) appear in aisles and smaller spaces, created when two barrel vaults intersect at right angles. At their intersection, ribs of stone provided visual emphasis and, potentially, some structural benefit.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Note" status="preserved"]

Romanesque churches emphasize:
- **Massive, dark interiors** creating a sense of enclosure and spiritual intensity
- **Rhythmic repetition** of arches, columns, and bays
- **Sculptural decoration** on facade portals and capitals, increasingly naturalistic
- **Fortified appearance** reflecting the feudal period's military reality
- **Fortress-like masses** of stone expressing permanence and spiritual strength

**Structural Terminology:**
- **Barrel vault**: A continuous semicircular vault spanning the full length of a space
- **Groin vault (cross vault)**: The intersection of two barrel vaults at right angles
- **Ribbed vault**: Structural ribs emphasizing vault edges; decorative in early Romanesque, load-bearing in Gothic
- **Flying buttress**: An external or semi-external arch transferring lateral thrust of a vault to an outer pier; in Romanesque, often internal or modest
- **Round arch**: A semicircular arch; primary Romanesque opening form

## Key Terms

- **Barrel vault**: A continuous semicircular vault; primary Romanesque structural system requiring thick walls
- **Groin vault (cross vault)**: The intersection of two barrel vaults at right angles; used in Romanesque aisles and smaller spaces
- **Ribbed vault**: A vault with structural ribs emphasizing edges; decorative in early Romanesque, load-bearing in Gothic
- **Round arch**: A semicircular arch; the dominant Romanesque opening form
- **Transept**: The cross arm of a church plan
- **Nave**: The central longitudinal space of a church
- **Aisle**: A lateral passageway flanking the nave
- **Flying buttress**: An external or semi-external arch transferring vault thrust to an outer pier; modest in Romanesque, structural skeleton in Gothic
- **Pier**: A vertical supporting element, typically more substantial than a column
- **Ambulatory**: A passageway around the choir and apse, allowing pilgrims to view relics without interrupting services
