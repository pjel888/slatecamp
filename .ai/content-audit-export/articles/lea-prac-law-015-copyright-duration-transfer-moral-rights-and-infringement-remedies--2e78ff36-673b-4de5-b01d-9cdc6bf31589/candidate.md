## Copyright Duration, Transfer, Moral Rights, and Infringement Remedies

## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- State the duration of copyright protection for architectural works (lifetime + 50 years).
- Explain the transfer of copyright through written assignment.
- List the moral rights of the architect-author.
- Identify infringement remedies: injunctions, damages, impounding of infringing materials.

---
### Copyright Duration and Post-Mortem Protection

Under RA 8293 as amended by RA 10372, copyright in architectural works by a natural person extends for the lifetime of the architect plus 50 years after death (Sec. 213.4).. This extended protection period acknowledges the enduring value and significance of architectural creations, which may appreciate in cultural and commercial importance over decades. The 50-year post-mortem term allows the architect's heirs, estate, and successors to benefit from the intellectual property rights long after the creator's death.

Calculating protection duration begins with the date of the architect's death, not the date the building was completed or the design was created. For example, if an architect died in 2020, copyright protection extends through 2070. This calculation applies to individual architects. For works owned by juridical persons (architectural firms or design companies), the protection term is 50 years from date of publication or date of making if unpublished (Sec. 213.2). The extended duration encourages original creative work by guaranteeing long-term commercial value.

### Transfer and Assignment of Copyright

The copyright in architectural works, as an intellectual property asset, may be transferred or assigned through written agreement. An architect may sell, license, or assign copyright rights to clients, developers, other architects, or corporate entities. The transfer does not require approval from the Board of Architecture or any government authority; it is a voluntary commercial transaction between willing parties governed by contract law.

A valid copyright assignment should explicitly identify the architectural work (the specific design or project), clearly state that copyright is transferred, specify the date of transfer and any term limitations, and identify both the transferor and transferee by legal name. Oral agreements or ambiguous language regarding "designs" or "plans" may create disputes about whether copyright actually transferred or whether only a license to use for a specific project was granted. Architects considering assignment should engage legal counsel to draft clear transfer documentation that protects both parties' interests and reflects their actual commercial intent.

### Rights After Assignment and Licensing

When an architect assigns copyright to a developer or client, the assignee becomes the copyright owner with full rights to reproduce, distribute, and control the use of the plans and designs. The assignee may license the designs to third parties (such as contractors, consultants, or other developers), sublicense usage rights, register the copyright in their name with IPOPHL, and pursue infringement actions against unauthorized users. The original architect, after assignment, loses the right to enforce economic copyright unless the assignment was limited or conditional.

Architects who license rather than assign retain ownership while granting limited usage rights to the licensee. A license agreement should specify the scope of permitted uses (e.g., construction of the specific project, but not reproduction for other projects), duration of the license (one-time use for the project, or perpetual), whether the license is exclusive or non-exclusive, and whether sublicensing is permitted. Clear licensing terms reduce disputes by establishing expectations regarding who may use the plans and for what purposes.

### Moral Rights and Their Inalienability

RA 8293 provides that moral rights—the right of attribution (being credited as author) and the right of integrity (objecting to distortions that damage honor or reputation)—are inalienable. This means architects retain moral rights even after assigning or licensing economic copyright. The architect cannot waive or transfer moral rights to another party; these rights remain with the original creator regardless of copyright ownership transfers.

The right of attribution ensures architects receive proper credit when their works are displayed, reproduced, or publicly acknowledged. Removing the architect's name from building signage, failing to credit the architect in published photographs, or claiming authorship of the design violates this moral right. The right of integrity protects against significant alterations that distort or mutilate the work in ways that damage the architect's professional reputation. Modifications that fundamentally change the design intent or eliminate distinctive design elements may violate this right, even when the copyright owner permits the alterations.

### Civil Remedies for Copyright Infringement

When architectural copyright is infringed, RA 8293 provides multiple civil remedies. The copyright owner may seek a preliminary injunction from the court to stop ongoing infringement while the case proceeds. The injunction prevents further reproduction, distribution, or public display of infringing copies, protecting against continued losses and erosion of the architecture's market value. Obtaining injunctive relief often requires demonstrating a likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable harm that cannot be compensated by money damages.

Monetary damages available under RA 8293 include actual damages (the actual losses suffered, such as lost fees or diminished professional reputation) plus profits earned by the infringer from unauthorized use of the architectural work. The copyright owner need not prove which remedy provides greater recovery; the owner may claim both actual damages and infringer's profits. In cases where actual damages and profits are difficult to calculate, statutory damages up to PHP 50,000 per work infringed (Sec. 216(a)) may be awarded without proof of specific losses.

### Criminal Penalties and Willful Infringement

RA 8293 provides criminal penalties for copyright infringement, but only in cases of willful and commercial infringement with intent to defraud or gain profit. Criminal violations include manufacturing infringing copies, distributing copies for commercial advantage, and importing counterfeit copies into the Philippines. Criminal penalties include fines from PHP 50,000 to PHP 200,000 and imprisonment of one to four years for first-time commercial infringers (Sec. 217).

Willful infringement requires proof that the infringer knew the conduct constituted infringement or acted with reckless disregard for copyright rights. A developer who knowingly constructs a building using stolen architectural plans without the architect's permission, or a contractor who reproduces copyrighted specifications for unauthorized use, commits willful infringement subject to criminal prosecution. However, unintentional copying, good-faith dispute regarding permission, or naive reliance on false assertions of authority typically do not constitute willful infringement triggering criminal liability.

### Infringement by Construction

For architectural works embodied in buildings, infringement includes constructing a building substantially similar in creative expression to the protected design without authorization. The owner of architectural copyright may prevent unauthorized construction that reproduces the distinctive design elements. However, courts must distinguish between copying the architect's protected creative expression and independently designing a functional building that meets the same program requirements.

A builder who constructs a building with the same general program and functional layout but with different aesthetic treatment, spatial arrangement, and creative conceptualization has not infringed copyright. A builder who copies the specific distinctive design features (such as the distinctive facade pattern, signature spatial flow, or innovative structural expression) has committed infringement. Expert testimony and comparative analysis of the original design and the allegedly infringing building are typically required to establish substantial similarity of protected expression.

### Remedies for Violation of Moral Rights

In addition to economic copyright enforcement, architects may pursue remedies for violation of moral rights. These remedies include the right to claim or deny authorship, the right to demand correction of misattribution, and the right to sue for violation of integrity. When an architect's name is removed from a work or falsely attributed to another, the architect may demand restoration of correct attribution and damages for harm to professional reputation.

Violations of the right of integrity—such as radical demolition of distinctive design features, unauthorized major alterations that destroy design intent, or vandalism that damages aesthetic integrity—may trigger legal action. Remedies include injunctions to prevent further distortion, damages for harm to the architect's reputation, and in some cases, mandatory correction or restoration of the work's integrity. The existence and strength of moral rights claims depend on the specific facts and the degree to which the work has been altered or misattributed.
