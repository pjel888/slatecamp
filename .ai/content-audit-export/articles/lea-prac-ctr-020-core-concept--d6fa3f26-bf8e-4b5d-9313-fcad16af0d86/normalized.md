## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- Describe dispute resolution methods: negotiation, mediation, arbitration, litigation.
- Distinguish the Construction Industry Arbitration Commission (CIAC) from regular courts.
- Explain the arbitration clause in standard construction contracts.
- Identify which disputes are subject to mandatory arbitration.

Construction claims are formal demands for money, time extensions, or other relief arising from alleged breach or non-performance by the other party. Claims are classified by basis: (1) delay claims for schedule extensions and associated costs, (2) defect claims for contractor failure to perform work per specifications, (3) changed work claims for modifications to original scope, and (4) differing site conditions claims for unforeseen conditions. The contractor bears the obligation to document all claims thoroughly and submit them within contractual timeframes, typically with detailed backup documentation demonstrating the factual and contractual basis.

## Core Concept

Construction claims create disputes that require documentation to substantiate. Under Philippine construction contracts (UAP Document 301), the contractor must submit claims in writing with supporting evidence: invoices, correspondence, proof of cost incurrence, time records, and interpretation of contract language. Claims are not casual requests but formal assertions that the other party (typically the owner or architect) is liable to pay or extend time. The doctrine of "claims discipline" requires that claims be documented, submitted promptly, and supported by evidence sufficient to prove the factual basis and the contract right being asserted.

Claims are distinguished by type based on root cause. Delay claims allege that the contractor is entitled to a schedule extension and associated costs because the owner failed to provide access, changed the work scope, or otherwise delayed performance. Defect claims arise when the contractor disputes architect rejection of completed work, arguing that the work complies with contract documents and the architect's rejection is improper. Changed work claims assert that work performed was not in the original scope and the contractor is entitled to payment above the contract price. Differing site conditions claims allege that unforeseen subsurface, environmental, or latent conditions make performance impossible at the contract price.

The documentation obligation is fundamental to claims discipline. The contractor must maintain daily site records, time sheets, cost records, correspondence with the architect, and copies of all change order requests. Without documentation, claims fail. The owner and architect are entitled to demand proof before considering a claim's merits. If the contractor fails to document performance or costs contemporaneously (during construction), subsequent claims for time or cost recovery face skeptical treatment in dispute resolution.

## Philippine Practice Context

Under Philippine construction practice and UAP Document 301, claims are governed by strict documentation and submission requirements. The contractor who claims delay must prove: (1) the event that caused delay (owner action/inaction), (2) that the delay was not contractor's responsibility, (3) the number of calendar days of delay, and (4) the contractor's entitlement under the contract to time extension and associated costs. Without all four elements documented, the claim fails. The contractor cannot simply assert delay; proof is mandatory.

Cost-based claims (changed work, differing site conditions) require the contractor to document: original price for the work, actual costs incurred, invoices from suppliers and subcontractors, labor time records, equipment rental evidence, and proof that costs were reasonable and not inflated. The contractor's failure to maintain cost records during construction eliminates the ability to recover additional costs later. The architect is authorized to demand documentation and reject claims that lack substantiation. If the contractor cannot produce invoices or cost records, the architect may recommend claim denial.

The submission deadline is strictly construed. Many contracts specify that claims must be submitted within 30 days of the event triggering the claim (change order request issued, delay ends, differing condition discovered, etc.). Claims submitted after the deadline are waived unless the contractor can show it was excused from timely submission by the owner's or architect's actions. The contractor's failure to track and submit timely claims is a common litigation scenario and typically results in claim denial.

The architect's role in claims review is central. The architect inspects the work, reviews the contractor's documentation, and prepares a written recommendation to the owner regarding claim merit. The architect's recommendation influences whether the owner will negotiate or deny the claim. If the architect's recommendation is unreasonable or fails to properly evaluate the contractor's evidence, the architect may face claims from the contractor for failure to impartially evaluate the claim.

## How It's Tested

**Question Pattern 1:** Claim documentation—What documentation must a contractor provide to substantiate a delay claim? (Tests documentation requirement and burden of proof)

**Question Pattern 2:** Delay claim elements—What must the contractor prove to establish entitlement to schedule extension? (Tests four-element delay claim framework)

**Question Pattern 3:** Cost documentation—If a contractor claims additional costs for changed work, what proof is required? (Tests cost substantiation and invoicing requirements)

**Question Pattern 4:** Submission deadline—What is the consequence of submitting a claim after the contract deadline? (Tests deadline strictness and waiver doctrine)

**Question Pattern 5:** Architect's claim review authority—What is the architect's role in evaluating and recommending on contractor claims? (Tests architect's claim review responsibility and impartial evaluation duty)
