## Learning Goal
After reading this article, you should understand the key concepts tested in the LEA for this topic, including relevant code provisions, dimensional standards, and common exam applications.

Conflicts of interest represent one of the most challenging ethical issues in architectural practice. Unlike clear violations (fraud, negligence), conflicts exist in shades of gray. An architect may have competing interests that cloud judgment, yet honestly believe she will act objectively. The Code of Ethical Conduct and RA 9266 require more than honest intention—they require **disclosure and client informed consent**.

This article examines how architects identify conflicts, the obligations triggered by their recognition, disclosure mechanics, and situations requiring recusal (withdrawal) from engagement.

## Definition: What Constitutes a Conflict of Interest?

A conflict of interest exists when an architect has competing financial, professional, or personal interests that could:

- **Compromise objectivity** in advising the client
- **Create divided loyalty** between client and another party
- **Motivate self-dealing** decisions that benefit the architect more than the client
- **Create appearance of impropriety** even if actual judgment remains objective

<Callout type="critical">
**Key Principle:** A conflict of interest is determined **objectively**, not by the architect's subjective belief in her own impartiality. Even an architect convinced she can remain objective must disclose conflicts that would reasonably appear to compromise objectivity.
</Callout>

The Code of Ethical Conduct establishes that architects must:

1. **Recognize conflicts** as they arise
2. **Disclose conflicts promptly** to affected clients
3. **Obtain informed consent** before proceeding
4. **Recuse themselves** when conflict cannot be mitigated by disclosure

## Category 1: Financial Conflicts

### Definition and Scope

Financial conflicts occur when the architect has direct or indirect economic interest in outcomes beyond professional fees, including:

- **Ownership stake** in contractors, suppliers, or service providers recommended to client
- **Commission arrangements** with suppliers, contractors, or referral sources
- **Profit participation** in products or services recommended
- **Competitive bidding situations** where architect benefits from selected contractor's choice
- **Investment interests** in project funding sources

### Type 1A: Undisclosed Supplier Commissions

**Scenario 1:** Architect Leax receives a 10% commission from a mechanical systems supplier whenever he specifies their equipment in projects. He does not disclose this arrangement to clients. When designing an HVAC system, he consistently selects the supplier's higher-cost premium line over equivalent lower-cost alternatives.

**Conflict Analysis:**
- **Financial interest:** Architect's fee increases with supplier's product cost
- **Compromise:** Architect's judgment on product selection is biased toward higher cost
- **Client harm:** Client pays premium prices unnecessarily
- **Ethical violation:** Code of Ethics Section 4 requires honest professional relationships
- **Disclosure solution:** Architect should disclose the commission arrangement and allow client to:
 - Accept the relationship with full knowledge
 - Prohibit the commission arrangement
 - Seek competitive bids from unaffiliated suppliers

<ExamTip>
**LEA Test Point:** The fact that equivleant products exist is irrelevant. If the architect benefits financially from selecting one supplier over another, that's a conflict requiring disclosure, regardless of product quality. Disclosure isn't always easy—clients may react negatively—but it's ethically mandatory.
</ExamTip>

### Type 1B: Investment in Contractor or Vendor

**Scenario 2:** Architect Baker's spouse owns an electrical contracting firm. When Architect Baker designs buildings, he has the contractor bid on the electrical work. He does not disclose the family relationship to clients.

**Conflict Analysis:**
- **Financial interest:** Architect's household income increases if the spouse's contractor is selected
- **Divided loyalty:** Architect's objectivity in evaluating contractor performance is compromised
- **Appearance:** Client and other bidders reasonably assume the contractor has inside advantage
- **Ethical violation:** Code of Ethics Section 3 (duty to client) and Section 4 (honest relationships)
- **Disclosure and consent:** Architect must disclose the relationship and allow client to:
 - Proceed knowing the relationship exists
 - Prohibit the spouse's contractor from bidding
 - Require competitive bid from independent contractors

<Callout type="requirement">
**Disclosure Elements:** Disclosure must include:
1. **Identification of the conflict** (what competing interest exists)
2. **Financial details** (how much do you stand to gain?)
3. **Impact on judgment** (how could this cloud your recommendation?)
4. **Mitigation steps** (what safeguards can address the conflict?)
5. **Client's right to consent or refuse** (client can require independent bids)
</Callout>

### Type 1C: Competitor Conflict in Bidding

**Scenario 3:** Architect Cho designs a building and also bids as a contractor for the general construction work. She does not disclose to the client that she is one of the bidders. She wins the contract and proceeds as both designer and GC.

**Conflict Analysis:**
- **Divided loyalty:** Architect's design decisions now affect her own costs as contractor
- **Self-dealing:** Architect may design in ways that maximize her profit as GC
- **Appearance:** Other bidders were unaware of the dual role
- **Ethical violation:** Code of Ethics Section 1 (honesty); fiduciary duty (Section 3)
- **Disclosure required:** Must inform client in advance of all intended roles and obtain consent before design work begins

**Key distinction:** Serving as both designer and contractor is not inherently prohibited, but **undisclosed dual roles constitute conflict**. Disclosure and client consent transform a conflict into manageable dual representation.

### Type 1D: Financial Interest in Project Funding

**Scenario 4:** Architect Delta recommends specific contractors, suppliers, and consultants for a client's project. Unknown to the client, Architect Delta has received equity investment incentives from a financing source that profits more if project costs are higher. The source has instructed Delta to recommend high-cost vendors.

**Conflict Analysis:**
- **Hidden financial interest:** Delta benefits when project costs increase
- **Corruption:** Delta is essentially paid by a third party to bias recommendations
- **Client fraud:** Delta presents herself as objective advisor while being biased
- **Serious violation:** This is fraud, not merely undisclosed conflict
- **No mitigation possible:** Even disclosure would not ethically solve this because the interest is fundamentally at odds with client interests

## Category 2: Professional and Employment Conflicts

### Type 2A: Multiple Client Representation with Opposed Interests

**Scenario 5:** Architect Eva agrees to design an office building for a developer (Client A). Simultaneously, without Client A's knowledge, she agrees to design the general contractor's temporary site management facility (Client B). The contractor's staging plan requires significant site area, conflicting with the developer's desired building location and outdoor space.

**Conflict Analysis:**
- **Competing clients:** Developer and contractor have materially opposed interests in site use
- **Divided loyalty:** Eva's recommendation on site layout affects both clients differently
- **Undisclosed representation:** Neither client knows Eva represents the other
- **Ethical violation:** Code of Ethics Section 3 requires exclusive loyalty; Section 4 requires honesty
- **Disclosure solution:** Eva must:
 1. Disclose to developer that she also represents the contractor
 2. Disclose to contractor that she also represents the developer
 3. Obtain informed consent from both clients
 4. Establish clear boundaries (only design, no construction management; disclose all decisions affecting both clients)

<Callout type="important">
**Critical Point:** The appearance of conflict is what triggers the disclosure requirement. Even if Eva believes she can remain objective, the existence of multiple opposed clients reasonably appears to compromise objectivity. Disclosure is mandatory.
</Callout>

### Type 2B: Consultant Conflict

**Scenario 6:** Architect Frank is in general practice and also serves as an expert witness in architectural malpractice litigation. A client brings a design commission to Frank, but unknown to the client, Frank is defending an architect in litigation involving a case with similar facts. Frank's defense strategy in the litigation might inform how he advises his new client.

**Conflict Analysis:**
- **Professional conflict:** Frank's litigation position could bias his client advice
- **Information flow:** Confidential information from the litigation might inform the new client work
- **Ethical violation:** Code of Ethics Section 3 (duty to client); potential confidentiality breach
- **Disclosure required:** Frank should disclose the litigation involvement and obtain consent, or recuse himself from the new engagement

### Type 2C: Prior Representation and Information

**Scenario 7:** Architect Grace previously designed a building for Developer A. The design included proprietary systems and cost estimates. Several years later, Developer B approaches Grace for a similar building type. Grace recognizes that her prior experience with Developer A gives her efficiency and cost advantages. However, Grace realizes that using insights from her prior Developer A work (without using confidential information directly) creates an appearance that Grace's recommendations might favor her own cost efficiency over Developer B's interests.

**Conflict Analysis:**
- **Information advantage:** Grace's prior experience creates asymmetric information
- **Appearance:** Grace is recommending approaches based on her familiarity, not Developer B's best interests
- **Ethical handling:** Grace should:
 1. Disclose the prior similar project (without confidential details)
 2. Explain how prior experience benefits Developer B
 3. Assure Developer B that advice is based on Developer B's interests, not Grace's efficiency
 4. Offer to recuse herself if Developer B has concerns

**Note:** Prior experience is valuable and doesn't itself create conflict. The disclosure and clarity about objectivity address the potential appearance of impropriety.

## Category 3: Personal and Relationship Conflicts

### Type 3A: Family Relationships

**Scenario 8:** Architect Henry is designing a municipal building project selected through competitive bidding. Henry's cousin is the City Engineer overseeing the project. Henry does not disclose the family relationship to either the city or to his own client.

**Conflict Analysis:**
- **Appearance of favoritism:** Cousin's position could provide inside advantage or preferential treatment
- **Corruption appearance:** Family relationship might create obligation to favor cousin's city interests
- **Ethical violation:** Code of Ethics Section 1 (honesty about relationships); Section 4 (honest professional relationships)
- **Disclosure required:** Henry should disclose to both his client and to the City Engineer. The City Engineer may need to recuse himself from evaluation aspects due to his conflict, but Henry's conflict is primarily with his own client (honesty and objectivity).

### Type 3B: Personal Romantic Relationship

**Scenario 9:** Architect Iris is designing a retail building and is in a new romantic relationship with the contractor bidding on the project. Neither her client nor the contractor's company management knows of the relationship. Iris influences her recommendation toward the contractor, and he is awarded the contract.

**Conflict Analysis:**
- **Personal relationship:** Romantic involvement with bidding contractor
- **Motivation to bias:** Personal relationship incentivizes favoring the contractor
- **Undisclosed influence:** Client unaware that personal relationship, not merit, drove the recommendation
- **Ethical violation:** Code of Ethics Section 3 (duty to client); Section 1 (honesty)
- **Disclosure required:** Iris must disclose the relationship and allow client to:
 - Proceed with full knowledge (rare approval in romantic relationship cases)
 - Require competitive evaluation by independent person
 - Disqualify the contractor from consideration

<ExamTip>
**LEA Test Point:** Personal romantic relationships with vendors or contractors create conflicts so inherent that disclosure may not be sufficient ethical remedy. Best practice: Recuse yourself entirely from recommendations involving persons with whom you have romantic relationships.
</ExamTip>

### Type 3C: Friendship and Social Relationships

**Scenario 10:** Architect Jack designs buildings and regularly has social relationship with a structural engineer. Jack frequently recommends this engineer as subconsultant, in part because of the friendship. Jack does not disclose the social relationship to clients or discuss how the friendship influences recommendations.

**Conflict Analysis:**
- **Friendship bias:** Social relationship affects technical judgment about which engineer is "best-qualified"
- **Appearance of preferential hiring:** Friendship, not qualifications, drives the recommendation
- **Client harm:** Client may not receive services from truly best-qualified engineer; competitive engineers are disadvantaged
- **Ethical violation:** Code of Ethics Section 3 (duty to select qualified consultants based on merit)
- **Disclosure and mitigation:**
 1. Disclose the social relationship to clients
 2. Explain how qualifications support the recommendation independent of friendship
 3. Offer to provide competitive bids from other qualified engineers
 4. Let client decide if they want independent consultant selection

## The Conflict Analysis Matrix

<ComparisonTable>
Use this framework to analyze whether a situation creates a conflict requiring disclosure:

**Question 1:** Do I have a competing financial, professional, or personal interest?
- No → No conflict; proceed without disclosure
- Yes → Continue to Question 2

**Question 2:** Could this competing interest compromise my objective judgment?
- No, clear separation → Likely no conflict, but consider disclosure for transparency
- Yes, interests are directly opposed → Clear conflict; proceed to Question 3

**Question 3:** Is the conflict already known or apparent to the client?
- Yes, client already knows → May not require additional disclosure, but confirm understanding
- No, client is unaware → Proceed to Question 4

**Question 4:** Could a reasonable third party view this competing interest as compromising my objectivity?
- No, unlikely → No disclosure required (confidential client relationships are rare exceptions)
- Yes, reasonably appears to compromise objectivity → Disclosure required

**Question 5:** Can I ethically serve the client despite the competing interest?
- No, interests are irreconcilable → Recuse yourself; decline the engagement
- Yes, with appropriate safeguards (disclosure, written client consent, limitation of scope) → Proceed with disclosure and documented consent
</ComparisonTable>

## The Disclosure Process

### Elements of Effective Disclosure

Disclosure is not a casual mention; it requires structured communication:

<ProcessFlow>
1. **Timing:** Disclose **before** accepting the engagement or **immediately** upon discovering the conflict
2. **Format:** Written disclosure (email or letter) establishing clear record
3. **Content:** Identify the conflict, explain how it could compromise judgment, describe any mitigation
4. **Clarity:** Use plain language; avoid legal jargon or minimization
5. **Right of Refusal:** Explicitly state client's right to decline proceeding given the conflict
6. **Client Response:** Obtain written informed consent or documented refusal before proceeding
7. **Documentation:** Retain the disclosure and client response in project file
</ProcessFlow>

### Sample Disclosure Language

**Scenario: Architect recommending spouse's electrical contractor**

"I am writing to disclose a potential conflict of interest regarding the electrical contractor work for your project. My spouse owns XYZ Electrical, a qualified electrical contractor. While I believe XYZ Electrical can provide excellent service, this family relationship creates an apparent conflict of interest because my household income is affected by whether XYZ Electrical is selected.

To ensure your interests are fully protected, I recommend one of the following approaches:

**Option 1:** You may consent to XYZ Electrical's participation with full knowledge of the family relationship. In this case, I will ensure competitive bidding among qualified contractors, and you will make the final selection decision based on merit and cost.

**Option 2:** You may require that XYZ Electrical not be considered for the work, and I will solicit bids from other qualified electrical contractors.

**Option 3:** You may require that a third-party construction manager independently evaluate all electrical bids to ensure objectivity in the selection process.

Please let me know which approach you prefer, or feel free to suggest an alternative that addresses your concerns. I am committed to serving your interests with complete transparency and objectivity."

**Key elements:**
- Clearly names the conflict
- Explains why it's a conflict (financial interest)
- Proposes specific mitigation options
- Gives client clear choice
- Documents the disclosure for the file

### Documentation of Consent

Obtain written acknowledgment of disclosure:

"I acknowledge that Architect [Name] has disclosed [specific conflict], and I understand how this could affect objectivity. I consent to [Architect Name] proceeding with the engagement under the following conditions: [specific conditions]. I understand that I may revoke this consent at any time."

**Signed and dated by client representative**

This documentation protects both architect and client by establishing that disclosure occurred and client made an informed choice.

## Category 4: When Disclosure Is Insufficient—Situations Requiring Recusal

Some conflicts are so fundamental that disclosure and consent cannot adequately protect the client. Recusal (withdrawal from engagement) is required:

### Recusal Situation 1: Irreconcilable Conflicts

**Scenario 11:** Architect Kyle is hired to design a building for Developer A. Before design begins, a major contractor asks Kyle to certify the quality of work on the same site (construction administration for developer B's concurrent project). The two projects involve the same contractor, creating the appearance of conflict (Kyle cannot objectively certify the contractor's work if Kyle's design depends on the same contractor performing well).

**Analysis:** Kyle should **recuse himself** from one of the engagements because the conflicts are irreconcilable. Disclosure alone cannot solve the fundamental problem of divided loyalty and compromised judgment.

### Recusal Situation 2: Confidentiality Risk

**Scenario 12:** Architect Laura previously represented Company A in designing their headquarters, learning confidential information about the company's technology, costs, and operational plans. Company B (a competitor) now asks Laura to design their new facility. Even with disclosure and Company A's theoretical consent, Laura cannot ethically proceed because:

- Laura possesses Company A's confidential information
- Designing for Company B will inevitably use insights from confidential Company A information
- There's no way to unlearn what Laura knows

**Analysis:** Laura should **recuse herself** from the Company B engagement. The confidential information creates an insurmountable barrier to objective service to Company B.

### Recusal Situation 3: Fundamental Values Conflict

**Scenario 13:** Architect Mike is asked to design a building for a company whose business practices he believes are deeply unethical (e.g., environmental harm, labor exploitation). Mike believes he cannot serve this client because his personal opposition to the company's practices will compromise his judgment and professional integrity.

**Analysis:** Mike may **recuse himself**, but note the distinction:
- This is not an ethical violation on the client's part
- This is Mike choosing to decline work based on his own professional values
- Declining work on values grounds is ethically permissible; proceeding while deeply opposed might compromise objectivity

<Callout type="important">
**Distinction:** Values conflicts (choosing not to serve clients whose practices you oppose) are different from economic conflicts (benefiting from biased advice). Both may justify recusal, but for different reasons. Values conflicts are about the architect's professional integrity; economic conflicts are about protecting the client from the architect's bias.
</Callout>

## Conflicts in Specialized Roles

### Role 1: Design-Bid-Build Services (Architect as Neutral Advisor)

In traditional design-bid-build, the architect certifies both design compliance and contractor performance. This creates an inherent conflict: the architect designed the building and may be reluctant to harshly certify contractor non-compliance.

**Mitigation:** Clear contractual separation of roles, explicit requirement that architect remains independent, client understanding that architect is not guarantor but rather certifier.

### Role 2: Design-Build Services (Architect as Part of Contractor Team)

In design-build, the architect is part of the contractor's team, creating inherent conflict of interest with building official or owner inspector role if the architect holds any inspection role.

**Mitigation:** Clear separation of roles; architect focuses on design; independent inspector (not affiliated with design-builder) certifies contractor work; transparent disclosure that architect is part of the delivery team.

### Role 3: Construction Management (Architect as Manager of Contractor)

If the architect serves as construction manager, additional conflicts arise:
- Architect designed the building; now architect is evaluating her own design
- Architect's fee structure might incentivize accelerating schedule or reducing costs in ways that conflict with quality
- Architect's judgment on contractor claims is influenced by architect's own design decisions

**Mitigation:** Written agreement clarifying architect's role, fee structure that doesn't incentivize compromises, clear separation between design decisions and cost/schedule decisions, transparent disclosure to all parties about the dual role.

### Role 4: Expert Witness (Architect Evaluating Other Architects' Work)

When architect serves as expert witness in a malpractice case, conflicts arise:
- Architect may be biased toward defending architects (professional solidarity) or toward plaintiffs (fear of establishing dangerous precedent)
- Architect's testimony may be influenced by the fee structure (plaintiff vs. defense attorneys)
- Architect may unconsciously favor positions that benefit architects 

**Mitigation:** Clear written engagement letter specifying that expert will provide objective analysis regardless of where analysis leads; fee structure that doesn't incentivize particular conclusions; transparent disclosure of any conflicts with parties or their counsel; willingness to recuse if conflicts are irreconcilable.

## Conflicts and Discipline

The PRBoA enforces conflict of interest rules through discipline:

- **Undisclosed financial interest:** Suspension 1-2 years; restitution of unjust gains
- **Undisclosed multiple representation:** Suspension 1-3 years; possible loss of client relationship; restitution
- **Accepting bribes or kickbacks:** Suspension 3-5 years; possible revocation; potential criminal prosecution
- **Concealing conflict and profiting from it:** Suspension 2-5 years; mandatory restitution; possible revocation
- **Refusing to recuse from irreconcilable conflict:** Aggravating factor in other discipline

<ExamTip>
**LEA Exam Insight:** Questions about conflicts often test whether candidates understand that:
1. A conflict exists based on objective circumstances, not subjective belief in objectivity
2. Disclosure is the primary remedy for most conflicts
3. Some conflicts are so fundamental they require recusal
4. Concealing a conflict and profiting from it is more serious than disclosing it
5. Even clients' consent doesn't eliminate the ethical duty to disclose
</ExamTip>
