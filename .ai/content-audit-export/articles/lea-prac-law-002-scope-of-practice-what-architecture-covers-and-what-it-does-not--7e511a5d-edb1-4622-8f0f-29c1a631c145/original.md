## Scope of Practice: What Architecture Covers and What It Does Not
## Learning Goal
After reading this article, you should understand the key concepts tested in the LEA for this topic, including relevant code provisions, dimensional standards, and common exam applications.

### The Comprehensive Definition of Architectural Scope

Section 3 of RA 9266 and the IRR's definitions establish that the scope of architectural practice encompasses the provision of professional services in connection with site, physical and planning, and the design, construction, enlargement, conservation, renovation, remodeling, restoration, or alteration of a building or group of buildings. The statute recognizes that architectural services are not limited to the enumerated categories but include all these and many others.

This broad framing reflects architectural practice's evolution from simple building design to comprehensive environmental stewardship, adaptive reuse, heritage conservation, and integrated project delivery. RA 9266 Section 3 enumerates ten (10) distinct service areas (labeled a through j) that collectively capture the full spectrum of architectural work.

### Planning, Design, and Structural Conceptualization

The first category encompasses planning, architectural designing, and structural conceptualization. Planning includes site planning (detailed site development planning of all areas surrounding a building or structure within property limits) and physical planning (detailed physical planning at site, community, or urban levels). Architectural designing covers schematic design, design development, contract documents, and construction phase deliverables.

Structural conceptualization—the act of conceiving, choosing, and developing the type, disposition, arrangement, and proportioning of structural elements—falls squarely within architectural scope. However, under RA 9266 and professional practice standards, the preparation, signing, and sealing of structural design documents remain the exclusive domain of licensed structural engineers. This distinction ensures architects lead structural thinking while engineers bear final structural calculation accountability.

### Consultation, Advice, and Pre-Design Services

The second category includes consultation, consultancy, giving oral or written advice and directions, conferences, evaluations, investigations, quality surveys, appraisals and adjustments, architectural and operational planning, and site analysis and other pre-design services. These services recognize that not all architectural engagement involves a full design; many clients need advisory input on feasibility, code compliance, site potential, or existing building assessment.

This category covers preliminary assessments, building condition evaluations, heritage significance studies, accessibility audits, energy performance reviews, and expert testimony in architectural disputes. Architects serving in these capacities may charge professional fees without delivering designs, reflecting the market reality that architectural knowledge extends far beyond new design.

### Schematic Design Through Construction Administration

The third category addresses schematic design, design development, contract documents, and construction phases including professional consultancies. This recognizes the iterative nature of design: from initial conceptual schemes through refined design development to detailed construction documents, specification writing, and on-site administration during construction.

Schematic design documents establish the architectural concept, spatial relationships, and massing. Design development refines these with system and material selections. Contract documents—specifications, detailed drawings, bid documents—translate design intent into constructible form. The construction phase encompasses site observations, daily reports, review of shop drawings and samples, and issuance of change orders and clarifications.

### Feasibility Studies and Project Delivery Services

The fourth category covers preparation of preliminary, technical, economic, and financial feasibility studies of plans, models, and project promotional services. This recognizes that many projects never reach construction; architects spend significant effort analyzing whether proposed projects are economically viable, structurally sound, and marketable.

These studies may include pro forma financial models, phased development scenarios, material cost analyses, structural system comparisons, and marketing materials for investor or public presentation. Architects also provide valuation services, appraisals, and expert opinions on property potential, building quality, and renovation versus replacement decision-making.

### Architectural Documents, Specifications, and Cost Management

The fifth category includes preparation of architectural plans, specifications, bill of materials, cost estimates, general conditions, and bidding documents. Architectural documents are defined as architectural drawings, specifications, and other outputs that only an architect can sign and seal, including vicinity maps, site development plans, architectural programs, perspective drawings, floor plans, elevations, sections, ceiling plans, schedules, detailed drawings, technical specifications, cost estimates, and other instruments of service in any form.

This category underscores that architects hold exclusive authority over the documents that bear their seal and signature. The bill of materials translates specifications into procurement language; cost estimates enable budget planning; general conditions address contract administration mechanics; bidding documents structure competitive procurement. All represent core architectural services.

### Construction Management and Project Coordination

The sixth category encompasses construction and project management, giving general management, administration, supervision, coordination, and responsible direction of planning, architectural designing, construction, reconstruction, erection, enlargement, demolition, renovation, repair, orderly removal, remodeling, alteration, preservation, or restoration of buildings or structures or complex buildings and their components, sites, and environs intended for private or public use.

This expansive category recognizes that architects often serve as the primary project coordinator, directing the efforts of contractors, consultants, and subcontractors. Project management may include scheduling, budget tracking, quality assurance, safety coordination (to the extent not delegated to contractors), and stakeholder communication. The responsible direction standard imposes accountability; architects cannot simply accept whatever contractors propose without independent judgment.

### Interior Design and Space Planning Within Buildings

The seventh category includes planning, architectural lay-outing, and utilization of spaces within and surrounding buildings or structures; housing design and community architecture; architectural interiors and space planning; architectural detailing; architectural lighting; acoustics; architectural lay-outing of mechanical, electrical, electronic, sanitary, plumbing, communications, and other utility systems; equipment and fixtures.

This recognizes that the built environment encompasses far more than exterior form. Architectural interiors cover spatial organization, material selections, lighting design, acoustic performance, and integration of building systems into finished spaces. Lighting design ensures both function and ambiance; acoustic design ensures speech intelligibility, noise isolation, and reverberation control. Equipment and fixture coordination ensures that HVAC, electrical, plumbing, and communications systems are integrated rather than conflicting.

Housing design and community architecture extend architectural scope to residential development and neighborhood-scale planning. These emerging practice areas reflect growing demand for sustainable, livable, equitable communities rather than isolated buildings.

### Building Programming, Arbitration, and Conservation

The eighth category covers building programming, building administration, construction arbitration, and architectural conservation and restoration. Building programming is the systematic process of analyzing client needs, site constraints, regulatory requirements, and program requirements to produce a documented program that guides design. Programming establishes performance criteria, functional relationships, spatial adjacencies, and quantitative requirements before design begins.

Building administration encompasses contract administration, change management, budget tracking, and schedule coordination. Construction arbitration involves serving as an expert or neutral party in disputes between owner, architect, and contractor over contractual interpretation, design adequacy, or work quality. Architectural conservation and restoration address heritage preservation, adaptive reuse, and restoration of historic structures, a specialized practice area with distinct technical and ethical requirements.

### Coordinating Works and System Integration

The ninth category includes all works which relate to the scientific, aesthetic, and orderly coordination of all works and branches of the work, systems and processes necessary for the production of a complete building or structure, whether for public or private use, in order to enhance or safeguard life, health, and property, and the promotion and enrichment of the quality of life, and the architectural design of engineering structures or any part thereof.

This catch-all provision recognizes that buildings are integrated systems, not collections of independent trades. Architects coordinate structural, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, fire safety, accessibility, and communications systems toward unified performance. The reference to engineering structures acknowledges that while structural engineers design the frame, architects often conceptualize and coordinate structural expression and integration.

### Emerging Practices and Specialized Services

The tenth category encompasses all other works, projects, and activities which require the professional competence of an architect, including teaching of architectural subjects and architectural computer-aided design. This provision ensures that as professional practice evolves—toward sustainable design, resilience, digital modeling, adaptive reuse, and other emerging fields—those services remain within architectural scope if they demand architectural competence.

Teaching architecture and conducting research into building performance, material innovation, or design methodology are recognized as legitimate architectural work. Computer-aided design (CAD) and building information modeling (BIM) fall within scope as tools that extend rather than replace architectural judgment.

### Boundaries: What Is Not Architecture

While scope is comprehensive, the statute and judicial interpretation establish clear boundaries. Structural design documents must be signed by structural engineers; architects cannot assume structural engineer responsibilities. Electrical system design requires a licensed electrical engineer; architects cannot design the power distribution system, though they coordinate electrical layout within spaces.

Similarly, mechanical system design (HVAC, plumbing) is engineered by licensed MEP engineers. Landscape design is the domain of landscape architects in jurisdictions with separate licensure. Construction itself—the physical act of building—is performed by contractors, not architects, though architects supervise. Civil engineering (site grading, storm management, utility coordination) belongs to civil engineers.

### The Demarcation Between AoR and AiCC Authority

Within architectural scope, two role distinctions carry significant implications. The Architect-of-Record maintains total design responsibility and signs all architectural documents. The Architect-in-Charge of Construction assumes supervision and coordination authority on site but does not create new design; the AiCC interprets the AoR's documents, observes compliance, and recommends corrective action.

These roles may be held by the same person or different persons. When different, the AoR cannot delegate design responsibility to the AiCC, nor can the AiCC expand supervision into design modification. Clear role definition prevents confusion and protects both the client relationship and the coordination chain.

### Service Agreements and Scope Definition

RA 9266 defines a service agreement as a duly notarized written contract or equivalent public instrument stipulating the scope of services and guaranteeing compensation of services to be rendered by a registered and licensed architect. The statute thus requires that architectural scope, in each engagement, be explicitly defined by written agreement.

Service agreements must explicitly define the scope of services per RA 9266 Section 3. Architects are accountable to their contractual undertakings; violation may result in civil liability and disciplinary action by the PRBoA. This standard encourages clear contracting and holds architects accountable to their undertakings.

### Scope Limitations and Protected Domains

Certain professional services remain reserved to other licensed professions. Structural design documents must be signed by structural engineers. Surveys and property boundary determinations are surveyor work. Geotechnical investigations are engineer work. Environmental impact assessments may require environmental science expertise. Code-mandated fire protection system design is fire protection engineer work.

Architects coordinate all these domains but do not perform them. This creates interdisciplinary teams in which the architect often holds leadership (as AoR or project manager) but respects other professions' reserved domains. Boundary clarity prevents liability exposure and ensures that each discipline's licensees remain accountable for their specialization.

### The Evolution of Scope Through Regulatory Guidance

The PRBoA's power under Section 7(f) to "monitor the conditions affecting the practice of architecture and adopt such measures as may be deemed proper for the enhancement and maintenance of high professional, ethical and technical standards" provides flexibility to evolve scope interpretation as practice develops.

Thus, new service categories—sustainable design certification, resilience planning, heritage documentation, digital fabrication coordination—can be recognized as falling within architectural scope without statutory amendment, provided the PRBoA determines they require architectural competence and warrant professional accountability.
