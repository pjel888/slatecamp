## Obligations to Fellow Architects — Non-Poaching, Non-Interference, and Professional Courtesy
## Learning Goal
After reading this article, you should understand the key concepts tested in the LEA for this topic, including non-poaching rules, notification requirements, successor architect obligations, and the prohibition on injurious conduct.

Professional relationships form the foundation of architectural practice. The Code of Ethical Conduct establishes clear obligations governing how architects interact with colleagues, protecting both individual practitioners and the profession's integrity.

### Non-Poaching of Commissions

Under Section 5-c of the Code, an architect shall not under any circumstances seek commissions previously endowed to another architect, whether the original arrangement has been definitively agreed upon or remains in negotiation. This provision prevents unfair competition and protects architects from losing work through improper means. The prohibition is absolute — it encompasses all circumstances and methods. An architect who learns of a commission assignment cannot attempt to acquire it, even if the original engagement appears uncertain.

### Non-Interference Obligations

Section 5-e establishes that an architect shall not undertake a commission previously assigned to another architect until: (1) proper written notification occurs, (2) the original employment termination is confirmed, and (3) compensation is settled. This creates a sequential requirement — notification first, then verification of termination, then proof of compensation completion.

Section 5-f extends protection to renovation and remodeling work. When another architect previously designed a structure, the new architect must notify the original architect even if the property owner has changed. When the original design's greater mass, area, or design remains substantially maintained, the new architect must limit public claims to only the work actually performed.

### Injurious Conduct Prohibition

Section 5-g prohibits architects from knowingly injuring, falsely or maliciously, another architect's professional reputation, prospects, or practice. This extends beyond simple non-poaching to active protection of colleagues' standing.

## How It's Tested

**Pattern 1:** Non-poaching scope — A competitor designed the original structure 15 years ago but has no current engagement. Must verify prior involvement regardless.

**Pattern 2:** Notification timing — The violation occurs at the attempted acquisition, not at successful diversion.

**Pattern 3:** Credit proportionality — Architect performs 40% renovation. Marketing must describe 40% contribution, cannot claim primary credit.
