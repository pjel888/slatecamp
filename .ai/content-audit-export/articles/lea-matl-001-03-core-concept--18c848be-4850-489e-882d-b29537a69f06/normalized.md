## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal

Classify wood joints (mortise-and-tenon, dovetail, lap, dado, rabbet, butt) by geometry, load capacity, and application, and identify correct fastening methods (nails, screws, bolts, adhesives) for each joint type in Philippine timber construction.
## Core Concept

Wood connections transform separate timber pieces into integrated structural elements. Each joint type distributes stress differently and develops strength through geometry, friction, or mechanical fastening. Understanding joint behavior under tension, compression, and shear is essential for designing reliable timber structures.

The strength of a connection depends on three factors: the joint geometry (how pieces overlap), the fastening method (nails, screws, glue, or bolts), and the wood grain orientation relative to applied loads.

### Mortise-and-Tenon Joint

A mortise is a rectangular cavity chiseled into one timber member. A tenon is a protruding rectangular element cut from the mating piece that slides into the mortise. This ancient joint excels in tension and resists racking because the tenon acts as both a mechanical key and a glue line.

### Dovetail Joint

Dovetails are interlocking tails (projections) and pins (recesses) that create a mechanical lock preventing lateral separation. The angled geometry distributes shear stress over larger glue lines. Dovetails excel in drawer boxes and cabinet construction where pull-out resistance is critical.

### Lap Joints

Half-lap, full-lap, and cross-lap joints are created by reducing one or both pieces to half thickness where they overlap. Lap joints rely on both glue area and fasteners (bolts or screws). They are faster to machine than mortise-and-tenon but weaker in tension.

### Dado and Rabbet Joints

A dado is a groove cut across the wood grain (perpendicular to edges), while a rabbet is a stepped recess on an edge. Both joints increase glue surface area and provide mechanical alignment. Dadoes are common in shelf construction; rabbets frame panel assemblies.

### Butt Joints

The simplest joint—one piece butts perpendicular against another with fasteners alone. Butt joints have minimal glue area and low strength, suitable only for non-critical applications or when reinforced with metal brackets.

## Fastening Methods and Mechanical Connections

### Nailed Connections

Nails work through friction and bearing against wood fibers. Single nails fail by bending or pulling through under high shear. Multiple nails in staggered patterns improve performance. Modern tests show a single 16d nail in softwood develops shear strength around 150—200 lbf.

### Screwed Connections

Screws develop strength through helical engagement and bearing. Screw connections resist withdrawal better than nails due to the mechanical thread action. Wood screws typically develop 2—4 times higher withdrawal resistance than comparable nails. Fasteners must be sized to avoid over-driving, which crushes wood fibers and reduces strength.

### Glued Connections

Wood glue (typically urea-formaldehyde or polyvinyl acetate) creates a permanent bond when grain surfaces are properly prepared. Glue strength depends on:

- Moisture content of wood (12—15% optimal)
- Clamping pressure (typically 100—200 psi for interior glues)
- Curing time (12—24 hours before loading)
- Surface preparation (planed or sanded within hours of gluing)

Glued joints often exceed the shear strength of the wood itself when properly executed, making them ideal for load-bearing frames.

### Combined Fastening

The strongest connections use both mechanical fasteners and adhesive. A bolted lap joint with epoxy or exterior-grade polyurethane glue is more reliable than either method alone under dynamic or impact loading.

## Timber Connectors for Structural Applications

### Split Ring Connectors

Split rings are cast iron rings with a circular split and internal tooth. The ring seats in grooves cut into both adjoining timber members. As bolt tension is applied, the split closes and the teeth bite into the wood, transferring load through bearing and friction.

### Shear Plate Connectors

Shear plates are circular malleable iron discs with a raised center tooth. Unlike split rings, one plate is embedded flush in each wood member and bolted together. Shear plates are intended primarily for wood-to-steel connections or demountable structures.

The flat geometry concentrates stress under the tooth, making shear plates more suitable for perpendicular loads than for lateral tension. A typical 2-5/8 inch shear plate on a 3/4 inch bolt develops 3000—4000 lbf in shear.

### Joist Hangers and Post Bases

Metal hangers suspend floor joists from beams or headers, transferring vertical load through steel fasteners rather than end-grain bearing (which is weak). A standard joist hanger rated for 2500—4000 lbf per nailing leg. Post bases secure timber posts to foundations, preventing uplift and lateral movement while allowing drainage beneath the post.

## Philippine Construction Context

Philippine hardwoods—particularly narra, molave, and yakal—are prized for joinery due to their fine grain and workability. Narra accepts mortise-and-tenon joints cleanly and glues well, making it the traditional choice for door and window frames. Molave's density and hardness suit heavy structural connections in timber frames used in heritage buildings and naval vessels.

High humidity and termite activity in the Philippines demand that all wood connections be sealed with approved exterior finishes. Split ring and shear plate connectors must be installed with stainless steel or hot-dipped galvanized fasteners (ASTM A 153 Class D minimum) to prevent rust and wood deterioration.

NSCP 2015 Sections 601—623 govern timber design and connections. The standard references NFPA Load and Resistance Factor Design (LRFD) equations and allowable stress design (ASD) approaches for computing connection capacity. Load factors and resistance reduction factors account for variability in wood species, moisture, and duration of load.

## How It's Tested

The LEA assesses your ability to:

1. Identify joint types from cross-sections and sketches
2. Rank joints by strength and stiffness
3. Select appropriate fasteners for specific loads and wood types
4. Apply NSCP 2015 spacing and edge-distance requirements
5. Determine when to use mechanical connectors versus traditional joinery
6. Estimate shear, tension, and withdrawal capacities

Typical questions present a building detail with a wood connection and ask whether it meets code, or ask you to match joint characteristics to applications (structural frame vs. finish carpentry).
