## 2. Core Concepts with Mnemonic

### The Governing Framework: Five Sections, One Foundation

Rule I of PD 1096 and the 2004 Revised IRR establishes the legal, policy, and definitional bedrock upon which every other rule rests. A mistake in interpreting Section 106 cascades through the entire permitting process.

---

**Principle 1 — The Code Sets MINIMUM Standards (Sec. 102)**

Per PD 1096, Section 102 (Declaration of Policy) and mirrored in IRR Sec. 102, the National Building Code provides a framework of **minimum standards and requirements**. The exact policy statement reads:

> "It is hereby declared to be the policy of the State to safeguard life, health, property, and public welfare, consistent with the principles of sound environmental management and control, and to this end, make it the purpose of this Code to provide for all buildings and structures, a framework of minimum standards and requirements…"

The operative word is **minimum**. The Code does not prescribe optimal, recommended, or maximum standards. Local governments, project specifications, and referral codes may exceed minimums; nothing in PD 1096 prevents that. What they cannot do is fall below.

**EXAM POINT:** If a question asks what the National Building Code provides, the correct answer is always **minimum standards** — not maximum, not ideal, not comprehensive.

---

**Principle 2 — Scope Is Comprehensive But Has Two Exceptions (Sec. 103)**

The basic law (PD 1096, Sec. 103) covers all public and private buildings and structures. The 2004 Revised IRR, Sec. 103 explicitly lists the covered disciplines:

1. Architectural
2. Civil / Structural
3. Electrical
4. Mechanical
5. Sanitary
6. Plumbing
7. Electronics

The IRR also explicitly lists regulated activities: design, location, siting, construction, alteration, repair, conversion, use, occupancy, maintenance, moving, demolition, and addition to buildings and structures.

**Two statutory exceptions to coverage:**

| Exception | Basis | Notes |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| Traditional indigenous family dwellings | PD 1096, Sec. 103 | Defined in the basic law; e.g., bahay kubo, Ifugao houses |
| Buildings covered by BP 220 | 2004 Revised IRR, Sec. 103 (IRR expansion) | Economic and socialized housing; covered by HLURB/DHSUD standards |

**IRR Expansion — Legalizing Existing Structures:** The 2004 Revised IRR adds a provision not explicit in the basic law: existing buildings constructed **without permits** may be **legalized** if they are made to conform to all requirements, subject to corresponding penalties, surcharges, and fines. This is frequently tested in professional practice questions.

---

**Principle 3 — Site Must Be Suitable for Human Habitation (Sec. 105)**

Per IRR Sec. 105, every site must be **sanitary, hygienic, and safe**. For sites intended for human habitation, safe distance must be maintained from:

- Streams, bodies of water, or sources of **air pollution**
- **Volcanic** sites or active/potentially active volcanoes
- Buildings that constitute a potential source of **fire or explosion**

This section is the statutory basis for site suitability review at the OBO level and connects directly to environmental clearance requirements, HLURB locational clearances, and zoning ordinances.

---

**Principle 4 — All Buildings Must Be Safe, Suited to Purpose, and Maintained (Sec. 104)**

Per IRR Sec. 104:
1. All buildings **shall conform** to principles of safe construction and be **suited to their purpose** (occupancy type and use)
2. Manufacturing buildings **shall observe** adequate environmental safeguards
3. ALL buildings **shall be maintained** in safe, sanitary, and good working condition — indefinitely, not just at time of construction

This last point is critical: maintenance is a continuing code obligation, not a one-time compliance event.

---

**Principle 5 — Referral Codes Supplement, Not Replace (Sec. 103)**

Per IRR Sec. 103, applicable provisions of codes and standards of various professional organizations, government agencies, and technical associations are deemed **Referral Codes** of the National Building Code. They are supplementary — applicable when PD 1096 does not directly address a technical matter. Examples: NSCP (structural), PEC (electrical), PSME Code (mechanical), PCAB standards.

## 4. In-Depth Technical Analysis

### Section 101 — Title

The full official title is: **"Revised Implementing Rules and Regulations of the National Building Code of the Philippines (P.D. 1096)"** — commonly abbreviated as **IRR**. The basic law (PD 1096) was enacted in 1977. The original IRR was issued then; the **2004 Revised IRR** is the currently operative version. When the LEA exam refers to "the IRR," it means the 2004 Revised IRR unless otherwise specified.

**Practical note:** PD 1096 (basic law) and the 2004 Revised IRR are two distinct documents. Board exam questions sometimes test whether candidates can distinguish between what the basic law says versus what the IRR adds, modifies, or operationalizes. Where the IRR is silent, the basic law governs. Where they conflict, the basic law prevails (the IRR cannot contradict its enabling statute).

---

### Section 102 — Declaration of Policy (Detailed Analysis)

The policy statement has four protected interests:

1. **Life** — structural integrity, means of egress, fire safety
2. **Health** — ventilation, sanitation, potable water, hazardous materials
3. **Property** — setbacks, fire-resistive construction, adjacent building protection
4. **Public welfare** — environmental management, land use compatibility

The qualifier "consistent with sound environmental management and control" was prescient in 1977; it anticipates the Environmental Impact Assessment system and DENR clearances that now form part of the building permit package.

**"Minimum standards" — full legal implications:**
- A building official cannot approve plans that fall below minimum standards
- A building official **can** approve plans that exceed minimum standards
- A local government unit (LGU) **can** adopt stricter standards via ordinance
- A private developer's specifications that exceed code are legally permissible
- Contractual requirements that fall below code minimums are void as against public policy

---

### Section 103 — Scope and Application (Detailed Analysis)

**The seven covered disciplines** are significant because each discipline requires a **licensed professional** to sign and seal the relevant plans and documents. Per RA 9266 (Architecture Act of 2004) and its implementing rules, architects are responsible for the architectural documents; civil engineers for structural; electrical engineers for electrical plans, etc.

**Regulated activities (IRR Sec. 103) — Full List:**

| Activity | Brief Description |
|----------|------------------|
| Design | Creation of plans/specifications |
| Location | Where on the lot the building sits (setbacks, easements) |
| Siting | Overall site planning and orientation |
| Construction | Physical building work (see Sec. 106 definition) |
| Alteration | Physical changes within existing footprint |
| Repair | Restoration of damaged portions |
| Conversion | Change in use/occupancy |
| Use | How the structure is utilized |
| Occupancy | Classification and loading of spaces |
| Maintenance | Continuing obligation of safe, sanitary condition |
| Moving | Transfer of structure to new location |
| Demolition | Systematic dismantling |
| Addition | Extension increasing height/floor area |

**Exception 1 — Traditional Indigenous Family Dwellings:**
These are structures built by indigenous cultural communities using traditional materials and methods as defined in the basic law. The exemption is narrow: it applies to family dwellings, not to commercial structures or institutional buildings, even if built by indigenous communities. The LEA exam may test the boundary of this exception.

**Exception 2 — BP 220 Buildings (IRR Expansion):**
Batas Pambansa Blg. 220 (1982) covers economic and socialized housing. The HLURB (now DHSUD) issues its own standards for BP 220 projects. The IRR explicitly removes these from PD 1096 jurisdiction to avoid dual regulation. Note: this exception is in the IRR, not in the original text of PD 1096 Sec. 103 — a distinction the LEA sometimes tests.

**Legalization of Existing Structures:**
The IRR provision allowing legalization of unpermitted buildings is an important professional practice point. The process requires:
- Full conformity with current code requirements (not just the code at time of original construction)
- Payment of all applicable fees, penalties, surcharges, and fines
- Inspection and approval by the Building Official

This is not an amnesty program — it is a compliance pathway with full penalty exposure.

---

### Section 104 — General Building Requirements (Detailed Analysis)

The word **"shall"** appears in all three requirements of Sec. 104. Under Philippine statutory construction, "shall" is mandatory. The three obligations are:

1. **Conformity to safe construction principles** — This connects to Rule IV (Types of Construction), Rule V (Fire Zones), Rule VI (Fire-Resistive Requirements), and the NSCP as a referral code for structural safety.

2. **Suitability to purpose** — A building must be designed for its intended occupancy group (Rule VII). A Group E (Business and Mercantile) building designed to residential standards is not code-compliant regardless of structural soundness.

3. **Maintenance in safe, sanitary, and good working condition** — This is a perpetual obligation. Building officials have authority under Rule II to order the repair, renovation, or demolition of buildings that fail this standard. The obligation runs with the land, not the original permittee.

---

### Section 105 — Site Requirements (Detailed Analysis)

The threshold standard has three descriptors: **sanitary, hygienic, and safe**. These are not alternatives — the site must satisfy all three.

For sites intended for human habitation, the three hazard categories requiring safe distance:

**Category 1 — Water and Air Pollution Sources:**
This encompasses rivers, creeks, esteros, lakes, Manila Bay and similar coastal waters, and any fixed installation producing air pollution. The "safe distance" is not numerically defined in Sec. 105 itself — it is determined by applicable environmental regulations (DENR), local zoning, and easement requirements under the Water Code of the Philippines (PD 1067). The PD 1067 easements (3 meters for creeks, 20 meters for rivers in urban areas, etc.) set baseline distances.

**Category 2 — Volcanic Sites:**
Relevant in the Philippine context given the country's position in the Pacific Ring of Fire. The Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology (PHIVOLCS) designates Permanent Danger Zones (PDZ) and Extended Danger Zones (EDZ) around active volcanoes. Sites within these zones do not satisfy Sec. 105 regardless of other code compliance.

**Category 3 — Fire or Explosion Risk Buildings:**
This connects to Rule V (Fire Zones) and the IRR provisions on hazardous occupancies. A site adjacent to a petroleum storage facility, LPG bottling plant, or fireworks factory may be disqualified under Sec. 105 even if the proposed building itself is fully code-compliant.

---

### Section 106 — Definitions (Detailed Analysis)

#### ADDITION vs. ALTERATION — The Central Exam Distinction

**ADDITION** (IRR Sec. 106): *"New construction added to an existing building or structure which increases its height and/or its floor area."*

Extracted summary:

\
- Height increase example: adding a third floor to a two-storey building
- Floor area increase example: extending the ground floor footprint into the rear setback (with variance)
- Both: adding a floor AND extending the footprint

**ALTERATION** (IRR Sec. 106): *"Any change, addition or modification in construction, or any change in the existing facilities of the building or a change in the use of the building — that does not increase the height and/or floor area of the building."*

The IRR definition of alteration encompasses:
- Changes in construction materials
- Changes in partitioning layout
- Changes in location or size of openings (doors, windows)
- Changes in structural parts (within existing envelope)
- Changes in utilities and equipment
- Changes in use — but ONLY if no height/area increase results

**The bridge concept:** When a project involves BOTH a change in materials/partitioning AND an increase in height or floor area, the permit is for an **addition** (the area/height increase triggers the higher classification). The alteration components are covered within the same permit application.

---

#### RENOVATION vs. REPAIR vs. ALTERATION — The Three-Way Distinction

**RENOVATION** (IRR Sec. 106): *"Any physical change made on a building to increase the value and/or improve the aesthetic quality of the building."*

Key markers:
- Goal: **increase value** and/or **improve aesthetics**
- Does NOT require functional change
- Does NOT require structural change
- Examples: installing new floor finishes, repainting with high-quality materials, replacing windows with higher-quality units, upgrading lobby finishes

**REPAIR** (IRR Sec. 106): *"Remedial work done on any damaged or deteriorated portion/s of a building to restore it to its original condition."*

Key markers:
- Goal: **restore to original** — not improve, not enhance
- Triggered by damage or deterioration
- A repaired element must match the original, not upgrade it
- Examples: patching a cracked CHB wall, replacing a broken floor tile with an identical one, repainting to match original color

**ALTERATION** revisited:
Key markers:
- Goal: **change** — not improve, not restore
- Physical change in materials, configuration, or components
- Does NOT increase height/floor area
- Examples: removing non-load-bearing partitions, relocating a door opening, upgrading plumbing from 3/4" to 1" pipe within existing envelope

**The practical test:**
- Same-for-same = **Repair**
- Better-for-same-function = **Renovation**
- Different-for-different-configuration = **Alteration**
- More space = **Addition**
- Different use = **Conversion**

---

#### CONVERSION — The Use-Based Definition

**CONVERSION** (IRR Sec. 106): *"Any change in the use or occupancy of buildings or parts thereof — that has different requirements."*

The qualifying phrase "that has different requirements" is significant: not every change in use is a conversion in the code sense. If a space moves from one occupancy subgroup to another subgroup with **identical** requirements, no conversion permit may be triggered. However, in practice, the OBO typically requires a conversion permit for any change in the stated use.

Conversion connects directly to:
- Rule VII (Occupancy Classification): each Group has specific requirements for area, height, ventilation, exits, etc.
- Rule III (Permits and Inspections): a conversion permit is required
- RA 9514 (Fire Code): change of occupancy triggers new fire safety evaluation

---

#### Key Administrative Definitions

**BUILDING OFFICIAL (BO):** The Executive Officer of the OBO, appointed by the DPWH Secretary. The BO has enforcement authority over PD 1096 within their jurisdiction. Per Rule II, the BO can:
- Issue building permits
- Issue Certificates of Occupancy
- Order stoppage of work
- Order demolition of dangerous buildings
- Impose fees, fines, and penalties

**BUILDING PERMIT:** A document — not an approval letter, not a verbal clearance — issued by the BO. It authorizes the permittee to proceed with specific construction/installation/addition/alteration/renovation/conversion/repair/moving/demolition after the submitted plans have been found satisfactory and conforming to the Code.

The building permit is NOT a certificate of occupancy. Construction may be permitted; occupancy requires a separate certificate. Occupying a building without a Certificate of Occupancy violates Rule III.

**APPLICANT/PERMITTEE:** The definition is notably broad — "any person, firm, partnership, corporation, or head of institution." This means juristic persons (corporations, partnerships) may be permittees, not only natural persons. The head of an institution (university president, hospital administrator) may be the permittee for institutional buildings.

## 5. PH vs. USA Comparative Analysis

### Structural Differences in Code Framework

| Dimension | Philippines (PD 1096 + 2004 IRR) | USA (IBC 2021 + IRC) |
|-----------|----------------------------------|----------------------|
| **Code structure** | Single national code for all building types | IBC (commercial/multi-family) + IRC (residential ≤3 stories) as separate model codes |
| **Adoption** | National law (PD 1096); IRR issued by DPWH | Model codes adopted by states/municipalities with local amendments |
| **Enforcement authority** | Building Official appointed by DPWH Secretary | Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ) — typically local government appointee |
| **Scope exceptions** | Indigenous dwellings; BP 220 housing | Detached 1-2 family dwellings ≤3 stories + accessory structures (covered by IRC instead) |
| **Definitions depth** | ~20 core terms in Sec. 106 | 500+ defined terms in IBC Ch. 2 |
| **Referral codes** | General supplementary status per Sec. 103 | Referenced standards listed in Ch. 35; mandatory compliance when cited |
| **Policy statement** | Explicit legislative policy in Sec. 102 (life/health/property/welfare) | IBC 101.3: Intent — public safety, health, welfare; does not use "minimum" as explicitly |
| **Legalizing unpermitted buildings** | Explicit IRR provision with penalties | Handled through local "code compliance" or "retroactive permitting" processes; not uniform nationally |
| **Renovation vs. Alteration** | Two separate defined terms with distinct meanings | Single category "Alteration" encompasses both; no separate "Renovation" definition |
| **Minimum standards language** | "Framework of minimum standards" — explicit in Sec. 102 | IBC does not use "minimum" as prominently; states the intent of safety, not the floor of compliance |

### Where PH Code Is More Explicit

The PD 1096 IRR's explicit separation of Renovation, Repair, Alteration, Addition, and Conversion into five distinct defined categories is more precise for permitting purposes than the IBC approach. Under the IBC, the catch-all "Alteration" category covers most physical changes, relying on the "Change of Occupancy" chapter to handle use changes. Philippine practitioners working on US projects must remember that what they call "Renovation" in PH practice would be categorized as an "Alteration" under the IBC.

### Where IBC Is More Comprehensive

The IBC's 500+ definition terms in Chapter 2 — versus PD 1096 IRR's ~20 core terms — means the IBC has far more precise regulatory vocabulary for edge cases. The IBC also explicitly covers **relocation** (moving a structure) with its own compliance pathway; PD 1096 defines "Moving" but the permit and compliance pathway for relocating an existing structure is less detailed in Rule I.

### US Scope Exception — IRC vs. IBC

The US exception for detached 1-2 family dwellings is analogous to the PH exception for indigenous dwellings — but the analogy is imperfect. The US separates residential from commercial using the **IRC** (International Residential Code), which is a full-featured code with its own standards. The Philippine exemption for indigenous dwellings has no alternative code replacing PD 1096; these structures simply fall outside regulation. BP 220 housing has its own parallel standards (HLURB/DHSUD), making it closer to the IRC model.

## 6. Exam Traps

### Trap Leart 1 — "The Code Sets Optimal Standards"

**Wrong answer:** The National Building Code establishes optimal or recommended standards for building construction.

**Why it's wrong:** Per PD 1096, Sec. 102 (and IRR Sec. 102), the Code expressly provides a framework of **minimum** standards and requirements. "Optimal" implies the best possible; "recommended" implies voluntary compliance. Both are wrong. The Code sets a legally enforceable floor below which no building may fall.

**Correct answer:** The National Building Code establishes **minimum** standards and requirements. Projects may exceed these minimums; they cannot fall below them.

**How the LEA tests this:** The question may offer choices with "minimum," "maximum," "recommended," and "optional." Only "minimum" is correct.

---

### Trap Leart 2 — Addition vs. Alteration (The Floor Area Ambiguity)

**Wrong answer scenario:** A contractor enclosing an existing open terrace to create a new room — adding floor area but not height — calls this an "alteration" because no new vertical construction is being added.

**Why it's wrong:** Per IRR Sec. 106, an **Addition** is new construction that increases height **and/or** floor area. The "and/or" means that increasing EITHER height OR floor area — not necessarily both — constitutes an addition. Enclosing a terrace increases gross floor area; therefore, the work is an **Addition**, requiring an addition permit.

**Correct answer:** Any work that increases gross floor area (even without increasing height) is an **Addition** under IRR Sec. 106. Conversely, adding a mechanical penthouse that increases height but not usable floor area would still be an Addition.

**How the LEA tests this:** The distractor presents a scenario where only one dimension (height OR area, not both) changes, hoping candidates assume both must change. The "and/or" in the definition is the key.

---

\

### Trap Leart 3 — Renovation vs. Repair (The "Like-for-Like" Trap)

**Wrong answer scenario:** A building owner replaces all damaged ceramic floor tiles with new, higher-quality imported marble. The answer choice calls this "Repair."

**Why it's wrong:** Per IRR Sec. 106, **Repair** is remedial work to restore damaged portions to their **original condition**. "Original condition" means same material, same quality, same configuration. Replacing ceramic tile with marble is not restoring to original condition — it is improving the value and aesthetic quality of the building. This is **Renovation**.

**Correct answer:** The work is **Renovation** per IRR Sec. 106 because it improves the value and/or aesthetic quality of the building, even though the trigger for the work was damage to the original floor.

**How the LEA tests this:** The exam mixes the trigger (damage = repair-like scenario) with the outcome (upgrade = renovation). Candidates must focus on the RESULT of the work, not what prompted it. If the result improves value or aesthetics beyond original, it is Renovation.

**Bonus trap:** Similarly, patching a cracked plaster wall with the same mix and texture = **Repair**. Patching the crack but then applying a textured finish to all walls = **Renovation** for the finish work, **Repair** for the structural patch.

## 7. Board-Prep Mnemonics

### Mnemonic 1: **"GARRC"** — The Five Change Types in Order of Scope

Remember the five change-type definitions by scope of intervention, smallest to largest:

> **R**epair → **A**lteration → **R**enovation → **C**onversion → Addition (**G**rows)

Rewritten as **"RARRC-G"** or visualize it as a **STAIR** going up:

| Step | Term | What Changes |
|------|------|-------------|
| Step 1 (lowest) | **REPAIR** | Nothing new — restore what was |
| Step 2 | **RENOVATION** | Materials improve — same use, same area |
| Step 3 | **ALTERATION** | Configuration changes — same area, possibly new use |
| Step 4 | **CONVERSION** | Use/occupancy changes — building reclassified |
| Step 5 (highest) | **ADDITION** | Building GROWS — height and/or floor area increases |

The stair metaphor works: each step up represents a greater intervention in the building's character, requiring progressively more comprehensive plan review.

---

### Mnemonic 2: **"SASH"** — Site Requirements (Sec. 105)

For site suitability, recall:

**S** — **S**treams / bodies of water (and air pollution sources)
**A** — **A**ctive volcanic sites
**S** — **S**afe distance required from all three
**H** — **H**abitation purpose triggers the requirement

And the three site qualities: **"SHySafe"**
- **S**anitary
- **Hy**gienic
- **Safe**

All three must be met. The site is not "code-compliant" on site suitability if it is safe but not sanitary, or sanitary but not safe.

## 8. Active Recall Questions

### Question 1
*[LEA Area 1: Professional Practice | Subtopic: Scope and Policy]*

Which of the following BEST describes the function of the National Building Code of the Philippines (PD 1096) with respect to construction standards?

- A. It establishes recommended guidelines that architects should follow when designing buildings.
- B. It provides a framework of minimum standards and requirements to regulate building construction and use.
- C. It sets the maximum allowable standards for all building materials and structural systems.
- D. It prescribes optimal standards for buildings to ensure the highest quality of construction in the Philippines.

> **Correct Answer: B**
> Per PD 1096, Sec. 102 (and IRR Sec. 102), the Code provides "a framework of minimum standards and requirements." Option A is wrong because the Code is legally mandatory, not advisory. Option C is wrong because the Code sets minimums, not maximums. Option D is wrong because "optimal" implies the best possible — the Code sets the legal floor, not the ceiling of quality. Minimum standards are enforceable; exceeding them is permitted but not required by the Code.

### Question 2
*[LEA Area 1: Professional Practice | Subtopic: Definitions — Sec. 106]*

An existing two-storey office building undergoes work that includes: (1) replacement of all interior partition walls with new lightweight steel-framed drywall partitions in different locations, (2) relocation of the main entrance door, and (3) installation of a new HVAC system — with no change in the building's height or gross floor area and no change in its occupancy classification. Under IRR Sec. 106, this work is classified as:

- A. Addition, because new structural elements (steel-framed partitions) are being installed.
- B. Renovation, because the value and aesthetic quality of the building will improve.
- C. Alteration, because materials, partitions, and utilities are being changed without increasing height or floor area.
- D. Conversion, because the interior configuration is significantly different from the original.

> **Correct Answer: C**
> Per IRR Sec. 106, an Alteration is defined as changes in materials, partitioning, location/size of openings, structural parts, utilities, or equipment — that does NOT increase height and/or floor area. The scenario meets all elements of Alteration exactly. Option A is wrong because an Addition requires an increase in height or floor area, which is absent. Option B is wrong because Renovation specifically refers to changes increasing value/quality/aesthetic — while there may be aesthetic improvement, the defining classification is the nature of the physical changes, which fall squarely within Alteration. Option D is wrong because Conversion requires a change in USE or OCCUPANCY, which is absent here.

### Question 3
*[LEA Area 3: Site Planning | Subtopic: IBC vs. PD 1096 — Scope Exceptions]*

Under the 2004 Revised IRR of PD 1096, which of the following building types is explicitly EXEMPT from the coverage of the National Building Code?

- A. A socialized housing subdivision project developed under BP 220
- B. A three-storey residential condominium in a mixed-use development
- C. A factory building owned and operated by a government-owned corporation
- D. A single-family residence built using modern materials in a rural area

> **Correct Answer: A**
> Per IRR Sec. 103, buildings covered by BP 220 (Economic and Socialized Housing) are explicitly exempt from PD 1096 and are instead governed by HLURB/DHSUD standards. This exception appears in the 2004 Revised IRR but NOT in the original text of PD 1096 Sec. 103 — the IRR adds this exemption. Option B is wrong because condominium buildings are not exempt regardless of occupancy type or location. Option C is wrong because GOCCs are not among the two statutory exceptions. Option D is wrong because modern-materials single-family residences are covered by the Code; only "traditional indigenous family dwellings" are exempt, not ordinary rural residences. Note: under IBC (USA), the comparable exception covers detached 1-2 family dwellings ≤3 stories, which are redirected to the IRC — a structural difference from the Philippine approach.

## 9. Cross-Referencing Section

Rule I is the definitional anchor for the entire PD 1096 IRR. The following cross-references are the most examination-relevant:

### Rule I → Rule II (Administration and Enforcement)
The "Building Official" and "Office of the Building Official" definitions in Sec. 106 are the foundation for Rule II's detailed provisions on OBO powers, jurisdiction, and the building official's authority to issue stop-work orders, assess fines, and order demolition. The "Secretary" and "Department" definitions connect to DPWH's supervisory role over building officials nationwide.

### Rule I → Rule III (Permits and Inspections)
Every permit-triggering activity defined in Sec. 106 — Construction, Alteration, Repair, Renovation, Conversion, Addition, Moving, Demolition — generates a specific permit requirement under Rule III. The precise classification of work under Sec. 106 determines which permit form, which required documents, and which professional signatories are needed. The "Building Permit" definition in Sec. 106 is the exact instrument Rule III operationalizes.

### Rule I → Rule VII (Occupancy Classification)
The "Conversion" definition in Sec. 106 links directly to Rule VII's occupancy group classifications. A conversion permit triggers a Rule VII evaluation: which occupancy group did the building belong to, which group will it belong to, and what requirements differ between the two? The "different requirements" qualifier in the Conversion definition points to Rule VII as the comparator.

### Rule I → Rule VIII (Light, Ventilation, and Site Development)
Section 105's site requirements (sanitary, hygienic, safe; distance from hazards) provide the policy basis for Rule VIII's site development provisions, including setbacks, open spaces, and ventilation requirements. The MCPD (Maximum Allowable Construction Area or Percentage of Site Occupancy) rules in Rule VIII implement the broader site suitability mandate of Sec. 105.

### Rule I → Rule IV (Types of Construction)
The "Construction" definition in Sec. 106 — encompassing all on-site work from site preparation through assembly and equipment installation — connects to Rule IV's classification of construction types (I through V). The type of construction determines the fire-resistive requirements (Rule VI) and the range of materials permitted.

### Rule I → RA 9266 (Architecture Act of 2004)
The definition of "Applicant/Permittee" and the requirement for design professionals to sign and notarize application forms (per "Application Forms" definition) connects to RA 9266, which mandates that an architect sign and seal architectural plans for buildings requiring permits. The IRR's "Application Forms" definition specifies notarization by the design professional — RA 9266 specifies which profession seals which document.

### Rule I → RA 9514 (Fire Code of the Philippines)
The "Conversion" definition triggers a change-of-occupancy review under both PD 1096 and RA 9514. The Bureau of Fire Protection (BFP) issues its own Fire Safety Inspection Certificate (FSIC), which is a prerequisite for the Certificate of Occupancy. A conversion that changes fire occupancy classification (e.g., from residential to assembly use) requires a new FSIC application.

### Rule I → PD 1067 (Water Code of the Philippines)
Section 105's requirement for safe distance from bodies of water is numerically operationalized by PD 1067, which prescribes easements along navigable and non-navigable waterways. These easement distances (3 m for non-navigable streams, 20 m for rivers in urban areas, etc.) define the minimum "safe distance" referenced in Sec. 105.

### Rule I → BP 220 and DHSUD Standards
The Sec. 103 exemption for BP 220 buildings creates a parallel regulatory universe administered by DHSUD (formerly HLURB). Understanding where PD 1096 ends and BP 220 begins is critical for professional practice in socialized housing projects, which constitute a significant portion of Philippine construction volume.
