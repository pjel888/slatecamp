# Embodied carbon questions should be answered through material life-cycle evidence

## What You Need to Know for the LEA

The audited source supports a conservative, source-safe framing: embodied energy is the energy used to harvest, manufacture, prepare, and transport materials to a construction site. Claims about embodied carbon factors, certification points, or percentage reductions need a specific audited standard or product declaration before they can be used.

## Core Concept

- Green Building Illustrated defines embodied energy as the energy required to harvest, manufacture, prepare, and transport materials to site.
- Material efficiency comes first: minimize new material use, then reuse and recycle where appropriate.
- Concrete can contain recycled aggregate, fly ash, or slag, and previously used concrete and steel can be sorted and processed for reuse.
- Recycled content is not automatically the best answer; tradeoffs include chemical content, durability, and embodied energy.
- Local or regional materials can reduce transportation-related embodied energy when they are suitable for the application.

## How It Is Tested

- Define embodied energy without inventing product-specific carbon factors.
- Identify recycled aggregate, fly ash, slag, and recycled steel as examples of material-resource strategies.
- Recognize that local sourcing helps mainly by reducing transport energy, not by automatically making a material structurally acceptable.
- Reject unsupported green-rating or carbon-per-kilogram claims unless the exact source is supplied.

## Common Mistakes

- Do not equate recycled content with zero embodied impact.
- Do not assume a percentage replacement equals the same percentage carbon reduction.
- Do not use certification-point claims without an audited rating-system source.
