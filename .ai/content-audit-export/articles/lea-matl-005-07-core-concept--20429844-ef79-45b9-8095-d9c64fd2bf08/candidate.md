## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal

Define embodied carbon and explain how material extraction, manufacturing, transportation, and installation contribute to a building's total carbon footprint, and identify strategies to reduce embodied carbon through material selection and specification.
## Core Concept

Embodied carbon measures greenhouse gas emissions generated during material extraction, processing, manufacturing, transport, and end-of-life disposal. Low-embodied-carbon materials either reduce emissions through efficient processing (recycled steel via electric arc furnace) or substitute high-impact materials with waste products (fly ash replacing Portland cement). The LEA focuses on three strategies: material substitution, recycling processes, and carbon sequestration in organic materials.

Recycled steel produced via electric arc furnace (EAF) generates approximately 0.9-1.1 kg CO2e per kg of material versus 1.8-2.0 kg CO2e per kg via blast oxygen furnace (BOF), representing roughly 50 percent lower emissions. Fly ash (Class F or Class C per ASTM C618) replaces 15-30 percent of Portland cement in concrete, reducing embodied carbon by 20-40 percent. Timber sequesters carbon during growth, storing approximately 0.46 kg CO2e per kg in the material itself.

## Key Provisions

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0001 component="WikiExamTable" status="preserved"]

Class F fly ash is derived from burning anthracite or bituminous coal, contains 50-70 percent silica, and requires 15-25 percent dosage by weight in concrete for pozzolanic reaction. Class C fly ash (from lignite or subbituminous coal) contains 20-40 percent calcium oxide, self-cements as well as reacts pozzolani­cally, and is dosed at 20-35 percent. Ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS) is a cooled byproduct of iron smelting meeting ASTM C989, contributing both pozzolanic and cementitious properties at 30-50 percent replacement rates.

Recycled aggregates (coarse and fine) must meet ASTM C33 gradation limits and are acceptable for non-structural applications (fill, base course) when absorption and durability are verified. Reclaimed wood from demolished structures is reused as framing timber when no chemical treatment is detected; treated wood reclamation requires specialized processing to avoid contamination.

Recycled plastic lumber is manufactured from post-consumer polyethylene or polypropylene, does not meet structural loading standards of ASTM D2105, and is primarily used for non-structural applications (decking, signage, landscaping components).

## Philippine Construction Context

The Philippines generates approximately 2.78 million tonnes of coal fly ash annually from power plants in Calaca (Batangas) and Mauban (Quezon) operated under PSALM (Power Sector Assets and Liabilities Management Corporation). Pozzolanic Philippines Inc. operates a classifying plant at Calaca Power Plant, supplying low-ignition fly ash meeting local specifications. Adoption of fly ash concrete reduces both embodied carbon and material costs, aligning with Green Building Code provisions promoting waste utilization.

DENR (Department of Environment and Natural Resources) timber harvest restrictions and DPWH (Department of Public Works and Highways) acceptance of alternative materials have driven increased specification of coco lumber, bamboo, and recycled aggregate in construction. Rice hull ash, a byproduct of rice milling, serves as a secondary pozzolan in some regional specifications, though standardization remains inconsistent across provinces.

BERDE (Building for Ecologically Responsive Design Excellence) version 5 allocates 8-9 percent of points to sustainable materials credits, with documented embodied carbon reductions earning certification points. Buildings achieving 15-20 percent reduction in structural embodied carbon may qualify for additional green building tax incentives under RA 9729 (Climate Change Act) provisions.

## How It's Tested

LEA candidates must identify material types by embodied carbon value, match pozzolanic materials to ASTM specifications, calculate percentage reductions in concrete embodied carbon when fly ash or GGBS replaces Portland cement, and apply replacement rate limits. Questions typically present a structural design scenario requiring 30 percent cost reduction while maintaining strength; the correct answer involves substituting 25 percent fly ash (within ASTM C618 Class F limits) without compromising concrete compressive strength or durability.

A second test approach compares environmental product declarations (EPDs) for equivalent materials: candidates select the option with lowest cradle-to-gate embodied carbon or recognize that recycled steel embodied carbon is roughly half that of primary steel. Calculation questions require converting percentage replacement (25% fly ash) to embodied carbon reduction using provided baseline values (e.g., cement = 0.82 kg CO2e/kg; fly ash = 0.15 kg CO2e/kg; reduction = ~37%).
