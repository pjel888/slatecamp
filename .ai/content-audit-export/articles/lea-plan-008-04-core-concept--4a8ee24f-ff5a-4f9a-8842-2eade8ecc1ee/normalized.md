## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal
Apply participatory planning methods — community engagement, dialogue facilitation, consensus-building, local knowledge integration — to community development scenarios, distinguish top-down from bottom-up approaches, and apply RA 7160 participation mandates.

Participatory planning and community-led development center the voices and agency of residents—especially marginalized communities—in planning decisions. Rather than planners imposing plans from above, participatory approaches build plans through dialogue, consensus-building, and the integration of local knowledge. This is both a legal mandate (RA 7160 Local Government Code, RA 9266 Architecture Act of 2004 (Environmental Planner profession)) and a theoretical foundation for equitable, legitimate urban planning.

## Core Concept

Participatory planning reframes the planner's role from technical expert imposing optimal solutions to facilitator of community dialogue. The foundational theories include:

1. **Advocacy Planning** (Paul Davidoff, 1965) — planners serve as advocates for underrepresented communities, develop competing plans reflecting diverse interests, and ensure multiple voices shape planning outcomes rather than a single expert plan.

2. **Communicative Planning** (Patsy Heleay, 1990s) — planning emerges through dialogue and deliberation among stakeholders; truth and legitimacy come from shared understanding, not technical expertise alone.

3. **Participatory Rural Appraisal (PRA) tools** (Robert Chambers, 1983) — community-led assessment methods (mapping, ranking, timeline construction, focus groups) that surface local knowledge and priorities.

In practice, participatory planning uses methods like:

- **Design charrettes** — intensive, facilitated collaborative design workshops where planners, community members, property owners, and other stakeholders work together to develop design concepts and plans.
- **Community mapping** — residents map assets, problems, and priorities on large-format maps, creating spatial representation of local knowledge.
- **Focus groups and interviews** — structured dialogue capturing community values, concerns, and vision.
- **Participatory budgeting** — residents directly decide how public funds are allocated to community projects.

In the Philippines, participatory planning is mandated through the Sanggunian (municipal council) consultations required by RA 7160 and the Environmental Planner's role under RA 10587. CLUPs must incorporate community input; planning cannot be done to communities, only with them.

## Philippine Context

RA 7160's devolution of planning power to LGUs inherently makes participatory planning necessary—municipalities cannot impose plans on barangays; they must work through consultation and consensus. Similarly, RA 9266 (Architecture Act of 2004) creation of the Environmental Planner profession places the responsibility for participatory CLUP development squarely on licensed professionals. In practice, Philippine CLUPs vary widely in participation depth: some incorporate genuine stakeholder dialogue; others conduct cursory consultations. Best practice CLUPs integrate PRA tools, design charrettes, and ongoing community monitoring into the planning cycle. Community-led development—where residents or communities lead development projects rather than external developers—is growing through programs like the Community Mortgage Program (SHFC) and in-situ upgrading of informal settlements.

## How It's Tested

1. **Theory application** — "Why did Paul Davidoff advocate for advocacy planning?" → Answer: To ensure that underrepresented communities have planners advocating for their interests, not just planners imposing expert plans. Tests understanding of the equity motivation behind advocacy planning.

2. **Method application** — "A barangay is developing its land use plan using PRA tools. Which of the following is a PRA tool?" → Options: focus groups, participatory mapping, community ranking of priorities. Tests knowledge of specific participatory methods.

3. **Design charrette scenario** — "A municipality is planning a new mixed-use waterfront district. What is the advantage of a design charrette over a traditional planning process?" → Answer: A charrette brings all stakeholders (residents, business owners, city planners, developers) into the same room simultaneously to develop design concepts together, rather than sequential stakeholder consultations that may miss integration opportunities. Tests understanding of the charrette method's specific value.

**Exam Tip:** Arnstein's Ladder of Citizen Participation: (1) Manipulation, (2) Therapy, (3) Informing, (4) Consultation, (5) Placation, (6) Partnership, (7) Delegated Power, (8) Citizen Control. Rungs 1-2 = non-participation, 3-5 = tokenism, 6-8 = citizen power. Know the rungs.
