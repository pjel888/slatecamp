## BERDE Rating System—Philippine Green Building Standard and the Architect's Role

BERDE (Building for Ecologically Responsive Design Excellence) is the Philippines' national green building rating system developed by the Philippine Green Building Council (PHILGBC). Launched in 2010 and continuously updated, BERDE provides a framework for measuring environmental performance and promoting sustainable design. For architects, BERDE is increasingly a contractual requirement and a competitive differentiator in the market.

### What is BERDE and Its Purpose

BERDE is a voluntary green building standard with separate rating schemes for new construction, existing buildings, renovations, and specific building types (educational institutions, commercial offices, residential, industrial). The rating system evaluates environmental performance across categories including site planning, water efficiency, energy efficiency, indoor environmental quality, materials and resources, and waste management.

BERDE certification recognizes buildings that achieve verified environmental standards. The system uses a credit-based approach: buildings earn points for design features and operational practices, and the total points determine the certification level—Certified, Silver, Gold, or Platinum. Unlike government building codes that set minimum requirements, BERDE targets excellence above baseline.

### BERDE for Educational Institutions—The Most Relevant Category

The BERDE-NC-EDU (New Construction—Educational Institutions) scheme version 1.1.0 (2013) is extensively used in Philippine school and university projects. This category requires architects to address site sustainability, building envelope efficiency, lighting design, water conservation, material sourcing, waste reduction during construction, and indoor air quality in classrooms and learning spaces.

Educational buildings demand careful attention to natural light (to reduce eye strain in student populations), acoustic performance (to prevent noise interference in classrooms), indoor air quality (to maintain student focus and health), and daylighting (to reduce energy consumption). BERDE-EDU credits reward design decisions that balance pedagogical needs with environmental responsibility.

### The Architect's Role in BERDE Certification

The architect is central to BERDE achievement. Many credits depend on design decisions made during schematic and design development phases. Site selection (walkability to transit, green space preservation) is set at the beginning. Building envelope design (orientation, window-to-wall ratio, thermal mass) determines energy performance. Material specification affects embodied carbon and waste impacts.

The architect must: (1) understand BERDE credit requirements from project inception; (2) integrate green design objectives into the design philosophy, not as an afterthought; (3) coordinate with structural, MEP, and landscape consultants to optimize system interactions (e.g., daylighting reduces lighting loads; efficient MEP systems reduce energy demand); and (4) document design decisions to support credit claims during certification.

### Integration with the National Building Code

BERDE is complementary to, not a replacement for, the National Building Code (NBC). The NBC establishes minimum safety, accessibility, and structural standards. BERDE targets excellence beyond the baseline. A building can meet NBC requirements yet fail to achieve BERDE certification if it does not implement green design features. Conversely, achieving BERDE certification demonstrates that the design exceeds baseline code requirements.

Architects should view BERDE and NBC compliance as overlapping mandates. Structural safety (NBC) does not conflict with energy efficiency (BERDE); accessibility (NBC, BP 344) aligns with BERDE's inclusive design principles. Universal design supports inclusive indoor environmental quality.

### Key BERDE Categories and Architectural Implications

_Site_ credits reward choosing locations with transit access, preserving green space, and managing stormwater on-site. Architects influence these through master planning and site layout. _Water_ credits address fixture efficiency, rainwater harvesting, and greywater reuse. MEP consultants specify fixtures, but architects determine where systems can be located and designed. _Energy_ credits focus on envelope performance, daylighting, HVAC efficiency, and renewable energy. This is the largest credit category.

_Indoor Environmental Quality_ addresses daylight access, views to nature, acoustic performance, and indoor air quality. Architects design window placement, spatial planning, and building form. _Materials and Resources_ rewards sustainable sourcing, local materials, and waste reduction. Architects make material selections; contractors manage construction waste. _Waste_ credits address demolition waste management and operational waste reduction.

### BERDE Certification Process and Timeline

BERDE certification is typically pursued in two phases: design phase review (validating that the design targets BERDE performance) and operational review (after occupancy, confirming that the building performs as designed). The architect usually prepares a BERDE submission during design development or contract documents, documenting how the design addresses each applicable credit.

The PHILGBC reviews submissions, requests clarifications, and issues a preliminary rating. Final certification depends on post-occupancy performance verification. This timeline should be built into the project schedule; the architect should budget time for BERDE documentation preparation.

### Architect Liability and BERDE Performance Guarantees

BERDE credits claim that design features will achieve certain environmental benefits. If the design claims daylighting benefits but actual daylighting is insufficient due to shading or window design error, the architect may bear responsibility for the credit not being achieved. Documentation is critical—the architect should note design assumptions, site conditions, and the basis for credit claims.

Architects should not guarantee specific BERDE certification levels if external factors (such as operational practices or occupant behavior) affect performance. For example, energy certification depends partly on HVAC operation; the architect can design an efficient system but cannot guarantee that operators maintain it properly. Service agreements should clarify what the architect is responsible for versus what depends on client operations.

### BERDE and Lifecycle Cost Considerations

Green design often requires higher upfront investment in efficient systems, premium materials, or detailed design. However, BERDE-certified buildings typically realize operational savings through reduced energy, water, and waste costs. The architect should educate clients about lifecycle costs and payback periods. A 5% higher design cost may be offset by 30% lower operating costs over 20 years.

Many institutional clients (schools, government facilities) are now prioritizing BERDE certification to demonstrate environmental stewardship and reduce long-term operational costs. Private developers recognize that BERDE-certified buildings command market premiums and attract tenants committed to sustainability. The architect's role is to help the client understand that green design is an investment, not an expense.
