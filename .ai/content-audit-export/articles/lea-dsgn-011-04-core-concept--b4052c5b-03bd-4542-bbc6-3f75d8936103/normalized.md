## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal
Distinguish one-point, two-point, and three-point perspective types, identify vanishing points and horizon lines, compare perspective with axonometric and orthographic projections, and apply visualization techniques for client presentations.

Perspective drawings and visualization techniques translate 2D orthographic projections (plans, elevations, sections) into 3D spatial representations. The LEA expects you to understand perspective types, identify vanishing points, and recognize how perspective differs from axonometric and orthographic views.

## Core Concept

A **perspective drawing** is a three-dimensional representation that mimics how the human eye perceives space: objects farther away appear smaller, parallel lines converge toward vanishing points, and depth is communicated through relative size and overlap. Perspective is the most intuitive visualization for clients and stakeholders because it closely matches the experience of being in a space.

**Perspective types are defined by the number of vanishing points:**

1. **One-point perspective** (1VP) is used when viewing an object straight-on, with one face parallel to the picture plane. The other receding faces converge to a single vanishing point on the horizon line. This is effective for interior corridor views, looking down streets, or frontal building facades. The parallel edges of the front face remain parallel on the drawing.

2. **Two-point perspective** (2VP) is used when viewing an object at an angle, such as a corner of a building. Two visible faces recede to two different vanishing points on the horizon line. This is the most common perspective for exterior building presentations because it shows more dimensional information and appears more realistic.

3. **Three-point perspective** (3VP) introduces a vertical vanishing point, making all lines converge—including vertical lines. This is used for dramatic views of tall buildings or when the viewpoint is elevated far above or below the object (bird's eye or worm's eye view). It is less commonly used in architectural practice because it introduces distortion that can appear unrealistic unless the extreme viewpoint is justified.

**Key perspective elements:**

- **Horizon line**: A horizontal line representing the observer's eye level. All receding lines that are parallel in reality converge to vanishing points on this line (in 1VP and 2VP).
- **Vanishing point (VP)**: The location on the horizon line where receding parallel lines converge.
- **Station point (SP)**: The position of the observer (camera) in space. Moving the SP changes the viewpoint and the perspective image.
- **Picture plane (PP)**: An imaginary vertical plane through which the observer sees the scene. The PP is where the perspective drawing is projected.

**Perspective vs. Axonometric:**
- **Perspective** mimics human vision; distant objects diminish; has vanishing points. More intuitive for spatial understanding but less useful for accurate dimension extraction.
- **Axonometric** (isometric, dimetric, trimetric) is a parallel projection where receding lines remain parallel and do not converge. Scales are consistent, making dimensions easier to extract, but the view is less intuitive than perspective. Axonometric is often used for technical or exploded views.

**Perspective in exam contexts:**
The LEA tests whether candidates can:
1. Identify perspective type from a drawing (1VP, 2VP, or 3VP).
2. Locate vanishing points and horizon lines.
3. Understand the relationship between viewpoint (station point) and the resulting perspective image.
4. Recognize perspective distortion and when it is appropriate to use (dramatic views) versus when to use axonometric or orthographic views (technical coordination).
5. Construct basic perspectives from orthographic projections (plan and elevation).

## Key Provisions

| Concept | Definition | Use Case |
|---------|-----------|----------|
| **One-point perspective** | Single vanishing point; front face parallel to picture plane | Interior corridors, street views, direct frontal building views |
| **Two-point perspective** | Two vanishing points; building shown at corner angle | Exterior building presentations, site context views |
| **Three-point perspective** | Three vanishing points (two horizontal, one vertical) | Tall building drama, bird's/worm's eye views (rare in practice) |
| **Horizon line** | Eye level line on which vanishing points lie | Defines observer's height and viewing angle |
| **Station point** | Observer position in 3D space | Controls viewpoint, perspective angle, and composition |
| **Axonometric (isometric)** | Parallel projection, no vanishing points, parallel lines remain parallel | Technical details, exploded assembly views, dimension clarity |
| **Orthographic (plan, elevation, section)** | True 2D projections, no perspective distortion, true to scale | Construction documentation, regulatory compliance verification |

**Construction methods:**
- **Perspective from plan-elevation method**: Construct a perspective grid from an orthographic plan and elevation, then project points onto the perspective plane to locate building edges and features. This is the traditional hand-drawing method.
- **Digital method**: Use 3D modeling software (SketchUp, Revit, Rhino) to create a digital model and render perspectives from chosen viewpoints.

## Philippine Practice Context

In the Philippines, perspective drawings are common in design presentations to developers, government agencies (BPBD, PIA), and the public to communicate design intent and massing. The typical presentation uses 2VP perspective at eye level (approximately 1.5 m above finished grade) showing the building in context with surrounding streets and structures. Tropical light and shadow are often exaggerated in perspectives to communicate the design response to sun angles and ventilation. Perspectives should reflect the Philippine urban context—showing street-level activity, vegetation, and typical building adjacencies.

## How It's Tested

**Pattern 1: Identifying perspective type and vanishing points**
A perspective drawing is shown. The candidate must identify it as 2VP, locate the two vanishing points on the horizon line, and explain why this viewpoint was chosen (e.g., to show building corner and street frontage simultaneously).

**Pattern 2: Perspective vs. orthographic distinction**
A candidate is asked when to use perspective versus axonometric versus orthographic views. The correct answer recognizes that perspective is best for spatial communication but axonometric or orthographic is better for technical accuracy and code compliance verification.

**Pattern 3: Station point and viewpoint analysis**
A perspective shows a building from street level, eye-level height. The candidate must explain why this viewpoint was chosen (human scale, pedestrian experience) and how moving the station point (elevating it or moving it laterally) would change the image.
