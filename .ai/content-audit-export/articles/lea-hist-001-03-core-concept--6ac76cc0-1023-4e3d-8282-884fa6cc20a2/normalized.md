# Achaemenid Persian Architecture

## LEA Point
Achaemenid Persian architecture is imperial architecture: elevated terraces, columned audience halls, monumental gates, animal capitals, relief programs, and palace landscapes used to express empire. The safest LEA anchors are Persepolis, Pasargadae, and Susa.

## Source Anchors
- UNESCO Persepolis anchors the Darius-founded terrace and palace complex as the central ceremonial reference.
- UNESCO Pasargadae anchors Cyrus II, the first dynastic capital, palaces/gardens, and the Tomb of Cyrus.
- UNESCO Susa anchors Achaemenid palatial and administrative layers in a long-lived city.
- Banister Fletcher's local conversion anchors good stone near Susa/Persepolis, palace platforms, columned halls, animal capitals, relief programs, and architectural synthesis from neighboring traditions.

## Core Pattern
The Persian apadana or columned audience-hall idea is not a Greek temple with a different label. It uses many interior columns to carry a roof over ceremonial space. Reliefs on stairs and platforms communicate imperial order, tribute, and subject peoples. Gates and animal capitals continue Western Asiatic guardian and symbolic traditions while adapting them into an Achaemenid court language.

## Exam Guardrails
- If the item describes a large columned Persian audience hall, prefer apadana over generic Greek temple terms.
- Do not claim Persians used stone only because it was cheaper; the local source supports stone availability near major palace centers.
- Do not use exact dimensions unless the article or local source explicitly anchors them.
- Fix the common typo: Alexander the Great, not Leaxander.

## Real World Scenario
A plan-review item describes an elevated terrace, a monumental stair, reliefs of subject delegations, and a large columned hall. The source-safe classification is Achaemenid imperial palace architecture at Persepolis, not a Greek peripteral temple or an Egyptian pyramid complex.
