# Change of Use and Occupancy

---

## Learning Goal

By the end of this article, you will be able to:

- Identify when a change of use or occupancy triggers PD 1096 Rule VII, Sec. 702 requirements
- Apply the "less hazardous" test (life risk + fire risk) to determine whether full code compliance is needed
- Trace the approval process from application through inspection to document issuance
- Distinguish between the Certificate of Occupancy (new construction, Sec. 309) and the "other Certificate of Occupancy" issued for an approved change of use
- Correctly identify occupancy groups under IRR Section 701 — especially Groups D, E, G, and H which are commonly confused

---

## Why This Matters for the LEA

The change-of-use topic sits squarely in **Area 1 (Professional Practice)** — approximately 30% of LEA exam weight. It tests both **procedural knowledge** (what form to file, who approves, what document is issued) and **conceptual knowledge** (hazard comparison, Building Official authority, occupancy classification). Expect at least 2-3 questions on this topic across the Professional Practice and Design subtests.

This is also a **real-world daily practice issue**. In Philippine cities with large stocks of aging buildings, adaptive reuse is the rule, not the exception. Your clients will routinely ask: "Can I turn this old apartment into a boarding house? Can I convert the ground floor into a pharmacy?" Knowing the answer — and the process — is part of what it means to practice competently.

---

## You Are Here

Imagine you are reviewing plans for a 1980s building in Cebu. The current Certificate of Occupancy says "Group D — Institutional" (the original use was a community clinic). The new owner wants to open a 15-room bed and breakfast (Group B — Residentials, Hotels and Apartments). The owner says: "The building was already inspected when it was built. Why do I need anything from the Building Official again?"

**Stop. This is the moment where the exam separates candidates who understand Sec. 702 from those who don't.** A change of occupancy is a regulated act under PD 1096 regardless of whether the building already has a valid Certificate of Occupancy. The building is moving from one occupancy group (D) to another (B). That triggers the full Sec. 702 process — application, hazard assessment, inspection, and a new Certificate of Occupancy for the new use.

## The Legal Basis

<LawCallout code="nbcp" section="Sec. 702">

**PD 1096, Rule VII, Section 702 — Change of Use or Occupancy**

No change in the character of occupancy or use of any building that places it in a different division of the same group or a different occupancy group shall be made unless the building is made to comply with all requirements of the Code for such new division or group. The Building Official shall determine whether the proposed new use is less hazardous (based on life and fire risk) than the existing use. If the new use is less hazardous, the Building Official may approve the change. If it is equally or more hazardous, full code compliance for the new occupancy is required before approval.

Additionally, the 2004 Revised IRR (page 48, line 12) states: *"A building for which a Certificate of Occupancy has been issued may further be issued other Certificates of Occupancy due to changes in use."* This confirms that an approved change of use results in an additional Certificate of Occupancy — not a separate "Certificate of Use" document, though the term is sometimes used administratively.

</LawCallout>

## Key Requirements

### 1. Occupancy Classification Under IRR Section 701

Every change-of-use analysis begins with properly classifying both the **existing** and **proposed** occupancy. The IRR Section 701 defines ten groups. Memorize these:

| Group | Full Name | Examples |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| **Group A** | Residential Dwellings | Single-family houses, duplexes, townhouses |
| **Group B** | Residentials, Hotels and Apartments | Hotels, apartment buildings, dormitories, boarding houses |
| **Group C** | Education and Recreation | Schools, museums, libraries, gymnasiums |
| **Group D** | Institutional | Hospitals, clinics, churches, rehabilitation centers, jails |
| **Group E** | Business and Mercantile | Offices, banks, shops, markets, retail stores |
| **Group F** | Industrial | Factories, plants, mills, workshops |
| **Group G** | Storage and Hazardous | Warehouses, storage yards, hazardous materials storage |
| **Group H** | Assembly Other Than Group I | Theaters, churches, restaurants, nightclubs (occupant load under 1000) |
| **Group I** | Assembly Occupant Load 1000+ | Stadiums, convention centers, coliseums (occupant load 1000 or more) |
| **Group J** | Accessory | Fences, signs, tanks, towers, sheds |

<Warn>

**Common confusion:** The exam frequently tests these mislabel pairs:
- Group G is **Storage and Hazardous** — NOT "Mercantile" (Mercantile is Group E)
- Group E is **Business and Mercantile** — NOT a storage or industrial group
- Group H is **Assembly Other Than Group I** — NOT "High-hazard" (hazardous materials fall under Group G)
- Group A is **Residential Dwellings** — NOT "Low-cost residential"
- Group D is **Institutional** — NOT "Residential"

</Warn>

### 2. The General Prohibition (Sec. 702)

The foundational rule is expressed as a prohibition: **no change** in occupancy or use that places a building in a different division or group shall be made unless the building complies with all code requirements for the new division or group.

This rule operates in two directions:

- **More hazardous new use** — Full code compliance with the new occupancy is required **before** the change is permitted
- **Less hazardous new use** — The Building Official **may** approve the change even if the building does not fully meet all standards for the new use, because life and fire risk are reduced, not increased

<Tip>

**Memory aid: "MORE = COMPLY, LESS = MAY APPROVE"**

- **More** hazardous (or same hazard) -> must **comply** fully before changing
- **Less** hazardous -> Building Official **may approve** without full retrofitting

The "less hazardous" pathway is the primary compliance route for older Philippine buildings. It acknowledges that full retrofitting may be economically impractical, but safety is enhanced — not diminished — when occupancy moves down the hazard scale.

</Tip>

### 3. The Hazard Assessment: Life Risk + Fire Risk

The code's test is binary: the new use must represent a lower level of **life risk** AND **fire risk** compared to the existing use. The Building Official makes this determination based on both factors considered together.

The general hazard hierarchy (from most to least hazardous):

1. **Group G** — Storage and Hazardous (hazardous materials, explosives, flammables)
2. **Group F** — Industrial (factories, heavy equipment)
3. **Group I** — Assembly Large (very high occupant loads)
4. **Group H** — Assembly Other (moderate to high occupant loads, public gatherings)
5. **Group C** — Education and Recreation (schools, vulnerable population)
6. **Group D** — Institutional (hospitals, jails - limited mobility occupants)
7. **Group E** — Business and Mercantile (offices, shops - ambulatory adults)
8. **Group B** — Residentials, Hotels and Apartments (sleeping risk)
9. **Group A** — Residential Dwellings (low density)
10. **Group J** — Accessory (incidental structures)

<Warn>

**Important:** The comparison is specific to the sub-groups involved and the actual conditions of the building, not a general category comparison. Moving from Group D-2 (clinic) to Group B-1 (hotel) requires the Building Official to assess whether the hotel is less hazardous — which depends on occupant load, egress, and fire safety provisions. Always anchor your analysis to the specific sub-groups and the actual risk profile.

</Warn>

### 4. The Process: Application, Assessment, Inspection, Issuance

**Step 1 — Application:** The building owner or authorized representative (architect/engineer of record) files an **Application for Change of Use or Occupancy**.

> **Note on forms:** The PD 1096 IRR does not explicitly prescribe a specific form number for change-of-use applications within the IRR text pages examined. **NBC Form No. B-12** is widely referenced in DPWH administrative practice and professional literature as the applicable form, but candidates should be aware that exam questions may refer to it by description ("Application for Change of Use or Occupancy") rather than by form number.

**Step 2 — Assessment of Compliance:** The Building Official's office reviews the application and assesses whether the proposed new use satisfies Sec. 702 — specifically, whether it is less hazardous (life + fire risk) or whether the building must fully comply with the new occupancy requirements.

**Step 3 — Building Official Inspection:** The Building Official (or authorized inspector) conducts an on-site inspection to verify the building's current condition and confirm compliance with requirements applicable to the proposed new use.

**Step 4 — Issuance of Certificate of Occupancy:** Upon approval, the Building Official issues a **Certificate of Occupancy** for the new use. Per the 2004 Revised IRR: "A building for which a Certificate of Occupancy has been issued may further be issued other Certificates of Occupancy due to changes in use." This is the legally operative document.

<Warn>

**Critical distinction for the exam:** The IRR does **not** create a separate "Certificate of Use" document distinct from the Certificate of Occupancy. Sec. 702 itself mentions only compliance requirements and Building Official approval — it does not name the document issued. The document issued after an approved change of use is **another Certificate of Occupancy** (sometimes referred to administratively as a "Certificate of Use," but the legal basis in the IRR is for additional Certificates of Occupancy). If an exam question asks what document is issued for a change of use, understand that:
- The IRR says: "other Certificates of Occupancy due to changes in use"
- The original PD 1096 text (page 364) says: "no change... shall be made until the Building Official has issued a **Certificate of Occupancy** therefor"

**Bottom line:** The safest exam answer is "Certificate of Occupancy" — with the understanding that it is a new/additional Certificate of Occupancy for the changed use, not an amended or reissued original.

</Warn>

### 5. Building Official Authority is Absolute

No change of use is valid under PD 1096 without **Building Official approval**. The owner, architect, and engineer cannot self-certify a change of use. The Building Official has the authority to:

- Require full code compliance for the new use before approval, if the new use is more hazardous
- Approve the change with conditions, if the new use is less hazardous but minor upgrades are warranted
- Deny the application if the proposed new use would create non-compliant conditions
- Order cessation of occupancy if a change occurs without proper application and approval

## What the Exam Tests

The LEA typically tests this topic in three ways:

### Type 1: Procedural Sequence
"Which form is filed? Who approves? What document is issued?" These are direct recall questions testing the process flow.

### Type 2: Hazard Comparison
"Given Existing Use X and Proposed Use Y, is the change to a more or less hazardous occupancy?" These require you to apply the occupancy classification system (Sec. 701) and make a relative hazard judgment.

### Type 3: Scenario Analysis with Legal Consequence
"A building owner converted X to Y without Building Official approval. What is the legal consequence?" These test your understanding that the process applies regardless of the owner's opinion about relative hazard.

<ExamTip>

**Exam strategy:** When you see a change-of-use question, immediately note:
1. Is the existing use classified? (If not, classify it under Sec. 701)
2. Is the proposed use classified? (Same)
3. Compare hazard levels: is the new use more hazardous, less hazardous, or the same?
4. Apply the rule:
   - More/same hazardous = full code compliance required first
   - Less hazardous = Building Official may approve
5. Answer: Who files? Who approves? What document is issued?

If the question mentions "Certificate of Use," check whether the IRR language is also referenced — the safest answer anchors to what the code actually says.

</ExamTip>

## Common Misunderstandings

### Misunderstanding 1: "Certificate of Use is a separate legal document from Certificate of Occupancy"

**Not quite accurate.** The IRR states that other Certificates of Occupancy are issued for changes in use. While the term "Certificate of Use" is commonly used in Philippine construction practice, the PD 1096 and its IRR do not establish it as a formally distinct document. The legally operative text refers to additional Certificates of Occupancy.

### Misunderstanding 2: "Group G = Mercantile"

**Wrong.** Group G is **Storage and Hazardous**. Group E is **Business and Mercantile**. These are the most frequently confused groups on the exam.

### Misunderstanding 3: "The less hazardous exception means no code compliance is needed"

**Wrong.** The "less hazardous" standard is the threshold for allowing the change without requiring full retrofitting to the new occupancy's standards. The Building Official still inspects the building, still issues findings, and may still impose conditions. It is not a waiver of all compliance obligations.

### Misunderstanding 4: "Any change between subdivisions of the same group is not regulated"

**Wrong.** Sec. 702 covers changes that place a building in a "different division of the same group" OR a "different occupancy group." Even moving within the same group (e.g., Group D-1 hospital to Group D-2 detention facility) triggers the requirement.

### Misunderstanding 5: "The Certificate of Occupancy section is Section 709"

**Wrong.** Section 709 is "Requirements for Other Group Occupancies." The Certificate of Occupancy is governed by **Section 309** of the IRR.

## Practice Questions

### Question 1 (Recall)

A building in Manila was originally classified as Group E (Business and Mercantile) — a retail store. The owner wants to convert it to a warehouse (Group G — Storage and Hazardous). Under PD 1096 Sec. 702, which of the following is the MOST accurate statement?

- A. The change is automatically allowed because both are non-residential uses under the same general category.
- B. The Building Official must determine whether the warehouse use is less hazardous; if so, a new Certificate of Occupancy may be issued for the new use.
- C. The change is prohibited because Group G always requires full code compliance regardless of the specific hazard comparison.
- D. No application is needed because the owner may self-certify a change within non-residential groups.

> **Answer: B**
>
> Under Sec. 702, the Building Official must assess whether the proposed new use (Group G — Storage and Hazardous) is less hazardous in terms of life and fire risk compared to the existing use (Group E — Business and Mercantile). A warehouse storing non-hazardous goods may be less hazardous than a retail store open to the public (lower occupant load, fewer egress demands). If the Building Official determines the new use is less hazardous, approval may be granted, and per the IRR, another Certificate of Occupancy is issued for the new use. Option A is wrong — the change is not "automatic." Option C is wrong — Group G is not automatically barred; the hazard comparison must be made. Option D is wrong — self-certification is never permitted.

---

### Question 2 (Apply)

An architect's client wants to convert a two-storey building formerly used as a light manufacturing workshop (Group F-1 — Industrial) into a daycare center (Group C — Education and Recreation). The building was constructed under PD 1096 and holds a valid Certificate of Occupancy as Group F-1. Which statement is MOST accurate?

- A. The change is automatically allowed because daycare is a lighter use than manufacturing.
- B. The Building Official must assess whether the daycare use is less hazardous; if approved, the existing Certificate of Occupancy is simply amended.
- C. The Building Official must assess whether the daycare use is less hazardous; a daycare serving children typically involves higher life-safety risk due to vulnerable occupants. If the Building Official determines the new use is more hazardous, full code compliance for Group C is required before approval.
- D. No application is needed because both uses are non-hazardous commercial activities.

> **Answer: C**
>
> A daycare center (Group C) serves children — a vulnerable population requiring stringent egress and fire safety. Compared to light industrial (Group F-1), the daycare almost certainly represents a higher life-safety risk (limited mobility of occupants, higher occupant density relative to floor area, sleeping/playing areas). Under Sec. 702, if the new use is more hazardous, the building must fully comply with all code requirements for Group C before the Building Official may approve. Option B is wrong — the document issued is a new/additional Certificate of Occupancy, not an amendment to the existing one. Options A and D are wrong because the process requires Building Official involvement and approval.

---

### Question 3 (Transfer)

A provincial hospital (Group D — Institutional) built in 1990 with a valid Certificate of Occupancy is being converted into a call center (Group E — Business and Mercantile). The owner argues that a call center is "less hazardous" because it has no medical waste, no contagious patients, and no overnight stays. The Building Official, however, notes that the call center will operate 24 hours a day, 7 days a week with 200 employees per shift. Under Sec. 702:

- A. The owner is correct — the hospital is clearly more hazardous, and the Building Official must approve the change.
- B. The Building Official is correct to assess both life risk and fire risk; the call center's 24-hour operation with 200+ employees may create egress and fire safety demands that require full code compliance for Group E.
- C. Hospitals and call centers are both classified as Group E, so no change-of-use application is needed.
- D. The change requires a new Certificate of Occupancy regardless of hazard level, but only if the building undergoes structural alterations.

> **Answer: B**
>
> This question transfers the Sec. 702 analysis to a realistic scenario where the hazard comparison is not obvious. While Group D (Institutional — hospitals) has certain hazards (medical waste, limited-mobility patients), Group E (Business and Mercantile — call center) has different hazards: high occupant density, 24-hour occupancy, complex egress demands for 200+ people, and potential fire hazards from electronics and continuous operation. The Building Official must assess **both** life risk AND fire risk for the specific building and proposed use. Option A is wrong — the owner's argument pre-judges the outcome. Option C is wrong — hospitals are Group D, not Group E. Option D is wrong — the change-of-use process is triggered by the change in occupancy group, regardless of structural alterations.

## Why This Matters IRL (In Real Life)

You will encounter this scenario within your first year of practice. A client will call and say: "I bought a building. It has a Certificate of Occupancy. I just want to change the use. That's just paperwork, right?"

Your answer determines whether your client avoids fines, cease-and-desist orders, and potentially dangerous conditions. The "just paperwork" assumption is how buildings end up operating as nightclubs without fire exits, or as boarding houses without adequate egress. **The change-of-use process exists because buildings that work safely for one use may be deadly for another.**

When you advise a client, you are not just navigating code — you are protecting lives. The exam tests whether you understand that responsibility.

## Cross-References

- **Rule VII, Sec. 701 (Occupancy Classification):** Required before any Sec. 702 analysis can begin. You must classify both existing and proposed uses.
- **Rule VII, Sec. 703 (Mixed Occupancy):** When a building has multiple uses and only one changes, Sec. 702 and Sec. 703 interact — the new combination must comply with mixed occupancy separation requirements.
- **Section 309 (Certificate of Occupancy):** The correct section governing Certificates of Occupancy for new construction and, per the IRR, for approved changes in use.
- **Section 709 (Requirements for Other Group Occupancies):** NOT about Certificates of Occupancy — this section covers specific technical requirements for Groups H, I, and other assembly-type occupancies.
