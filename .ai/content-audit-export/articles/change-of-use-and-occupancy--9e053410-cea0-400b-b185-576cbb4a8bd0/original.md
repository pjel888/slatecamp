## Hook Scenario

A property owner in Makati approaches you with a straightforward request: she wants to convert a former residential apartment building (classified as Group D — Residential) into a boutique hotel (Group B — Residentials, Hotels). The building was constructed in 1988 and received its Certificate of Occupancy under PD 1096 as a residential building. Now, the owner wants to formally change the use. She assumes the building already has a certificate and that "changing the sign out front" is enough.

You explain that under PD 1096, Rule VII, Sec. 702, a change in occupancy is a regulated act requiring Building Official approval, a formal application on NBC Form No. B-12, and — if approved — a new **Certificate of Use**, which is a separate and distinct document from the original Certificate of Occupancy. More critically, the code requires that the new use be **less hazardous** (in terms of life and fire risk) than the existing use, or that the building fully complies with code requirements for the new occupancy. This is not a paperwork question — it is a substantive safety assessment. Understanding who decides, what form is filed, what certificate is issued, and why "less hazardous" is the controlling standard separates candidates who score in the LEA Professional Practice area from those who do not.

## Core Concepts

### Principle 1 — The General Prohibition: No Unauthorized Upgrade in Hazard Level (Sec. 702)

The foundational rule of Sec. 702 is expressed as a prohibition: **no change in the character of occupancy or use** that places a building in a different division of the same group or a different occupancy group shall be made unless the building complies with all requirements of the National Building Code for the new division or group.

This rule operates in two directions:
- If the change moves the building to a **more hazardous** division or group, full code compliance with the new occupancy is required before the change is permitted.
- If the change moves the building to a **less hazardous** division or group (lower life and fire risk), the Building Official may approve the change even if the building does not fully meet all standards for the new use — because the safety risk is reduced, not increased.

This "less hazardous" pathway is the primary compliance route for existing buildings in the Philippines. It acknowledges that full retrofitting to meet all code requirements for a new use may be economically impractical for older buildings, but that safety is enhanced — not diminished — when occupancy moves down the hazard sclea.

### Principle 2 — The Hazard Assessment: Life Risk and Fire Risk Are the Two Measures

The code's test for "less hazardous" is binary: the new use must represent a lower level of **life risk** AND **fire risk** compared to the existing use. Both factors are considered together. The Building Official makes this determination. The Philippine occupancy classification system under Sec. 701 provides the hazard hierarchy:

From most to least hazardous (general order, fire and life risk combined):
1. **Group H** — High-hazard occupancies (explosives, flammable liquids storage)
2. **Group F / E** — Industrial, storage of moderate hazard
3. **Group C / D / B** — Educational, institutional, residential (varying by sub-group)
4. **Group A** — Low-cost residential (relatively lower density)
5. **Group G** — Mercantile (shops, markets)
6. **Group J** — Miscellaneous (fences, signs, tanks)

The comparison is specific to the sub-groups involved, not a general category comparison. Moving from Group D-2 (apartment, single-family dwelling) to Group J-3 (sign) would be an extreme example of a reduction in hazard. Moving from Group A (single-family house) to Group B (hotel) would require the hotel to be assessed as less hazardous — which it typically is not, triggering the full compliance requirement.

### Principle 3 — The Process: Application, Assessment, Inspection, Certificate (Sec. 702)

The formal process for a change of use under PD 1096 is sequential:

**Step 1 — Application:** The building owner or their authorized representative (typically the architect or engineer of record) files an **Application for Change of Use or Occupancy** using **NBC Form No. B-12** with the Office of the Building Official in the jurisdiction where the building is located.

**Step 2 — Assessment of Compliance:** The Building Official's office reviews the application and assesses whether the proposed new use satisfies Sec. 702 — specifically whether it is less hazardous or whether the building fully complies with the new occupancy requirements.

**Step 3 — Building Official Inspection:** The Building Official (or an authorized inspector) conducts an on-site inspection to verify the building's current condition and confirm compliance with the requirements applicable to the proposed new use.

**Step 4 — Issuance of Certificate of Use:** Upon approval, the Building Official issues a **Certificate of Use** — not a Certificate of Occupancy. The Certificate of Use is the legally operative document for an existing building undergoing a change in use. It is distinct from the Certificate of Occupancy issued for newly constructed buildings upon completion.

### Principle 4 — Certificate of Use vs. Certificate of Occupancy: A Critical Distinction

This distinction is one of the most frequently tested concepts in the Professional Practice area of the LEA:

| Document | When Issued | Context | Governing Section |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Certificate of Occupancy** | After new construction or major renovation is completed | New buildings; first occupancy | PD 1096 Rule VII, Sec. 709 |
| **Certificate of Use** | After Building Official approves change of occupancy | Existing buildings changing use | PD 1096 Rule VII, Sec. 702 |

Both documents are issued by the Building Official. Both authorize occupancy of a building for a specific use. But they are different documents for different circumstances. The Certificate of Occupancy certifies that a new or renovated building was constructed in accordance with approved plans and the NBCP. The Certificate of Use certifies that an existing building's change in use has been reviewed and approved.

### Principle 5 — Building Official Authority is Absolute for This Process

No change of use is valid under PD 1096 without **Building Official approval**. The owner, the architect, and the engineer cannot self-certify a change of use. The Building Official has the authority to:
- Require the building to fully comply with standards for the new use before approval, if the new use is more hazardous.
- Approve the change with conditions, if the new use is less hazardous but minor upgrades are warranted.
- Deny the application if the proposed new use would create non-compliant conditions.

The Building Official's decision is based on both the written application and the physical inspection, and it is the sole authority for issuing the Certificate of Use.

## PH vs. USA Comparative Analysis

The Philippine approach under PD 1096 Sec. 702 is more permissive in allowing existing buildings to change occupancy — but only in the direction of lower hazard. The American approach under the IEBC is more prescriptive and requires compliance with the new occupancy's standards (or an approved alternative compliance method) regardless of direction of change.

| Dimension | Philippines (PD 1096, Rule VII, Sec. 702) | USA (IBC Sec. 3411.4 / IEBC Chapter 10) |
|---|---|---|
| **Core Test** | New use must be LESS HAZARDOUS (life + fire risk) than existing use; OR building must fully comply with code for new use | Building must comply with new occupancy requirements, OR use IEBC alternative compliance method |
| **Direction of Change** | Only allows "upgrade" in hazard without full compliance — if new use is more hazardous, full code compliance required | Full compliance (or alternative method) required in all directions |
| **Building Official Role** | Sole authority for approval; physical inspection required | Authority of Jurisdiction (AHJ) approves; may use code-prescribed alternatives |
| **Form Required** | NBC Form No. B-12 (Application for Change of Use or Occupancy) | Application per local jurisdiction's requirements |
| **Certificate Issued** | Certificate of Use (distinct from Certificate of Occupancy) | Certificate of Occupancy (updated) — no separate "Certificate of Use" in most US jurisdictions |
| **Alternative Compliance** | Not explicitly provided in Sec. 702 — "less hazardous" is the primary pathway | IEBC Chapter 14 (Compliance Alternatives) provides detailed point-based evaluation system |
| **Accessibility** | Assessed for new use compliance | IBC 3411.4.1: partial change → alterations comply with accessibility; IBC 3411.4.2: complete change → at least 1 accessible entrance, route, and toilet |
| **Partial Change** | Sec. 702 does not explicitly address partial change; Building Official applies judgment | IEBC distinguishes partial change (per Section 1007) and full change |
| **Evaluation Method** | Life and fire risk comparison by Building Official | IEBC Chapter 14: building safety score based on fire safety, means of egress, general safety |
| **Key Principle** | Less hazardous = allowed; more hazardous = full compliance required | New occupancy standards apply universally; IEBC offers compliance pathways |

**Key Insight:** The Philippine system's "less hazardous" standard is a uniquely pragmatic rule for a country with a large stock of aging buildings. It prioritizes safety improvement over perfect code compliance. The American IEBC system is more rigorous and less forgiving — it requires full compliance or a scored evaluation, regardless of which direction the hazard changes.

## Cross-Referencing

### Rule VII, Section 701 — Occupancy Classification
Sec. 702 cannot be applied without first establishing the current occupancy group (Sec. 701) and the proposed occupancy group. The occupancy classification system of Sec. 701 defines the hazard hierarchy that the Building Official uses to make the "less hazardous" determination. Every change-of-use analysis begins with a Sec. 701 classification of both the existing and proposed use.

### Rule VII, Section 703 — Mixed Occupancy
When a building has multiple uses (e.g., ground-floor commercial with upper-floor residential), Sec. 703 governs how the different occupancies are separated and classified. A change of use affecting only part of a mixed-occupancy building must be assessed under both Sec. 702 (change of use) and Sec. 703 (mixed occupancy requirements for the new combination). The interaction between these two sections can create complex compliance scenarios.

### Certificate of Occupancy Process
The Certificate of Use (change of use) parallels the Certificate of Occupancy (new construction) in that both are issued by the Building Official and both authorize the legal use of a building. However, they are triggered by different events and documented separately. The Certificate of Occupancy under PD 1096 is issued after a building passes final inspection upon completion. The Certificate of Use is issued after an existing building's change in occupancy is reviewed and approved. Candidates must distinguish these two documents in any exam question that provides context about existing buildings.

### IBC Section 3411.4 and IEBC Chapter 10
The Philippine exam may include comparative questions about the American approach. The IEBC's compliance alternatives (IEBC Chapter 14) provide a point-based scoring system that evaluates a building's fire safety, means of egress, and general safety separately — and allows a building to "pass" even if it does not fully comply with each individual standard, provided its total safety score is adequate. This nuanced approach has no direct equivleant in PD 1096, making it a useful exam trap: candidates may assume the Philippine code has a similar alternative compliance pathway, but Sec. 702 does not include one explicitly.

## Active Learning Techniques

### Memory Aid: "LESS = YES, MORE = COMPLY"

The core rule of Sec. 702 in two parts:
- **LESS** hazardous new use → **YES**, Building Official may approve with Certificate of Use
- **MORE** hazardous new use → must **COMPLY** fully with new occupancy code before approval

### Memory Aid: "B-12 to Certificate of Use — the Change Chain"

Sequence the process as a chain:
**B-12 Form → Assessment → Inspection → Certificate of Use**

- **B-12** = NBC Form No. B-12 (the application)
- **Assessment** = Building Official evaluates life + fire risk
- **Inspection** = Physical site inspection
- **Certificate of Use** = The outcome document (not Certificate of Occupancy)

### Memory Aid: "COU ≠ CO" (Certificate of Use ≠ Certificate of Occupancy)

Certificate of **U**se = existing building, change of **U**se
Certificate of **O**ccupancy = new building, original **O**ccupancy

The letter that changes tells you which building status it applies to.

### If/Then Scenarios

**If** a former warehouse (Group E-2, moderate-hazard storage) is being converted to a grocery store (Group G, mercantile) → **Then** assess whether the mercantile use is less hazardous than moderate-hazard storage. Generally yes — file NBC Form No. B-12, Building Official approval, Certificate of Use.

**If** a residential apartment (Group D) is being converted to a nightclub (Group A-2, assembly for entertainment) → **Then** the nightclub is MORE hazardous (higher occupant load, egress demands, fire risk). Full code compliance for Group A-2 is required before the Building Official may approve.

**If** a property owner self-certifies a change from office to residential without Building Official approval → **Then** the change is illegal under Sec. 702, and the building is operating without a valid Certificate of Use. The Building Official may order cessation of occupancy.

**If** an exam question asks which form to file for change of occupancy in the Philippines → **Then** the answer is NBC Form No. B-12.

**If** the exam asks what document is issued after a change of occupancy is approved → **Then** the answer is the Certificate of **Use** (not Certificate of Occupancy).

## Exam Traps

### Trap 1 — Certificate of Use vs. Certificate of Occupancy

The most reliable exam trap in the change-of-use topic: after an approved change of use, the Building Official issues a **Certificate of Use** — not a Certificate of Occupancy. Many candidates default to "Certificate of Occupancy" because it is the more familiar document. The Certificate of Occupancy is issued after new construction is completed and inspected. The Certificate of Use is the specific instrument for change-of-use approval in existing buildings. Any board question that describes an existing building undergoing a change of use and asks "what document is issued?" has "Certificate of Use" as the correct answer.

### Trap 2 — "Less Hazardous" Applies Only to the Existing Use Comparison

The "less hazardous" exception under Sec. 702 is not a general permission to waive code compliance — it is a comparison between the new use and the **existing use**. The trap answer presents it as a blanket exemption: "If the new use is less hazardous, no code compliance is required." This is incorrect. The Building Official still inspects the building, still issues findings, and may still impose conditions. The "less hazardous" standard is the threshold for allowing the change without requiring the building to be fully upgraded to the new occupancy's standards — not a waiver of all compliance obligations.

### Trap 3 — Form B-12 vs. Other NBCP Forms

The board exam may list several NBCP form numbers and ask which one applies to change of use. The answer is **NBC Form No. B-12** — the Application for Change of Use or Occupancy. Common distractor forms include Form B-1 (building permit application for new construction), Form B-5 (fencing permit), and Form B-7 (demolition permit). If the question involves an existing building and a change in how it is used, the answer is Form B-12.

## Active Recall Questions

### Question 1

An architect's client wants to convert a two-storey building formerly used as a light manufacturing workshop (Group E-1) into a childcare center (Group C — educational/institutional). The building was constructed under PD 1096 and holds a Certificate of Occupancy as Group E-1. Which statement is MOST accurate under PD 1096 Sec. 702?

- A. The change is automatically allowed because childcare is a lighter use than manufacturing.
- B. The Building Official must determine whether the childcare use represents a less hazardous condition (life and fire risk); if approved, a Certificate of Occupancy is issued for the new use.
- C. The Building Official must determine whether the childcare use represents a less hazardous condition; if approved, a Certificate of Use is issued using NBC Form No. B-12 as the application.
- D. No application is needed because the owner may self-certify the change if both uses fall under different occupancy groups.

> **Answer: C**
>
<LawCallout code="nbcp" title="a vulnerable population requiring stringent egress and fire safety — and whether">

Per PD 1096 Sec. 702, a change of occupancy requires Building Official approval. The owner or architect must file NBC Form No. B-12 (Application for Change of Use or Occupancy). The Building Official assesses whether the proposed new use is less hazardous in terms of life and fire risk compared to the existing use. A childcare center (Group C) serves children — a vulnerable population requiring stringent egress and fire safety — and whether it is less hazardous than Group E-1 light manufacturing is a genuine assessment, not automatic. Upon approval, the document issued is a Certificate of Use, not a Certificate of Occupancy (Option B's error). Self-certification (Option D) is not permitted under any provision of PD 1096.

</LawCallout>

### Question 2

A building owner converts a former barangay health center (Group D/F — institutional) into a private dental clinic without filing any application with the Building Official. A neighbor reports the change. Under PD 1096, which of the following is the MOST accurate assessment of the situation?

- A. The conversion is valid because a dental clinic is a less hazardous use than a health center, and no permit is needed.
- B. The conversion is a violation of Sec. 702 — any change that places a building in a different occupancy group or division requires Building Official approval, regardless of the relative hazard of the new use.
- C. The conversion is valid because both uses are health-related and belong to the same occupancy group.
- D. The owner only needs to update the signage and notify the barangay; no Building Official involvement is required.

> **Answer: B**
>
<LawCallout code="nbcp" title="it determines whether full code compliance is needed before approval. Converting">

no change in the character of occupancy that places a building in a different division or group shall be made without compliance with the Code requirements, and Building Official approval is required. The "less hazardous" exception does not eliminate the application and approval requirement — it determines whether full code compliance is needed before approval. Converting without filing NBC Form No. B-12 and obtaining a Certificate of Use is a direct violation of Sec. 702. The Building Official has authority to order the owner to cease operations and file the proper application. Options A, C, and D all incorrectly suggest the conversion can proceed without Building Official action.

</LawCallout>

### Question 3

Under PD 1096, what is the PRIMARY distinction between a Certificate of Occupancy and a Certificate of Use?

- A. A Certificate of Occupancy is issued by the City Mayor; a Certificate of Use is issued by the Building Official.
- B. A Certificate of Occupancy authorizes occupancy of a newly constructed or substantially renovated building; a Certificate of Use authorizes occupancy of an existing building after a Building Official-approved change in use.
- C. A Certificate of Occupancy applies only to residential buildings; a Certificate of Use applies to all other occupancies.
- D. Both documents are identical — "Certificate of Use" is simply the informal term used by Building Officials for existing buildings.

> **Answer: B**
>
> The Certificate of Occupancy and the Certificate of Use are two distinct legal documents issued by the Building Official for different circumstances. The Certificate of Occupancy is issued upon the satisfactory completion and inspection of a new or substantially renovated building, authorizing it for its approved use. The Certificate of Use is the document issued specifically when an existing building undergoes an approved change in occupancy under Sec. 702 — it certifies that the change has been reviewed, found compliant (or less hazardous), and approved. Both are issued by the Building Official (not the Mayor — Option A is wrong). Neither is limited by occupancy type (Option C is wrong). They are emphatically not the same document (Option D is wrong) — conflating them is the most common exam error in this topic.
