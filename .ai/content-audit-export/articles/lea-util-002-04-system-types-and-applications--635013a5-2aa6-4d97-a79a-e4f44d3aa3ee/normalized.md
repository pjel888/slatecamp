# HVAC system types and applications

HVAC system selection in the LEA should be answered from calculated load, building use, zoning, efficiency, control, and maintainability. The checked local sources support this decision method. They do not support market cost tables, live tariff paybacks, or refrigerant phaseout timelines unless those facts are supplied by the question.

## Exam Anchor

The DOE Guidelines require cooling system design loads to be determined using the latest ASHRAE Handbook of Fundamentals procedures or equivalent publications. They also set default comfort design conditions for ordinary air-conditioned spaces: design dry-bulb temperature of 25 deg C, design relative humidity of 55 percent, maximum dry-bulb temperature of 27 deg C, minimum dry-bulb temperature of 23 deg C, maximum relative humidity of 60 percent, and minimum relative humidity of 50 percent. The outdoor design conditions are 35 deg C dry bulb and 27 deg C wet bulb.

The selection question is therefore not "which system is newest?" It is whether the equipment is sized close to the calculated load and matched to the nature of the application, building construction, indoor and outdoor conditions, internal load patterns, controls for efficient energy use, and economic factors.

## Local System-Selection Rules

Air-conditioning systems and equipment should be sized as close as possible to the calculated space and system loads. Engineered systems should have good unloading characteristics and, where appropriate, be arranged in multiple units or capacity increments so they can meet partial and minimum loads without short cycling. The DOE Guidelines give a chiller-count rule for chilled-water systems by capacity range: 700 kW or less needs a minimum of 2 chiller units; above 700 kW to 4218 kW needs a minimum of 3; above 4218 kW to 8787 kW needs a minimum of 4; above 8787 kW depends on the design engineer's judgment.

Centralized monitoring and control should be considered at design stage to achieve optimum operation with minimum energy consumption. Fan and pump systems are also part of the selection: individual VAV fans of 7.5 kW and larger need controls such as variable-speed drives to operate efficiently at reduced flow, and variable-flow pumping systems must be able to reduce flow to 50 percent of design flow or less unless an exception applies.

Air-conditioning equipment performance is measured by EER or kWe/TR as applicable. The designer should consider air-conditioning units with the highest EER label available, especially for window and split types, to secure high cooling capacity with low power consumption.

## System Types In Source-Safe Terms

Small room or unitary systems cool air directly at or near the space. Salvan describes through-the-wall and window-type units as simple air-conditioning devices that draw room air over evaporator coils and reject heat outdoors through the condenser side. The same local source notes practical installation rules for window-type units: condensate must be discharged through a drain pipe or tubing to a suitable container or drainage point, and units along corridors or above the first floor must be installed not less than 2.13 m above the floor level.

Central chilled-water systems suit larger buildings and varied occupancy because the refrigeration plant is centralized, the condenser is cooled by water sent to a cooling tower, and chilled water is pumped to the areas that need cooling. Salvan's key architectural point is that air ducts are large while water piping for the same thermal flow is small, so air-handling centers should be located close to served areas while the chiller plant remains centralized. Central cooling can also keep fan and compressor noise out of occupied spaces.

## Selection Checklist

1. Compute the cooling load by an accepted load-calculation method.
2. Confirm indoor and outdoor design conditions.
3. Match system type to building use, operating schedule, zoning, humidity, and maintenance capacity.
4. Avoid oversizing; provide unloading, staging, or multiple units where the load varies.
5. Check fan, pump, and air-distribution energy criteria.
6. Check EER or kWe/TR performance and available labeling.
7. Coordinate equipment rooms, condensate drainage, air-handling locations, electrical supply, and fire/life-safety constraints.

## How It Is Tested

Exam items usually ask why one system is better for a small room, a tenant floor, a large multi-zone building, or a chilled-water plant. Choose the answer that follows calculated load, zoning, controls, efficiency, maintenance, and coordination. Reject answers based only on first cost, brand, outside capacity ranges, or broad claims that one system is always superior.
