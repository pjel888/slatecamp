## The Real-World Hook

Imagine you are a licensed architect walking through a heritage-era shophouse district in Paco, Manila — one of those double-party-wall blocks where the buildings share structural walls and lean slightly into each other like old friends propping one another up. You have been hired to assess a two-storey masonry structure built in 1952. The owner received a notice from the City Buildings and Architecture Office three weeks ago. Now they want to know: can it be saved, or will the Building Official order it torn down?

You step inside. The front staircase has been eaten by termites. The rear wall — a 200 mm hollow masonry bearing wall — has a diagonal crack running from the upper-right corner of a window all the way to the foundation. You probe it with a rebar and pieces fall out. The electrical panel has exposed wiring with no circuit breakers. A section of the second-floor parquet has rotted through, open to the ground floor. Squatters occupied the right bay for eighteen months after the owner left for Saudi Arabia. There are improvised cooking fires in the corner.

This building hits multiple categories of the Sec. 214 definition of a dangerous building simultaneously: structural hazard (cracked bearing wall), unsafe electrical wiring (exposed conductors), fire hazard (open flame inside deteriorated structure), architectural deficiency (missing egress floor sections), and abandonment (eighteen months with no supervision). The Building Official is not being bureaucratic — the neighborhood's children walk past this wall every day.

The law that governs what happens next — notice, hearing, repair order, vacation order, demolition order — is contained in three sections of PD 1096's Rule II: Sections 214, 215, and 216. These sections are relatively short in the code text, but their implications are enormous. They touch on property rights (Article 482 of the Civil Code), public nuisance law (Articles 694–707), due process, and the full police power authority of local government. Understanding these provisions is essential not only for the board exam — it is the legal scaffolding of every dangerous building investigation an architect may ever conduct.

## Core Concepts

### Principle 1: What Makes a Building "Dangerous or Ruinous" — The Sec. 214 Definition

Section 214 opens with a broad statutory definition designed to capture the full range of building failure modes:

> Buildings or structures that are **structurally unsafe**, **not provided with safe egress**, **constitute a fire hazard**, **otherwise dangerous to human life**, or **constitute a hazard to safety, health, or public welfare** because of:
> - Inadequate maintenance
> - Dilapidation
> - Obsolescence
> - Abandonment
>
> Or which **contribute to pollution to an intolerable degree**.

This is not an exhaustive list of failure conditions — it is a definition of the legal category. The six-category enumeration that follows (in the Abatement provisions and IRR) provides specific conditions that meet this definition. An important drafting feature: a building does not need to fail in all categories, or even in multiple sub-items within one category. **A single sub-item within any one category is sufficient** to render a building dangerous under Sec. 214.

---

### Principle 2: The Six Categories of Dangerous Building Defects (Sec. 215 / 2004 IRR)

The 2004 Revised IRR enumerates six distinct categories of defects. Every exam question about dangerous buildings can be traced back to one or more of these.

---

#### Category 1: Structural Hazards (10 Sub-Items a through j)

This is the most complex category and the most exam-tested. Learn each sub-item.

**(a) Damage below NSCP minimums:** The building has been damaged by fire, earthquake, wind, flood, or other cause to an extent that its remaining capacity falls below the minimum structural requirements of the NSCP. Note: damage alone does not trigger this sub-item — the residual capacity must fall below NSCP minimums.

**(b) Portions likely to fall or collapse:** Any portion of the building is likely to fall, be dislodged, or collapse and injure persons or damage property. This sub-item is the most commonly cited — it applies to overhanging cornices, deteriorated cantilevered balconies, failing parapet walls, and any projecting element that could drop on a passerby.

**(c) Cannot resist half wind pressure:** The building or structure cannot resist at least **one-half (1/2) of the wind pressure** specified in the NSCP. A building that cannot resist even 50% of code wind load is imminently dangerous in a typhoon belt country. The Philippines averages 20 typhoons per year; this sub-item has direct life-safety implications.

**(d) Wrecked, warped, or buckled walls:** The building has walls, counterforts, or supporting members that are wrecked, warped, buckled, or have overstressed structural components.

**(e) Dilapidation, faulty construction, or ground instability:** Includes deterioration greater than normal wear and tear, faulty original construction, inadequacy of foundations for the imposed loads, or ground instability (subsidence, liquefaction risk).

**(f) Plumb-line test failure:** The exterior walls or vertical structural members lean or buckle such that **a plumb line (vertical line) through the center of gravity does not fall inside the middle third (1/3) of the base.** This is a geometric test with classical structural significance. The middle-third rule in masonry and unreinforced concrete: if the resultant force falls outside the kern (middle third), the base develops tension stresses, which masonry cannot reliably resist. A wall leaning this far is on the verge of overturning.

**(g) The Threshold Percentage Rule (Two-part):** This sub-item contains the two most frequently tested specific percentages in the entire dangerous buildings provision:

> **Supporting members:** If **33% or more** of the structure's supporting members are damaged or deteriorated.
>
> **Non-supporting members:** If **50% or more** of the structure's non-supporting (secondary) members are damaged or deteriorated.

These percentages reflect engineering judgment about structural reserve. Supporting members (columns, beams, bearing walls, foundations, shear walls) carry gravity and lateral loads directly. Damage to 33% of them means the structure has lost more than a third of its primary load path — an unacceptably high compromise. Non-supporting members (partition walls, non-load-bearing infill panels, secondary decking) contribute less to overall stability, hence the higher 50% damage threshold before the building is condemned on this sub-item alone.

**(h) Attractive nuisance or harbor for vagrants:** The building or structure constitutes an attractive nuisance to children or a harbor for vagrants or criminals. This sub-item is non-structural but recognizes that buildings dangerous in a social/criminological sense are dangerous under the code. An abandoned building that children explore and fall through is a "dangerous building" even if its walls are technically standing.

**(i) Strength/resistance below threshold percentages:** This sub-item introduces two more specific percentages:

> **Non-supporting parts:** If any part that is not a primary supporting member has **less than 50%** of the strength, fire-resistance, or weather-resistance required by the code for a new building.
>
> **Supporting parts:** If any primary supporting member has **less than 66%** of the strength, fire-resistance, or weather-resistance required by the code for a new building.

The distinction between sub-items (g) and (i) is important:

- Sub-item **(g)** asks: how many members are damaged? (Percentage of members affected.)
- Sub-item **(i)** asks: how much capacity remains in the affected members? (Percentage of required capacity retained.)

A building could pass sub-item (g) (only 20% of columns are cracked) but fail sub-item (i) (those cracked columns retain only 50% of code-required strength, below the 66% threshold).

**(j) Abandonment for 12+ months:** The building has been **abandoned for 12 months or more** and has become a nuisance or hazard. The abandonment threshold is specific: **twelve calendar months.** A building left empty for eleven months does not qualify under this sub-item. The additional condition "has become a nuisance or hazard" means abandonment alone does not trigger the provision — the building must have deteriorated to a hazardous state during the abandonment period.

#### Category 2: Fire Hazards (4 Sub-Items)

A building constitutes a fire hazard if:

1. It contains materials — combustible or otherwise — that when ignited are liable to cause injury or damage to adjoining or nearby property.
2. It has a heating apparatus, chimney, or flue in disrepair and a fire hazard.
3. It has electrical wiring that is defective or inadequate and constitutes a fire hazard.
4. It has any condition constituting a fire hazard per the National Fire Protection Code or BFP regulations.

Fire hazard under this category focuses on **potential** harm — the risk of ignition causing damage beyond the building's boundaries. This is why dilapidated buildings near markets, heritage zones, or densely packed residential neighborhoods receive heightened scrutiny.

---

#### Category 3: Unsafe Electrical Wiring (2 Sub-Items)

1. **Defective wiring or overloading:** Electrical installations that are overloaded, improperly connected, or otherwise present a shock or fire risk.
2. **Absence or failure of overcurrent protection:** Circuits without functional circuit breakers or fuses, or with overcurrent protection that has been bypassed (common in informal construction where occupants replace blown breakers with coins or foil).

This category interfaces directly with the Philippine Electrical Code (PEC) and the authority of the Board of Electrical Engineering. A building with grossly substandard electrical installations can be declared dangerous under PD 1096 independent of any structural deficiency.

---

#### Category 4: Unsafe Mechanical Installation (4 Sub-Items)

1. **Mechanical equipment in disrepair** creating hazard to life.
2. **Elevators, escalators, or hoisting equipment** without proper safety devices or permits.
3. **Gas or steam installations** that are leaking or otherwise unsafe.
4. **Air conditioning or refrigeration systems** with refrigerant leaks or unsafe pressure vessel conditions.

In a post-2010 context of aging commercial buildings, this category increasingly applies to outdated hydraulic elevators and buildings with chiller plants that have exceeded service life.

---

#### Category 5: Inadequate Sanitation/Plumbing (5 Sub-Items)

1. **Inadequate toilet or sewage facilities** for the occupant load.
2. **Sewage leakage** creating health hazard (open drains, failed septic tanks).
3. **Infested premises** — presence of vermin, rodents, or insects at hazard levels.
4. **Accumulation of filth or garbage** in quantities hazardous to health.
5. **Inadequate water supply** for occupant health and fire suppression.

This category brings environmental health agencies into the dangerous buildings framework. A building with a failed septic system discharging into a shared yard can be declared dangerous under Sec. 215 even if it is structurally sound.

---

#### Category 6: Architectural Deficiency (5 Sub-Items)

1. **Inadequate exits or egress** — the building lacks proper means of egress for its occupancy type and load.
2. **Windows or other openings** that are inadequate for ventilation and light.
3. **Corridors and passageways** that are obstructed, deteriorated, or otherwise unsafe for egress.
4. **Absence of required handrails or guardrails** on stairs or elevated surfaces.
5. **Any architectural condition** rendering the building unsafe for its intended occupancy.

This category specifically names egress inadequacy — a building can be declared dangerous not because walls are falling but because people cannot safely get out. This is the architectural profession's direct responsibility. Architects designing additions, renovations, or changes of occupancy must ensure egress compliance to avoid placing a building in Category 6.

### Principle 3: The Four Specific Percentages — A Deep Dive

The exam will test numerical recall. The four percentages in Sec. 215 are not arbitrary:

| Percentage | Applies To | Context | Sub-item |
|---|---|---|---|
| **33%** | Damage to **supporting members** | If ≥ 33% of primary structural members are damaged → dangerous | (g) first part |
| **50%** | Damage to **non-supporting members** | If ≥ 50% of non-structural members are damaged → dangerous | (g) second part |
| **66%** | Remaining **strength of supporting parts** | If a supporting member retains **< 66%** of required strength → dangerous | (i) second part |
| **50%** | Remaining **strength of non-supporting parts** | If a non-supporting member retains **< 50%** of required strength/fire-resistance/weather-resistance → dangerous | (i) first part |

**Why are there two "50%" entries?** They describe opposite conditions:
- Sub-item (g): *50% of non-supporting members are damaged* (quantity threshold)
- Sub-item (i): *remaining capacity of non-supporting parts is less than 50%* (quality/capacity threshold)

A board exam question may exploit this distinction by presenting a scenario where only 20% of non-supporting members are cracked (passes sub-item g) but each cracked member retains only 40% of required strength (fails sub-item i, because 40% < 50% threshold). The building is still dangerous.

### Principle 4: Section 215 — The Building Official's Tripartite Response Authority

Section 215 grants the Building Official three graduated response options depending on the degree of danger assessed:

**Option 1: Order to Repair**
When the building is dangerous but can be made safe through rehabilitation within a reasonable time, the Building Official may order the owner to carry out specific repairs. The repair order must identify the deficiencies and set a compliance deadline.

**Option 2: Order to Vacate**
When the building is dangerous to its occupants but rehabilitation is possible, the Building Official may order occupants vacated before or during repair work. Vacation does not mean demolition — the building may be repaired and reoccupied once compliance is certified.

**Option 3: Order to Demolish**
When the degree of danger is severe — typically when structural failure is imminent, the cost of repair exceeds the value of rehabilitation, or the owner fails to comply with a repair order — the Building Official may order demolition. Demolition orders under Sec. 215 are subject to the abatement procedure (due notice and hearing) except in cases of imminent danger.

**The "Without Prejudice" clause:** Section 215 explicitly states these actions are taken "**without prejudice to**" Civil Code Articles 482 and 694–707. This means the Code's remedies are additional to, not in replacement of, the owner's civil law obligations and neighbors' civil law rights.

---

### Principle 5: Section 216 — Other Remedies

Section 216 is brief but legally significant: **the rights, actions, and remedies provided in PD 1096 are IN ADDITION to all other rights under existing laws.** This means:

1. A Building Official who orders demolition does not extinguish a neighbor's right to sue for damages under the Civil Code.
2. A BFP citation for fire hazard does not prevent the LGU from also proceeding under Sec. 215.
3. Criminal liability under PD 1096 (fines, imprisonment for violations) is separate from civil liability.
4. The abatement procedure under Rule II does not bar an independent action in court by an aggrieved private party.

This "non-exclusivity" of remedies reflects the code's position as one layer in a multi-layer system of building safety law.

---

### Principle 6: The Abatement Procedure — Step by Step

The 2004 Revised IRR at the end of Rule II provides the procedural framework. Critically, this procedure is not just an administrative formality — it is the **due process protection** that prevents arbitrary demolition orders. It has four stages:

**Step 1: Complaint or Motu Proprio Investigation**
The process may begin in two ways:
- A **complaint** filed by any person (neighbor, tenant, concerned citizen, local official) with the Building Official.
- **Motu proprio action** by the Building Official — meaning the BO may initiate on their own motion based on inspection findings, without waiting for a complaint.

The motu proprio authority is important: it means the Building Official has an affirmative duty to act, not merely a reactive role. When an inspector finds a dangerous condition during a routine inspection, they are not required to wait for a neighbor to complain.

**Step 2: Due Notice and Hearing**
Upon initiation:
- The Building Official issues a **written notice** to the building owner (and occupants if different) describing the dangerous conditions found.
- The owner is given an opportunity to be **heard** — to present evidence, rebut findings, and propose remedial actions.
- The hearing requirement is the constitutional guarantee of due process applied to property rights. An owner cannot have a building demolished without a fair opportunity to respond.

**Exception — Imminent Danger:** When a building presents an immediate threat to life (e.g., a wall that could collapse within hours), the Building Official may act without prior notice and hearing — similar to the IPMC's "imminent danger" provision. Post-action notice and hearing are provided after the emergency action.

**Step 3: Actions Available After Hearing**

The Building Official may, following the hearing, take any of the following actions:

1. **Non-issuance, suspension, or revocation of building permit or Certificate of Occupancy (CO).** Stopping the legal authorization for occupancy is often the first formal action — it places on record that the building should not be occupied and establishes liability if the owner continues occupation despite the order.

2. **Work stoppage order.** If construction, renovation, or occupancy is ongoing in a dangerous building, all work is halted immediately.

3. **Declaration as Ruinous or Dangerous.** The formal administrative act that classifies the building. This declaration triggers the owner's legal obligation to repair, vacate, or demolish. It is also the basis for civil nuisance law enforcement.

4. **Imposition of fines and penalties.** Per PD 1096 and its IRR, building violations carry administrative fines and potentially criminal penalties (imprisonment up to 2 years and/or fines up to PHP 20,000 per PD 1096, Sec. 212). Continuing violations after notice may attract escalating daily penalties.

**Step 4: Compliance, Appeal, and Enforcement**

- The owner may appeal to the Secretary of the Department of Public Works and Highways (DPWH) / the Office of the Building Official Appellate Authority.
- Non-compliance within the deadline set in the order empowers the Building Official to cause the work to be done (including demolition) **at the owner's expense** — charged as a lien against the property.
- Costs of abatement-by-government that go unpaid become real property tax liens.

## PH vs. USA Comparative Analysis

| Dimension / Requirement | Philippines — PD 1096 Rule II, Sec. 214–216 / 2004 IRR | USA — IBC 2021 Sec. 116 + International Property Maintenance Code (IPMC) |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary governing statute** | PD 1096, Rule II, Sections 214–216; 2004 Revised IRR Abatement provisions | IBC 2021 Section 116 (Unsafe Structures and Equipment); IPMC (International Property Maintenance Code) — adopted by reference in most jurisdictions |
| **Enforcement authority** | **Building Official (BO)** of the city/municipality; supervised by DPWH | **Code Official** (building official) of the jurisdiction; authority may be shared with fire marshal, health officer |
| **Who can initiate action** | BO motu proprio OR by complaint from any person | Code official's own motion OR complaint from neighbor/citizen/tenant |
| **Definition of dangerous building** | Structurally unsafe, no safe egress, fire hazard, dangerous to human life, health hazard, polluting to intolerable degree (Sec. 214) | IPMC Sec. 108.1: Unsafe structure = inadequate means of egress, inadequate light/ventilation, structural weakness, fire hazard, inadequate sanitary facilities, vermin infestation |
| **Categories of defects** | **6 categories:** Structural Hazards (10 sub-items), Fire Hazards (4), Unsafe Electrical (2), Unsafe Mechanical (4), Inadequate Sanitation (5), Architectural Deficiency (5) | IPMC organizes by same broad areas but does not use a numbered 6-category system; IBC Sec. 116 is performance-based — "deemed dangerous" if it endangers occupants or the public |
| **Specific structural % thresholds** | 33% supporting / 50% non-supporting damaged; 66% / 50% remaining capacity thresholds (Sec. 215 IRR) | IPMC **does not enumerate specific % thresholds** — code official makes engineering judgment based on general "inadequate strength" standard; structural reports by licensed engineers typically required |
| **Abandonment threshold** | **12 months** explicit in code (Sec. 215 IRR Category 1j) | IPMC Sec. 108.1.3: Vacant structures must be secured; no universal "12-month" rule — local ordinances vary (some as low as 6 months vacancy before enforcement) |
| **Wind resistance trigger** | Cannot resist **1/2 of NSCP wind pressure** | IBC: "inadequate structural strength" per engineered analysis; ASCE 7 is the load standard; no 1/2-wind shortcut rule in IBC |
| **Due process procedure** | Notice → Hearing → Order (repair/vacate/demolish); appeal to DPWH Secretary | IPMC Sec. 108.3: Notice served; right to appeal to Board of Appeals; emergency action possible for imminent danger without prior hearing |
| **Imminent danger power** | BO may act for imminent danger; post-action hearing provided | IPMC Sec. 108.1.1: Code official may vacate or demolish immediately for imminent danger; retroactive hearing available |
| **Remedies available** | Non-issuance/revocation of permit or CO; work stoppage; declaration as dangerous; fines/penalties; cost-of-work lien | IPMC Sec. 108.4: Demolition by authority; cost charged to property as lien; criminal penalties for non-compliance; IBC Sec. 116.5: disconnection of utilities |
| **Utility disconnection** | Implied through permit revocation; no explicit "cut utilities" provision | IBC Sec. 116.5: Code official may authorize **disconnection of utility services** to dangerous buildings — explicit power |
| **Penalty quantum** | Administrative fines; PD 1096 Sec. 212: up to PHP 20,000 and/or imprisonment up to 2 years | Varies by jurisdiction: IPMC violations typically misdemeanor level; fines range from USD 100 to USD 10,000/day; criminal penalties for persistent non-compliance |
| **Appeal route** | Appeal to DPWH Secretary (administrative); then RTC for judicial review | Appeal to local Board of Appeals (administrative); then state court for judicial review |
| **Civil law interface** | Sec. 216 / Civil Code Articles 482 and 694–707 (nuisance law applies cumulatively) | Common law nuisance; tort liability; Sec. 216 analog is the "non-exclusivity" doctrine in US administrative law |
| **Demolition at owner's expense** | Yes — costs become a lien on property | Yes — IPMC Sec. 110.3: Cost of demolition by authority assessed against the property |
| **Key philosophical difference** | PH code is highly **prescriptive** — specific percentages, 12-month abandonment, 1/2-wind threshold written in code text. Building Official applies fixed triggers. | US (IPMC/IBC) is **performance-based** — code official makes holistic engineering judgment, often assisted by private engineer's report. No universally fixed percentage triggers. |

### Key Observations

**Where PH is MORE specific:** The explicit enumeration of percentages (33/50/66/50) and the 12-month abandonment threshold make Philippine enforcement more predictable and less susceptible to arbitrary application. An owner knows exactly when the threshold is crossed.

**Where USA is MORE flexible:** The IPMC's performance-based approach allows code officials to declare a building dangerous based on engineering judgment even when specific numerical thresholds are not met — useful for novel failure modes. The explicit utility-disconnection power (IBC Sec. 116.5) is a practical enforcement tool not present in PD 1096.

**Where they are identical:** Both require notice and hearing before demolition (except imminent danger). Both allow costs to be charged to the property as a lien. Both classify their building code remedies as non-exclusive additions to other legal rights.

## Cross-Referencing: How Sections 214–216 Connect to the Broader Legal Framework

### Civil Code of the Philippines

**Article 482** addresses the rights of property owners regarding structures that may cause injury. The key rule: if a building or structure is in a state of ruin such that it may fall and cause damage, the owner shall be obliged to demolish or make necessary repairs. If the owner fails to act after being notified, the authority (formerly the courts, now the Building Official under PD 1096) may cause the action at the owner's expense.

This is the Civil Code antecedent to Sec. 215. The relationship is explicit in Sec. 215 itself: actions are taken "without prejudice to Civil Code Article 482." The Building Official's authority under PD 1096 is a statutory acceleration of the remedy that previously required a court proceeding.

**Articles 694–707** define **nuisance** in Philippine law. Article 694 defines a nuisance as "any act, omission, establishment, business, condition of property, or anything else which injures or endangers the health or safety of others; or annoys or offends the senses; or shocks, defies or disregards decency or morality; or obstructs or interferes with the free passage of any public highway or street, or any body of water; or hinders or impairs the use of property."

A dangerous building fits this definition multiple times over. Article 695 classifies nuisances as:
- **Public nuisance:** affects a community or neighborhood or the public generally
- **Private nuisance:** affects only an individual or a small number of persons

Most dangerous buildings are **public nuisances** under Article 695 — they threaten not just the owner or immediate occupants but the surrounding neighborhood. Articles 698–707 describe remedies, including:
- Action by private individual specially injured
- Action by public official (the Building Official's Sec. 215 authority is the administrative exercise of this)
- Abatement at the defendant's expense

**Section 216** specifically preserves all of these Civil Code rights. The building owner who fails to comply with a Sec. 215 order may simultaneously face: (1) administrative fines from the BO; (2) a Civil Code Article 482 enforcement action; (3) a nuisance abatement suit under Articles 694–707; and (4) potential tort liability for any injuries that occur.

### Rule III — Building Permits and Certificates of Occupancy (Sec. 308–309)

When a building is declared dangerous under Sec. 215, the Building Official may:
- **Revoke the Certificate of Occupancy** under Sec. 309 — the legal authorization for use. Without a CO, the building cannot lawfully be occupied.
- **Suspend or revoke the building permit** for any ongoing work.

Conversely, the issuance of a CO under Sec. 309 is a statement that a building complies with the Code. If conditions subsequently deteriorate to the Sec. 214 dangerous building level, the CO does not immunize the owner — the code's dangerous building provisions apply to the current state of the building, not its condition at CO issuance.

### Rule V — Fire Zones (Sec. 506)

Section 506 governs restrictions on **existing buildings in fire zones.** Key intersection: if a building in a fire zone is declared dangerous and requires repair, the cost-of-repair limits in Sec. 506(c) apply — repairs in any 12-month period may not exceed 20% of the building's value without full compliance with the fire zone's construction type requirements. This means a building that is 40% structurally compromised (meeting the Sec. 215 dangerous threshold) may face the additional constraint that full repair to bring it back to 100% would trigger mandatory reconstruction to current fire zone standards — making demolition and rebuilding economically rational.

### Rule XI — Protection of Pedestrians (Sec. 1101–1108)

The demolition component of Rule XI (Sec. 1108) is directly triggered by Sec. 215 demolition orders. When the Building Official orders a dangerous building demolished, the demolition contractor must comply with Rule XI's full pedestrian protection requirements:
- Temporary walkways (Sec. 1105)
- Fences, railings, canopies (Sec. 1106)
- Utility protection (Sec. 1104)
- No mortar mixing on public streets (Sec. 1103)
- Demolition permit (Sec. 1108)

A Sec. 215 demolition order does not suspend Rule XI obligations — it triggers them.

### RA 9514 — Fire Code of the Philippines

Fire hazard conditions (Category 2 of the dangerous building defects) interface directly with the Bureau of Fire Protection's enforcement authority under RA 9514. The BFP fire inspector may issue a Fire Hazard Removal Notice for the same conditions that the Building Official cites under Sec. 215. Both agencies may act concurrently — Sec. 216's "other remedies" clause confirms this.

### National Structural Code of the Philippines (NSCP)

Three specific Sec. 215 conditions directly reference NSCP standards:
- Sub-item (a): damage below NSCP minimums
- Sub-item (c): inability to resist 1/2 of NSCP wind pressure
- Sub-items (g) and (i): damage and strength thresholds that compare against NSCP-required structural capacity

The NSCP is therefore an implicit enforcement standard embedded within the dangerous buildings provision. An architect or structural engineer preparing a dangerous building assessment must apply NSCP Chapter 2 (Combinations of Loads) and NSCP Chapter 2 (Wind Loads, Sec. 207 under the 7th Edition) to calculate the 1/2-wind-pressure threshold for sub-item (c).

> **F** — **Fire Hazards** (Category 2 — 4 sub-items)
> **E** — **Unsafe Electrical Wiring** (Category 3 — 2 sub-items)
> **M** — **Unsafe Mechanical Installation** (Category 4 — 4 sub-items)
> **S** — **Inadequate Sanitation/Plumbing** (Category 5 — 5 sub-items)
> **A** — **Architectural Deficiency** (Category 6 — 5 sub-items)

Memory hook: **"S-F-EMS-A"** — "Safety First: Every Maintenance Slippage Accumulates." Say it when you encounter a deteriorating building: **Structural, Fire, Electrical, Mechanical, Sanitation, Architectural.** These are the six lenses through which the code evaluates danger.

---

### Mnemonic 2: "**3-5-6-5**" — The Four Percentage Thresholds

Read as a rhythm:

> **33** percent — supporting members **damaged**
> **50** percent — non-supporting members **damaged**
> **66** percent — supporting parts' remaining **strength**
> **50** percent — non-supporting parts' remaining **strength**

Memory hook: **"33-50 for damage; 66-50 for strength."** Or think of it as a scorecard:
- Damage: **33 (support) / 50 (non-support)**
- Strength: **66 (support) / 50 (non-support)**

The logic: **supporting elements get stricter thresholds** because they carry the building. 33% damage triggers condemnation (lower tolerance). Non-supporting elements get 50% before condemnation (more tolerance). For remaining strength, the supporting member must retain at least 66% (high bar); non-supporting must retain at least 50%.

---

## Exam Trap Learts

**TRAP 1:** "If 40% of the non-supporting walls in a building are cracked, the building is automatically dangerous under Sec. 215 because 40% > 33%."
→ **TRUTH:** This is WRONG. The 33% threshold applies **only to supporting members** (sub-item g, first part). For **non-supporting members**, the damage threshold is **50%** — not 33%. A building with 40% damaged non-supporting members does NOT meet the sub-item (g) dangerous condition. The examiner exploits the natural instinct to apply the lower threshold universally. Always check: supporting or non-supporting?

---

**TRAP 2:** "A building abandoned for 10 months automatically becomes a dangerous building under PD 1096."
→ **TRUTH:** Two errors here. First, the abandonment period is **12 months**, not 10. Second, abandonment alone is **not sufficient** — the abandonment must be coupled with the condition that the building "has become a nuisance or hazard" during that period. A well-maintained but unoccupied heritage building that has been locked for 11 months is not a dangerous building under sub-item (j). Both conditions must be met: 12+ months AND nuisance/hazard status.

---

**TRAP 3:** "The Building Official cannot demolish a dangerous building without a court order, since demolition deprives the owner of property without judicial process."
→ **TRUTH:** Under PD 1096 Sec. 215, the Building Official has **statutory administrative authority** to order demolition after following the Abatement Procedure (notice and hearing). No court order is required for administrative demolition. This is an exercise of police power — the government's inherent authority to regulate property for public health and safety. The owner's remedy is administrative appeal (to the DPWH Secretary) and then judicial review by the Regional Trial Court if still aggrieved. Section 216 preserves all other legal remedies, but the BO does not need a court order to act under Sec. 215.

## Active Recall Questions

**Question 1**
A structural engineer's inspection report on a 1960s commercial building in Binondo finds that 6 out of 20 concrete columns (30%) show severe cracking and reduced cross-section due to corrosion, and that the 6 damaged columns retain only 55% of the structural strength required by the NSCP for new construction. Based solely on this report, is this building dangerous under PD 1096 Sec. 215?

**A.** No — 30% of supporting members are damaged, which is below the 33% threshold, so the building does not qualify as dangerous.
**B.** Yes — 30% of supporting members are damaged, which exceeds the 33% threshold.
**C.** No — the 55% remaining strength exceeds both the 50% and 66% thresholds for strength.
**D.** Yes — although the 30% damage does not trigger sub-item (g), the 55% remaining strength is below the 66% threshold for supporting parts under sub-item (i).

> **Answer: D — Yes, because 55% remaining strength is below the 66% threshold for supporting parts (sub-item i)**
>
> This question tests the critical distinction between sub-items (g) and (i). Sub-item (g): 30% of supporting members damaged — this does NOT trigger sub-item (g), which requires ≥ 33%. Option B is wrong. Sub-item (i): supporting parts must retain at least 66% of required strength; these columns retain only 55%. Since 55% < 66%, the building meets the dangerous condition under sub-item (i). A building can pass sub-item (g) but still be declared dangerous under sub-item (i) — the two tests are independent. Option C is wrong because it misreads the threshold: the supporting part threshold is 66%, and 55% < 66%, not "exceeds both thresholds."
>

**Question 2**
The owner of a residential building in Pasay City abandoned the property in January 2024 after a flood. By October 2024 (9 months later), the fence has collapsed, the roof has partially caved in, and children have been observed playing inside the open structure. Under PD 1096 Rule II, Sec. 215, the Building Official should:

**A.** Declare the building dangerous under the 12-month abandonment sub-item since children entering the structure prove it is a nuisance/hazard.
**B.** Not declare the building dangerous on the abandonment ground because 9 months have not yet reached the 12-month threshold, but may declare it dangerous on other grounds.
**C.** Declare the building dangerous only if the owner is formally notified and 30 days have elapsed.
**D.** Take no action until 12 months have passed and the owner files for a new building permit.

> **Answer: B — Not dangerous on abandonment grounds (9 months < 12 months), but may declare dangerous on other grounds**
>
> Sub-item (j) requires BOTH conditions: abandonment for ≥ 12 months AND nuisance/hazard status. At 9 months, the abandonment threshold has not been met. However, the building almost certainly meets other dangerous conditions: the partially caved-in roof constitutes "portions likely to fall or collapse" (sub-item b); children playing inside presents an "attractive nuisance" (sub-item h); the collapsed fence may constitute an architectural deficiency (Category 6). Option A is wrong because the 12-month period has not elapsed. Options C and D are wrong — the BO does not need to wait for a permit application, and declaration of danger under non-abandonment sub-items does not require 30 days post-notice before the declaration itself (that period applies to compliance, not the declaration).
>
> *LEA Area: Building Laws, Codes & Ethics / Environmental Planning & Community Architecture*

**Question 3**
Under PD 1096 Sec. 216 (Other Remedies), a building owner whose structure has been declared dangerous and ordered demolished by the Building Official:

**A.** Retains no further legal rights because the Building Official's order is final and cannot be appelead.
**B.** May appeal only to the DPWH Secretary and has no right to file a civil case.
**C.** Is subject only to the penalties under PD 1096 and is shielded from any Civil Code liability.
**D.** Retains all rights of appeal and civil remedy, because the code's remedies are in addition to, not in replacement of, all other existing legal rights.

> **Answer: D — The owner retains all rights of appeal and civil remedy**
>
<LawCallout code="pd1096" section="Rule I - Dangerous Buildings">


Section 216 is explicit: "The rights, actions, and remedies provided in this Code shall be in addition to all other rights under existing laws." This means (1) the owner may appeal the demolition order administratively to the DPWH Secretary; (2) if still aggrieved, may file for judicial review before the Regional Trial Court; (3) any neighbor or third party injured by the building's condition retains all Civil Code rights (Articles 482, 694–707) independently of the BO's action. The code does not extinguish pre-existing rights — it adds to them. Option A is wrong — the BO's order is an administrative act subject to appeal. Option B is wrong — judicial remedy is available after exhausting administrative remedies. Option C is wrong — PD 1096 liability and Civil Code liability run concurrently.


</LawCallout>
>
> *LEA Area: Building Laws, Codes & Ethics*
