# The 19th Century: Industrial Revolution and Architecture

## LEA Point

Industrial Revolution architecture is the bridge between historicist nineteenth-century design and twentieth-century modernism. The exam pattern is not "industry replaced style overnight"; it is that iron, glass, steel-frame logic, standardization, and reform movements made new building types and new design arguments possible.

## Source Anchors

- Joseph Paxton's Crystal Palace anchors prefabricated iron-and-glass exhibition architecture for the 1851 Great Exhibition.
- Gustave Eiffel's tower anchors exposed open-lattice iron as both engineering structure and public image for the 1889 Paris exposition.
- William Le Baron Jenney's Home Insurance Building anchors early Chicago high-rise skeleton-frame development, with iron/steel structure reducing dependence on load-bearing masonry walls.
- Louis Sullivan anchors the "form follows function" exam phrase; Arts and Crafts anchors material honesty, craft reform, and resistance to careless industrial imitation.

## Exam Pattern

If the stem says 1851, Great Exhibition, Paxton, modular prefabrication, or glass-and-iron hall, answer Crystal Palace. If it says 1889 Paris exposition, open iron lattice, and engineering made visible, answer Eiffel Tower. If it says Jenney, Chicago, 1885, or internal metal skeleton frame, answer Home Insurance Building. If it asks for the design idea that pushes against historicist ornament, use Sullivan/function or Arts and Crafts/material honesty as the cue.

## Guardrails

Do not say Gothic Revival used no iron at all; the safer contrast is historical ornament and masonry/stylistic language versus industrial prefabrication and visible metal/glass technology. Do not say the Home Insurance Building eliminated masonry completely. Do not collapse Jenney, Paxton, Eiffel, Sullivan, and Morris into one modernist doctrine; they are separate nineteenth-century anchors that later modernism builds from.

## Real World Scenario

For adaptive reuse of an old market hall, a source-safe precedent study would separate the lessons: Crystal Palace for modular daylighted enclosure, Eiffel for exposed structural expression, Home Insurance for frame logic, Sullivan for function-led massing, and Arts and Crafts for honest material repair rather than fake historical applique.
