# Structural bolts join steel members through frictional force or bearing action

## What You Need to Know for the LEA

For LEA purposes, structural-bolt questions should stay at connection behavior level unless the exact grade table is supplied in the item. The audited sources support bolted and welded steel connection concepts, high-strength friction grip bolts in site connections, and the difference between simple, semi-rigid, and rigid connection behavior.

## Core Concept

- Steel connections may use bolts, welds, tees, angles, plates, or combinations of these parts.
- European Building Construction Illustrated notes that off-site steel connections tend to be welded where quality can be controlled, while site connections tend to be bolted to reduce erection risk and speed assembly.
- High-strength friction grip bolts are commonly used in bolted structural steelwork connections on site.
- Connection strength depends on the connected member sizes, the connecting plates or angles, and the bolt or weld configuration.
- Simple or pinned connections resist shear and direct axial loading; they need a separate horizontal bracing strategy. Rigid connections maintain their angle with little deformation under load.

## How It Is Tested

- Identify why a site connection may be bolted rather than welded.
- Distinguish simple, semi-rigid, and rigid steel connection behavior.
- Recognize seated and framed connections as shear-resisting details.
- Avoid importing unsupported bolt-grade numbers when the source item only asks for connection behavior.

## Common Mistakes

- Do not claim exact bolt grades, preload percentages, coating systems, or code values unless the exam item supplies or cites the exact standard.
- Do not treat every bolted connection as slip-critical.
- Do not treat simple shear connections as full moment-resisting connections.
