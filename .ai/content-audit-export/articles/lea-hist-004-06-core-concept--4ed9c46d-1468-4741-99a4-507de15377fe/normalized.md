**Ottoman architecture** (15th–19th centuries) represents Islam's most successful synthesis of Byzantine structural logic with Islamic spatial needs. The Ottomans conquered Constantinople (1453) and inherited Hagia Sophia—a monumental Byzantine dome surrounded by half-domes. Rather than abandon this model, Ottoman architects embraced it and developed it further. The result is the **centralized domed mosque**, a distinctly Ottoman contribution to Islamic architecture.

The founder of Ottoman architectural theory is **Sinan** (c. 1490–1588), chief architect under sultans Süleyman and Selim II. Sinan designed over 300 buildings and established design principles that dominated Ottoman practice for centuries. His innovation was the **interior unified dome space**: a massive central dome that appears to float on light, supported by four half-domes, with smaller domes filling the corners. This creates a sensation of infinite space—entirely different from the confined courtyard intimacy of Andalusian palaces or the gridded columns of hypostyle mosques.

The Ottoman mosque plan typically begins with a covered portico (portico with dome), followed by a central prayer hall beneath the dominant dome, surrounded by galleries for women and secondary prayers. The courtyard (avlu) lies outside, containing a fountain for ablution. Unlike Umayyad and Abbasid models, the Ottoman courtyard is not essential to the prayer experience—it is preparatory space. The spiritual experience concentrates inside, beneath the dome.

Structural brilliance distinguishes Ottoman design. Sinan and his successors solved the problem of supporting massive domes over wide spans using cascading half-domes and pendentives (curved triangular surfaces). Stone and marble replace brick; architectural expression becomes about purity of line and luminosity. Windows beneath the dome flood light inward, making the dome appear weightless. This is rational, mathematical beauty—not the intricate ornamental fragmentation of muqarnas but the geometric clarity of spherical forms.

Decoration in Ottoman mosques is restrained compared to Andalusian or Persian traditions. Tilework (Iznik tiles with blue, white, and red glazes) frames windows and lower walls. Calligraphy appears in bold script on the walls. But the primary decorative strategy is the play of light through windows and the proportional relationships of geometric forms. The dome itself is the decoration.

The Ottoman empire was administratively centralized, and this centralization appears in architecture. The same dome-and-half-dome system appears in mosques from Istanbul to Baghdad to Cairo—recognizable as Ottoman, regardless of location. Regional variation is minimal compared to Umayyad, Abbasid, or Andalusian diversity. This uniformity reflects Ottoman bureaucratic power and the prestige of Sinan's models.

Ottoman architecture also includes new building types: the **külliye** (complex of religious and social buildings surrounding a mosque—madrasa, hospital, public bath, caravanserai, and soup kitchen). These complexes demonstrated the sultan's piety and provided social services. The külliye became the Ottoman architectural signature, as important as the centralized mosque itself.

By the 18th century, Ottoman architecture absorbed European Baroque elements (floral decoration, color, curved lines). This period, called the **Tulip Period** (1718–1730), represents European influence creeping into Islamic tradition. However, the fundamental dome-and-half-dome structural system remained unchanged.

## Key Terms

**Ottoman Empire** — Turkish empire (1299–1922 CE); conquered Constantinople in 1453; dominated Middle East, North Africa, and Eastern Europe; produced distinctive architectural tradition synthesizing Byzantine and Islamic forms.

**Mimar Sinan** — Chief architect (c. 1490–1588 CE) under sultans Süleyman and Selim II; designed over 300 buildings; perfected the centralized domed mosque system; established Ottoman architectural principles.

**Centralized Domed Mosque** — Ottoman building type featuring a massive central dome supported by cascading half-domes and pendentives; interior is unified and luminous; signature Ottoman contribution to Islamic architecture.

**Half-Dome** — Hemispherical vault (half of a full dome); used in Ottoman mosques to support and balance the central dome; creates spiritual and geometric balance.

**Pendentive** — Curved triangular surface transitioning from a square or polygonal base to a dome; structural innovation allowing domes to sit atop various base shapes.

**Külliye** — Complex of religious and social buildings surrounding a mosque; typically includes madrasa (school), hospital, public bath, caravanserai, soup kitchen, and other facilities; Ottoman signature building type.

**Iznik Tilework** — Glazed ceramic tiles produced in Iznik, Turkey; typically blue, white, and red or turquoise; used extensively in Ottoman mosque decoration; demarcates Ottoman period.

**Avlu** — Open courtyard of a mosque; contains ablution fountain; Ottoman mosques treat avlu as preparatory space rather than essential prayer space.

**Minaret** — Tall tower from which call to prayer is broadcast; Ottoman mosques often have multiple minarets (compared to one or two in earlier traditions); slender, cylindrical form.

**Tulip Period** — Ottoman period (1718–1730 CE); marked by Baroque influence and decoration; represents European stylistic integration into Ottoman tradition.
