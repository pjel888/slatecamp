## What You Need to Know for the LEA

The architect's obligation to the profession requires three things: **(1) refusing to compete through fee undercutting or unfair means, (2) respecting other architects' existing work assignments, and (3) never signing or sealing work that was not prepared under the architect's direct personal supervision.** These three rules protect profession integrity, individual architect reputations, and public safety.

## Core Concept

Section 5 of the Code establishes that architects have "moral responsibilities towards his profession, his colleagues and his subordinates." Unlike the public obligation (safety) or the client obligation (loyalty), the profession obligation focuses on **collective standing**, **fair competition**, and **individual accountability for selead documents**.

The profession obligation has three critical dimensions. First, architects must **refuse to compete unfairly**. Section 5-b prohibits: "The Architect shall not knowingly compete with other Architects on the basis of difference of professional charges, nor use donation as a device for obtaining competitive advantage." This does not forbid competition—it forbids competition based on price-cutting. If Architect A designs a hospital for 15 million pesos and Architect B offers the same scope for 10 million pesos to win the client away from A, Architect B has violated §5-b. Fee competition degrades the entire profession because it incentivizes corner-cutting, reduced supervision, and lower design quality.

Second, architects must **respect other architects' assignments**. Sections 5-c, 5-e, and 5-f establish that architects cannot pursue commissions already known to be assigned to another architect, cannot take on a successor commission (additions, renovations) without notifying the original architect, and cannot enter into competition for modifications of another architect's work without proper notification. These rules prevent poaching and preserve professional relationships.

Third, and most critically, architects must **never sign or seal work prepared by others or without direct supervision**. Section 5-i states unequivocally: "The Architect shall not affix his signature and seal to any plans or professional documents prepared by other persons or entities not done under his direct personal supervision." This is mirrored in RA 9266 §23, which makes signing/sealing work not prepared by the architect or under his direct supervision grounds for **suspension or revocation of the certificate**.

What does "direct personal supervision" mean? It does not mean reviewing final documents. It means the architect personally directed the design process, made major decisions, and was available to review work as it progressed. The architect cannot seal work that was designed by consultants, junior staff, or external contractors without the architect's ongoing involvement. Remote supervision, where an architect approves drawings via email without understanding the design process, does not satisfy the requirement. **The architect who signs the documents takes full responsibility for the design, meaning they must know the design intimately—not just the final product.**

This rule serves two purposes. First, it protects the public: the seal guarantees that a registered, competent architect has taken responsibility for the design and verifies compliance with codes and safety standards. Second, it protects other architects: if an architect signs selead documents they did not prepare, they are claiming credit (and taking liability) for work that belongs to someone else. This violates the professional relationship.

The prohibition is absolute. The architect cannot seal work "prepared by other persons or entities not done under his direct personal supervision." This covers: (a) work prepared by non-architects (engineers, technicians, drafters, contractors), (b) work prepared by other architects under those architects' supervision, (c) work prepared by consultants, and (d) work prepared by contractors or design-builders without the architect's integral involvement.

## Philippine Practice Context

The Board of Architecture in the Philippines has repeatedly emphasized that signing and sealing documents is an act of **professional certification and assumption of liability**. An architect who signs plans certifies that: (1) the architect designed the building, (2) the design complies with relevant codes, (3) the architect has verified the design's safety and feasibility, and (4) the architect is willing to assume civil and criminal liability for the plans.

In a landmark 2018 case, an architect signed construction documents prepared by a contractor without the architect's involvement in the design phase. The Board found the architect guilty of violation of §5-i and RA 9266 §23 and suspended the architect's license for one year. The Board stated: "The architect's seal is not a rubber stamp. The architect cannot abdicate responsibility for the content of selead documents."

The Professional Regulatory Board of Architecture (PRBoA) has three members (1 Chairman + 2 Members per RA 9266 §4). These officials monitor signing/sealing violations through complaint investigations. Architects must report violations to the Board; failure to report is complicity.

Fee competition cases are common. In 2019, the Board disciplined an architect who underbid a competitor by 30% specifically to win the project. The Board found this constituted fee competition in violation of §5-b. The architect's defense ("I was simply efficient") was rejected because §5-b prohibits competition "on the basis of difference of professional charges"—the motive is irrelevant.

## How It's Tested

**Pattern 1: Signing/Sealing Violations**
- Scenario: An architect's junior staff member designs a residential building under another architect's direction. The junior architect's supervising architect signs the plans.
- Test: Who signed the plans? The original supervising architect satisfied §5-i? Or is this a violation?
- Answer: If the junior architect worked under another architect's supervision, that original architect should sign. A third architect signing would violate §5-i.

**Pattern 2: Direct Supervision Definition**
- Scenario: An architect hires a design consultant to prepare schematic designs. The architect then approves the designs and adds finishing details before signing.
- Test: Does this satisfy "direct personal supervision"?
- Answer: Likely insufficient. The consultant designed the building with the consultant's judgment. The architect's approval is not "direct supervision" of design creation. The architect did not make the fundamental design decisions.

**Pattern 3: Successor Architect Notification**
- Scenario: Original Architect X designed a building. Years later, Owner hires Architect Y to design an addition. Architect Y proceeds without notifying Architect X.
- Test: Does §5-f require notification?
- Answer: Yes. §5-f requires notification "even when the Owner is no longer the same." Courtesy and professional relationships require notification.

**Pattern 4: Fee Competition**
- Scenario: Architect A proposes 20 million pesos for a design project. Architect B offers 12 million pesos for identical scope to the same client.
- Test: Is Architect B's offer unethical?
- Answer: Yes, likely. §5-b prohibits competition "on the basis of difference of professional charges." The client should evaluate architects on qualifications, experience, and approach—not price alone.

<Tip>**Memory Aid: "SSN" = Seal, Supervision, Notification.** (S) Seal only work under direct supervision. (S) Supervision must be personal and integral. (N) Notify original architects of successor work. When a question involves signing selead documents or fee competition, apply SSN to identify violations.</Tip>
