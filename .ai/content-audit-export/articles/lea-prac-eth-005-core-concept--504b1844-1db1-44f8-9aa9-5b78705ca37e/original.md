## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal

After reading this article, you should be able to:

- Explain the ethical prohibition on unfair fee competition and collusive fee-fixing agreements.
- Describe the direct supervision requirement: the architect who signs and seals must have prepared or directly supervised the work.
- Identify the obligation to respect other architects' existing commissions and not supplant them.
- Apply professional-obligation standards to LEA scenarios involving fee competition, signing-sealing, and colleague relations.

The architect's obligation to the profession requires three things: **(1) refusing to compete through fee undercutting or unfair means, (2) respecting other architects' existing work assignments, and (3) never signing or sealing work that was not prepared under the architect's direct personal supervision.** These three rules protect profession integrity, individual architect reputations, and public safety.

## Core Concept

Section 5 of the Code establishes that architects have "moral responsibilities towards his profession, his colleagues and his subordinates." Unlike the public obligation (safety) or the client obligation (loyalty), the profession obligation focuses on **collective standing**, **fair competition**, and **individual accountability for sealed documents**.

<LawCallout code="ra9266" section="Section 5 (a-k)" title="Profession Obligations — Key Prohibitions">
Section 5 establishes the profession obligation. Key subsections: (b) shall not compete on basis of fee differences or use donation for competitive advantage; (c) shall not seek commissions known to be already endowed to another architect; (e) shall not assume commission until written notification and proof of termination; (f) shall notify original architect of successor work even if owner changed; (g) shall not injure another architect's reputation; (i) shall not sign/seal work not under direct personal supervision; (j) shall mentor subordinates.
</LawCallout>

The profession obligation has three critical dimensions:

1. **Refuse to compete unfairly** — Section 5-b prohibits knowing competition "on the basis of difference of professional charges." If Architect A proposes 15M pesos and Architect B offers 10M pesos for the same scope to win the client, Architect B has violated §5-b. Fee competition degrades the entire profession.

2. **Respect other architects' assignments** — Sections 5-c, 5-e, and 5-f establish that architects cannot pursue commissions already assigned to another architect, cannot take on successor work without notifying the original architect, and cannot compete for modifications without proper notification.

3. **Never sign/seal work without direct supervision** — Section 5-i states: "The Architect shall not affix his signature and seal to any plans or professional documents prepared by other persons or entities not done under his direct personal supervision." This is mirrored in RA 9266 §23 as grounds for suspension or revocation.

## Philippine Practice Context

The Board of Architecture has repeatedly emphasized that signing and sealing documents is an act of **professional certification and assumption of liability**. An architect who signs plans certifies that: (1) the architect designed the building, (2) the design complies with relevant codes, (3) the architect has verified the design's safety and feasibility, and (4) the architect is willing to assume civil and criminal liability for the plans.

The PRBoA (3 members: 1 Chairman + 2 Members per RA 9266 §4) monitors signing/sealing violations. Architects must report violations to the Board; failure to report is complicity.

<Warn>
  **Critical Exam Trap:** "Direct supervision" does NOT mean reviewing final documents. It means the architect personally directed the design process, made major decisions, and was available to review work as it progressed. Remote supervision, email-based approval, or post-hoc review of finished work does NOT satisfy §5-i. The architect who signs the documents must know the design intimately — not just the final product.
</Warn>

## How It's Tested

**Pattern 1: Signing/Sealing Violations**
Architect signs plans prepared entirely by junior staff without oversight. Violation of §5-i — requires direct personal supervision during the design process, not just final approval.

**Pattern 2: Direct Supervision Definition**
Architect hires a design consultant to prepare schematics, then approves and signs. Likely insufficient — the consultant did the design. The architect's approval is not "direct supervision" of design creation.

**Pattern 3: Successor Architect Notification**
Original Architect X designed a building. Years later, Owner hires Architect Y to design an addition. Architect Y must notify Architect X under §5-f, even if the owner has changed.

**Pattern 4: Fee Competition**
Architect A proposes 20M for a project. Architect B offers 12M for identical scope. Architect B violates §5-b — competition "on the basis of difference of professional charges."

<ExamTip>
  **LEA Memory Aid:** "SSN" — (S)eal only work under direct supervision; (S)upervision must be personal and integral; (N)otify original architects of successor work. When a question involves signing sealed documents or fee competition, apply SSN to identify violations.
</ExamTip>

  mistakes={[
    "Confusing 'final review' with 'direct supervision' — §5-i requires direct supervision during the design process, not just approval of final documents",
    "Assuming junior staff designs can be sealed by the supervising architect if the architect 'reviewed' them — if the junior staff worked under another architect's supervision, only that supervising architect may sign",
    "Thinking remote oversight qualifies as direct supervision — direct personal supervision requires active involvement in design decisions as they are made, not post-hoc review",

    "Confusing professional price negotiation with fee competition — negotiating fees within a relationship is different from undercutting competitors. §5-b prohibits the latter",
