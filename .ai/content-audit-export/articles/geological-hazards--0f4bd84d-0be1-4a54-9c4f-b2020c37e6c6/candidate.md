# Geological Hazards

## Board Exam Quick Check

A geological-hazard question is usually asking whether the site decision reduces exposure and instability. The checked local sources support risk mapping, soil classification, slope analysis, erosion control, and land-use restrictions in high-risk areas.

## Source-Grounded Core

The climate-resilience reference tool frames adaptation around hazards, exposure, vulnerability, and local conditions. It says successful adaptation requires understanding risks and vulnerabilities, then designing actions according to local needs and the interaction of vulnerability indicators with hazards, the built environment, the natural environment, and adaptive capacity.

For local planning, the tool describes climate and disaster risk assessment as a way to derive risk information from hazard and vulnerability analysis. It also identifies hazard and vulnerability mapping, managing development in flood-prone and other high-risk areas, land zoning laws, easements, protected areas, and building codes and practices as adaptation actions.

## Geological-Hazard Reading

A geological hazard is not solved by a generic structural detail alone. First identify the risky condition: unstable slope, erodible bank, poor subgrade, floodplain, lahar-prone area, riverbank exposure, or other mapped hazard. Then connect the design response to the risk: avoid or restrict development, increase open space or easements, stabilize slopes, protect drainage paths, or use resilient site and building standards.

The climate-resilience source highlights cases where green-grey infrastructure, wetland parks, renaturing lahar-susceptible areas, coastal parks, mangrove restoration, expanded river easements, and risk-sensitive zoning reduce exposure and improve hazard management.

## Sitework Evidence

The Siteworks sources identify soil classification and testing as part of judging drainage and bearing capacity. They also define fill materials, granular filters, riprap, gabions, geotextiles, geocells, and erosion-control materials. Riprap resists displacement by moving water according to stone weight, size, shape, channel or bank geometry, and filter blanket. Gabion thickness is tied to erodibility of bank soil, maximum water velocity, and bank slope.

These are not substitutes for hazard avoidance, but they are technical tools when development must manage erosion, slope stabilization, drainage, or channel protection.

## LEA Answer Pattern

For exam items, prefer the answer that starts from mapped risk and local site evidence. Avoid choices that build first and mitigate later. Strong answers combine hazard mapping, reduced exposure, protected open-space or easement systems, soil and slope assessment, and appropriate stabilization or drainage measures.
