# Color, Texture, Light, and Shadow in Architecture

Color, texture, light, and shadow are not afterthoughts applied after form is complete. In the local TOA source map, they are part of how architecture is perceived through surfaces, openings, movement, and sensory experience.

Texture is both visual and tactile. It comes from the size, shape, arrangement, and proportion of surface parts, and it changes how a surface reflects or absorbs incident light. A rough wall under grazing light will read differently from the same wall under flat, diffuse light because shadow exaggerates or softens the surface relief.

Color is a phenomenon of light and visual perception. The source map describes it through hue, value, temperature, and intensity or chroma. Color can distinguish a form from its surroundings and affect visual weight, but Batch 130 avoids unsupported universal psychology claims such as one color always producing the same emotion in every context.

Light and shadow reveal form through openings, edges, and depth. Three-dimensional openings can create a pattern or texture of light, shade, and shadow, making a wall read as thick, layered, or porous instead of flat.

## Real World Scenario

In a tropical classroom corridor, the exam-safe question is not whether shadow is good or bad. The better review asks whether deep openings reduce glare, whether surface texture makes light legible, and whether color/value contrast helps students read doors, edges, and circulation points.
