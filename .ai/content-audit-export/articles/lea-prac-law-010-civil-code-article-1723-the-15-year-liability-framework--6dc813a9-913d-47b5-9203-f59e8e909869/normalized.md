## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- State the 15-year liability period under Civil Code Article 1723.
- Identify who bears liability: the architect (AoR) and the contractor are solidarily liable.
- Distinguish Article 1723 from Article 1720 (engineer liability) and Article 2176 (quasi-delict).
- Apply the 15-year prescription period to exam fact patterns.

### The Fifteen-Year Exposure Window from Building Completion

Civil Code Article 1723 establishes the foundational liability rule for architects and engineers in Philippine law: the engineer or architect is liable if within 15 years from completion of the structure, it collapses by reason of a defect in the plans and specifications or defects in the ground. This critical timeline defines when an architect or engineer is exposed to damages claims for structural defects or building collapse. The 15-year clock begins on the day the building is officially completed and accepted, not from the date the construction contract was signed or when the building permit was issued. For example, if a building's construction was completed on March 15, 2020, the 15-year liability period extends until March 15, 2035, after which no liability for structural defects accrues under this article. This extended window reflects the reality that structural and foundational defects often emerge only years after construction, as environmental stresses, weather cycles, and settlement patterns develop over time.

### Liability for Defective Plans and Specifications

Article 1723 holds _the architect or engineer who drew the plans and specifications liable for damages if the building collapses by reason of a defect in those plans, or due to defects in the ground_. Defective plans encompass errors in structural design, load calculations, material specifications, detailing, or any design flaw that directly caused the building's failure. Liability for plan defects is _strict_ under this article—the architect bears responsibility even if the contractor failed to follow the specifications exactly, provided the defect originated in the design itself. An architect cannot escape liability by blaming a contractor's poor workmanship if the architectural design itself was fundamentally flawed. The liability extends to structural conceptualization, material selection, and environmental considerations like soil bearing capacity and geological hazards that should have been analyzed during design.

### Liability for Defective Construction and Solidary Liability

When a building collapses due to _defects in construction or use of inferior materials_, both the _contractor and the architect who supervised construction bear liability solidarily_. This means the party suffering loss may sue either or both defendants, and the defendants are jointly and severally liable for the entire judgment amount. If an architect served as Architect-in-Charge of Construction and supervised the contractor's work, she assumed a dual responsibility: first, to verify that construction conformed to plans and specifications, and second, to detect and correct deficiencies during progress. An architect cannot delegate away this supervisory duty—failure to discover inferior materials, poor workmanship, or deviation from specifications, despite opportunity to do so, constitutes negligence. The contractor and architect cannot modify this solidary liability by contract; it is imposed by law to protect building owners and the public.

### The Ten-Year Prescriptive Period and Two Temporal Clocks

Civil Code Article 1723 establishes a _second independent timeline: the action must be brought within 10 years following the collapse of the building_. This creates two temporal mechanisms: the 15-year exposure window (from completion) and the 10-year statute of limitations (from collapse). For example, if a building collapses in year 12 of the 15-year period, the owner has 10 years from that collapse date to file suit. If no collapse occurs by year 15, no liability attaches under Article 1723. These dual timelines apply only if collapse occurs within 15 years from completion: the action must then be brought within 10 years following the collapse. The 10-year statute of limitations is strictly construed; suits filed even one day late are time-barred and must be dismissed.

### Acceptance of Building and Waiver of Rights

Article 1723 provides (as interpreted in jurisprudence and secondary sources) that acceptance of the building after completion does not imply waiver of any cause of action by reason of any defect mentioned. This provision prevents architects and contractors from requiring building owners to surrender their legal rights as a condition of project completion. An owner's acceptance of keys and occupation of the building does not extinguish the owner's right to sue for defects discovered later. However, the owner must still comply with the 15-year window and 10-year post-collapse filing deadline. Furthermore, the provision does not prevent parties from negotiating express written waivers or compromise agreements that explicitly release all claims; the law prevents only _implied_ waiver through acceptance alone. A prudent architect should retain documentation of the owner's acknowledgment of known defects and agreed-upon remediation, as such records provide evidence of actual knowledge and consent.
