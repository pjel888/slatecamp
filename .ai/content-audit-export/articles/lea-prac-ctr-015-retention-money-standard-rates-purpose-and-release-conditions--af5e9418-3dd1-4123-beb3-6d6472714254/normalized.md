## Retention Money — Standard Rates, Purpose, and Release Conditions
## Learning Goal

After reading this article, you should be able to:

- Define retention money and explain its purpose: performance security, defect remediation fund, and completion incentive.
- Identify standard retention rates (5%-10%) and how retention is applied to each certified payment.
- Describe the conditions for release of retention at Substantial Completion and Final Completion.
- Apply retention money rules to LEA scenarios involving contractor default, punch-list completion, and final accounting.

Retention money is a percentage of contract payments withheld by the owner as security for contractor performance, quality of work, and defect remediation during and after construction.

### Purpose and Protective Function of Retention

Retention money serves multiple protective purposes for the owner. It provides financial leverage encouraging the contractor to complete all work to specification and maintain progress on schedule. The contractor's knowledge that a percentage of earned payment will be withheld until project completion creates incentive for diligent performance and timely finishing of all items.

Retention also provides a fund to cover costs if the contractor defaults before completion. If the contractor abandons the project or fails to complete work, the owner can use retention funds to pay a replacement contractor or hire labor to finish incomplete items. Additionally, retention serves as security for the contractor's obligation to remedy defects discovered during the defects liability period. If the contractor fails to correct punch-list items or warranty defects, the owner can draw from retention funds to pay for needed corrections.

### Standard Retention Rates and Contract Specification

Retention rates of 5% to 10% of contract payments are standard industry practice. A typical specification might require 5% retention on the contract price with the rate applied to each monthly certified payment. For example, if the architect certifies payment of 100,000 in a given month, the owner pays the contractor 95,000 and retains 5,000 until final completion.

The contract documents must specify the retention percentage clearly to avoid disputes. Some contracts specify different rates for different phases—a higher rate during construction and a reduced rate as the project nears completion. The engineer or architect should be involved in establishing the retention percentage as part of contract negotiation, ensuring the retained amount provides adequate security without creating excessive financial hardship for the contractor.

### Calculation and Application of Retention

Retention is calculated and deducted from each monthly or periodic payment throughout the project duration. The owner maintains a retention account holding all withheld funds. The architect's payment certificates explicitly identify both the total certified amount and the retention deduction, ensuring transparency to all parties. The contractor can track how much retention has accumulated over the project period.

The total retention amount grows as the project progresses through multiple payment periods. On a contract of 1 million with 5% retention, the retained amount can accumulate to 50,000 or more depending on the payment schedule and project duration. This significant amount provides meaningful security motivating contractor performance while remaining proportional to contract value.

### Release Conditions and Final Payment

The primary condition for release of retention is satisfactory completion of all work to contract specifications. The architect must verify that all items on the punch list have been corrected, all visible defects have been remedied, and the work is ready for final acceptance. Only after this verification does the owner release retention to the contractor as final payment.

Many contracts specify that retention is released in two tranches: partial release upon substantial completion and full release upon final completion. For example, the contract might provide that 50% of retention is released when substantial completion is certified, with the remaining 50% released after the one-year defects liability period expires and all warranty defects have been corrected.

### Retention During the Defects Liability Period

The one-year defects liability period creates particular importance for retention. During this year, defects may emerge that the contractor must remedy. Retention money held during this period provides security ensuring the contractor will correct these defects when discovered. If retention has been fully released upon substantial completion, the owner loses leverage to compel the contractor to perform warranty repairs.

Some contracts require that a portion of retention be held through the entire defects liability period specifically to ensure contractor responsiveness to defect remediation. The contractor has incentive to correct defects promptly to receive the final retention payment. Without retention holding, contractors may delay warranty work or ignore minor defects.

### Owner's Obligation to Account for Retention

The owner has a fiduciary obligation to account for and properly manage retained funds. Retention funds belong to the contractor, not the owner, even though the owner holds them. The owner must not commingle retention funds with the owner's general operating funds or use them for purposes unrelated to project security.

Interest on retained funds creates a point of contract negotiation. Some contracts specify that the owner must pay the contractor interest on retention funds, recognizing that the owner holds the contractor's money. Other contracts are silent on interest, resulting in the owner retaining use of the funds interest-free throughout the project duration.
