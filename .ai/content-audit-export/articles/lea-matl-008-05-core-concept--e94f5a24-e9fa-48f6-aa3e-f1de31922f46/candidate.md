## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal

Classify adhesive systems (construction adhesive, epoxy, contact cement, PVA glue, polyurethane, silicone, acrylic) by bonding strength, setting time, substrate compatibility, and application, and select the correct adhesive for structural, semi-structural, and non-structural bonding.
## Core Concept

Adhesives create permanent bonds between substrates (wood, concrete, metal, glass) through mechanical interlocking or chemical adhesion. Sealants remain flexible after curing, accommodating joint movement and preventing air and water infiltration. The primary difference is that adhesives are rigid after cure (low flexibility), while sealants remain elastic (high flexibility).

Elastomeric sealants are classified by ASTM C920 based on joint movement capability. Class 25 sealants accommodate ±25 percent movement (equal tension and compression). Class 50 sealants accommodate ±50 percent movement. Class 100 sealants accommodate +100 percent extension but only -50 percent compression. Silicone sealants are typically Class 50 or higher (Class 50 or 100/50), providing excellent movement accommodation; however, some formulations may be Class 25 or 35 and water resistance, with a service life of 20+ years in temperate climates. Polyurethane sealants are Class 50, providing good adhesion to diverse substrates, though less UV resistant than silicone. Acrylic sealants are water-based, Class 12.5 or lower, suitable for low-movement joints, with cure times of 24-48 hours and shorter service life (10-15 years) than silicone. Polysulfide sealants are typically Class 25 or higher (Class 25, 35, 50, or 100/50 depending on formulation), historically used in joints with moderate to high movement, but largely replaced by polyurethane and silicone. Butyl sealants are single-part, non-hardening products used for glazing and weather sealing in windows, with indefinite life but minimal movement accommodation.

Joint movement depends on slab size and temperature swing. A 100-foot-long concrete slab with a temperature change from 40°F to 90°F (50-degree swing) and thermal expansion coefficient of 0.004 inches per degree Fahrenheit per inch expands: 100 feet × 12 inches/foot × 0.004 × 50°F = 24 inches total. If expansion joints are spaced 40 feet apart (typical), each joint must accommodate 24 ÷ (100/40) = 9.6 inches of movement. ASTM C920 specifies that joint width must be at least twice the expected movement; a joint accommodating 9.6 inches of movement requires a width of at least 20 inches (impractical). Therefore, closer joint spacing (20-25 feet) is preferred, reducing per-joint movement to manageable levels.

Construction adhesives bond substrates without mechanical fasteners. Epoxy adhesives are two-part, high-strength, suitable for concrete-to-concrete and metal-to-concrete applications, with cure times of 24-48 hours. Polyurethane adhesives are moisture-curing, expanding slightly during cure to fill gaps, suitable for wood-to-wood and wood-to-metal applications. Silicone adhesives are flexible, used for movement-accommodating applications. Pressure-sensitive adhesives adhere immediately upon pressure application, used in tapes and membranes.

## Philippine Construction Context

The Philippines' tropical climate (85-95°F year-round, 80+ percent humidity during monsoon) extends sealant and adhesive cure times significantly compared to temperate regions. Acrylic sealants, sensitive to rain during cure, are difficult to use during the 6-month monsoon season (May-November) when construction typically slows. Polyurethane adhesives and sealants are preferred in the Philippines due to moisture-curing capability and shorter cure times than two-part epoxy. Water absorption in tropical concrete (due to high humidity and rain exposure) compromises adhesive performance if primer is omitted; thorough surface preparation is critical. Silicone sealants are expensive but justified for high-performance applications; many projects substitute inferior acrylic sealants, resulting in premature failure and water damage. UV exposure from intense tropical sun degrades acrylic and some polyurethane products faster than in temperate climates; color fade and adhesion loss occur within 5-7 years instead of 10-15 years. Thermal cycling in air-conditioned buildings (cool interior, hot exterior) creates stress on sealants at perimeter joints; polyurethane and silicone are superior choices. Many Philippine contractors lack understanding of joint movement requirements, resulting in undersized joints that fail under thermal expansion.

## How It's Tested

**Exam Pattern 1: Sealant Class Selection.** A concrete slab with 40-foot expansion joint spacing experiences a 60-degree Fahrenheit temperature swing. Using a thermal expansion coefficient of 0.004 in/in/°F, what is the required joint width, and what sealant class is appropriate? Answer: Joint width = (40 ft × 12 in/ft × 0.004 × 60°F) = 11.52 inches; minimum joint width = 2 × 5.76 = 11.52 inches. A silicone Class 50 sealant accommodating ±50 percent movement is appropriate.

**Exam Pattern 2: Adhesive Product Selection.** A concrete wall must be bonded to a wood frame under high moisture conditions. Which adhesive type accommodates moisture during cure? Answer: Polyurethane adhesive, which is moisture-curing and expands to fill gaps.

**Exam Pattern 3: Tropical Cure Time Adjustment.** An epoxy adhesive requires 24-hour cure in temperate climates. For a Philippine project with 90°F and 85 percent humidity, estimate the cure time. Answer: Multiply by 1.5-2.0: expected cure time = 36-48 hours (or even longer if temperature remains below 75°F at night).

**Exam Pattern 4: Incompatibility Risk.** A specification calls for silicone sealant on top of acrylic sealant in an existing joint that failed. What is the problem? Answer: Silicone will not adhere to acrylic; the old sealant must be completely removed, or incompatibility will cause adhesion failure.
