# Gothic Architecture

## LEA Point

Gothic architecture is the integrated system of pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and flying buttresses that lets walls become lighter screens for stained glass and clerestory light. The safest exam anchors are Chartres, Amiens, Reims, Cologne, Sainte-Chapelle, pointed arch, ribbed vault, flying buttress, triforium, clerestory, verticality, and colored light.

## Source Anchors

- UNESCO Chartres, Amiens, Reims, and Cologne anchor major Gothic cathedral recognition.
- The official Sainte-Chapelle page anchors the chapel as a high-yield Gothic glass-and-light example.
- The local Fletcher conversion anchors pointed arches, ribbed vaults, flying buttresses, Gothic elevation, and the French Gothic structural system.

## Structural Pattern

The pointed arch directs loads down through narrower supports. The ribbed vault concentrates the roof load along ribs. The flying buttress carries lateral thrust over the aisle to an exterior pier. Together, these parts reduce the wall's load-bearing role, allowing taller interiors and expanded stained glass.

## Exam Guardrails

- Do not define Gothic by pointed arches alone. The exam-safe definition is the whole structural system.
- Do not import unsupported exact glass areas, window counts, or scene counts.
- Do not treat Sainte-Chapelle architect attribution as a safe claim unless a local capture supports it; use the chapel as a glass-and-light recognition anchor instead.
- Distinguish Gothic lightness from Romanesque heavy wall and barrel-vault logic.

## Real World Scenario

A board item says lateral vault thrust is carried over the aisle by an exterior half-arch to an outer pier. The source-safe answer is flying buttress, and the style is Gothic.
