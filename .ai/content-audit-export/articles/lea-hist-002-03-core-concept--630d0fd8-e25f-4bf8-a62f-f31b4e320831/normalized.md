## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal

Identify Gothic architectural innovations — pointed arch, ribbed vault, flying buttress — and explain how the structural system enabled verticality, natural light, and the skeletal stone framework exemplified at Chartres, Notre Dame, and Amiens.
## Core Concept

**Gothic architecture** (mid-12th–16th centuries) represents the culmination of Medieval ambition: to build impossibly tall cathedrals with walls so thin they nearly disappear, flooded with stained-glass light. The innovation is not decorative but *structural*—a complete rethinking of how to distribute weight.

The **pointed arch** replaces the Romanesque round arch. A pointed arch distributes weight more efficiently, with thrust directed downward and slightly outward along its legs rather than purely outward as a round arch does. This subtle engineering shift enables higher vaults and thinner walls.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0002 component="Tip" status="preserved"]

The **ribbed vault** evolved from Romanesque decoration into a structural skeleton. In Gothic, ribs carry vault weight to specific points—typically the corners and mid-spans of bays. This concentration means the vault surface between ribs can be lighter (sometimes just infill web), and the ribs themselves can span longer distances. Ribs channel load vertically.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Note" status="preserved"]

The **flying buttress** is the revolutionary external element. An external half-arch transfers the lateral thrust of the vault from the upper nave wall to a distant outer pier, **outside the building envelope**. This liberation of interior space is everything: walls no longer need massive thickness to contain vault thrust. Walls become *screens* (though still load-bearing). Windows can grow to fill the wall surface.

Together—**pointed arch + ribbed vault + external flying buttress**—these three elements form an integrated *skeleton*. Weight flows from the vault ribs down to the walls, then via flying buttresses to external piers, then straight down to the ground. The system is rational, efficient, and visually transparent.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0004 component="Warn" status="preserved"]

**Aesthetic consequences:**
- **Verticality dominates**. Pointed arches and tall proportions draw the eye upward; exterior elevation soars vertically
- **Interior light becomes theology**. Stained glass floods the interior, transforming stone into colored illumination. This symbolizes divine light and sacred space
- **Skeletal transparency**. Viewers sense the structural frame—external buttresses, diagonal bracing—making the building itself a diagram of forces
- **Visual complexity increases**. Tracery (stone framework in windows), moldings, ribs, and buttresses create intricate surface patterns absent in Romanesque
- **Height as aspiration**. Cathedrals compete to build higher, creating soaring spatial experiences and engineering spectacle

**Regional variations:**
- **French Gothic**: Classical proportions, emphasis on verticality, perfect harmony of elements (Chartres, Amiens, Reims)
- **English Gothic**: Emphasis on horizontal length (nave extends deep), elaborate vaulting (fan vaults in Perpendicular period), decorated tracery
- **Italian Gothic**: Facade emphasis (stripes and arcades), shorter and broader proportions, less skeletal than French
- **Iberian Gothic**: Strong fortress influence, regional geometric patterns

## Key Facts and Testable Items

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0005 component="WikiExamTable" status="preserved"]

**Structural Terminology:**
- **Pointed arch**: An arch that comes to a peak at its apex; distributes weight vertically and slightly outward, enabling thinner walls
- **Ribbed vault**: A vault system where ribs (arches) carry weight to specific support points; allows the vault web (surface) to be lighter
- **Flying buttress**: An external half-arch transferring lateral vault thrust to an outer pier, allowing interior walls to be thin
- **Tracery**: Ornamental stone framework in windows, typically branching and intricate
- **Triforium**: A gallery above the main arcade but below the clerestory windows
- **Clerestory**: The upper windows of the nave, often in the vault springline
- **Pier**: A vertical support element (typically thicker and more substantial than a column)
- **Buttress**: A vertical exterior projection strengthening a wall or pier

## Key Terms

- **Pointed arch**: An arch coming to a peak; distributes weight more efficiently than a round arch
- **Ribbed vault**: A vault system with structural ribs carrying weight to specific points; primary Gothic structural element
- **Flying buttress**: An external half-arch transferring lateral thrust of a vault to an outer pier; enables thin walls
- **Tracery**: Ornamental stone framework in windows, often branching and geometric
- **Stained glass**: Colored glass used as a wall material, especially dominant in Gothic
- **Clerestory**: Upper windows of a nave, allowing light to flood the interior space
- **Triforium**: A secondary gallery above the main arcade, below clerestory
- **Transept**: The cross arm of a cruciform church
- **Nave**: The central longitudinal space of a church
- **Buttress**: A vertical exterior projection strengthening walls or piers
