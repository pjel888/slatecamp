## What You Need to Know for the LEA

The architect-client relationship rests on good faith and the architect's unquestioned loyalty to the client. The architect must disclose all conflicts of interest, refuse compensation from third parties without client consent, and prioritize client welfare even when this conflicts with fee maximization.

## Core Concept

Section 2 of the Code establishes that the architect-client relationship is "depended upon good faith" and that the architect's position "carries with it certain moral obligations to his Client and to himself." This creates a **fiduciary relationship**—the architect is a trusted adviser who places the client's interests above the architect's own profit.

The obligation has six interconnected dimensions. First, the architect must maintain absolute **loyalty**—defined in §2-h as receiving compensation "solely through his professional fee charged directly to the Client" with "no other returns in whatever form from any interested source other than the Client." Any offer of compensation from contractors, suppliers, material manufacturers, or competitors is suspect and must be disclosed. Accepting such compensation without disclosure constitutes a breach of fiduciary duty.

Second, the architect must disclose **conflicts of interest** fully. Section 2-i requires: "If the Architect has any business interest which will relate to, or affect the interest of his Client, he should inform his Client of such condition or situation." This is not optional or discretionary. The test is whether the business interest "tend[s] to weaken and discredit his standing as an unprejudiced and honest adviser." A relationship with a building supplier, contractor, property developer, or financing entity that even _potentially_ influences recommendations must be disclosed.

Third, the architect must **refuse money from third parties**. Section 2-h is unambiguous: the architect "shall not accept nor ask for any other returns in whatever form from any interested source other than the Client." This encompasses commissions, discounts (except market discounts credited to the client per §4-b), gifts, favors, or benefits from contractors, suppliers, manufacturers, or any other party with financial interest in the project.

Fourth, the architect must maintain **confidentiality**. Although not explicitly detailed in Section 2, the implied duty of a fiduciary is to protect client information. The architect cannot disclose the client's budget, timeline, decision-making process, negotiating position, or proprietary information to contractors or competitors.

Fifth, the architect must advise the client truthfully, even when advice costs the architect money. Section 2-c requires: "The Architect shall advise a Client against proceeding with any project whose practicability may be questionable due to financial, legal or arresting or exigent conditions, even if such advice may mean the loss of a prospective commission to the Architect." This supersedes fee maximization.

Sixth, fees must be fair and transparent. Section 2-f requires fees "commensurate with the work involved and with his professional standing and experience based upon the Basic Minimum Fee prescribed under the 'Standards of Professional Practice.'" The architect cannot undercut fees or engage in fee competition that degrades the profession (§5-b).

## Philippine Practice Context

Under RA 9266 §33, the architect retains copyright in all drawings and specifications prepared, but the client receives a right to use these documents for the commissioned project only. This ownership distinction reinforces the fiduciary relationship: the architect owns the intellectual property but serves the client's interests. The architect cannot reuse design solutions or documents from one client's project for another client without explicit consent—this would breach confidentiality and loyalty.

The Professional Regulatory Board of Architecture (PRBoA) has consistently disciplined architects who accepted contractor payments, discounts, or commissions without client knowledge. In one case, an architect received 5% from a preferred contractor in exchange for steering contracts to that contractor; this resulted in suspension. The Board views such arrangements as inherent conflicts that distort professional judgment.

Architects employed by government agencies face heightened scrutiny: their loyalty obligation extends to the public (as taxpayers), not just to the government entity as contracting party. An architect in a government office cannot accept benefits from suppliers seeking government contracts.

## How It's Tested

**Pattern 1: Undisclosed Compensation**
- Scenario: A contractor offers architect 8% commission on project costs. Architect accepts but tells client he received "market-rate fee."
- Violation: §2-h (undisclosed compensation violates sole-source requirement). The architect must disclose and refuse or obtain client consent.

**Pattern 2: Conflict of Interest Concealment**
- Scenario: Architect is part-owner of a building supply company and specifies that company's materials. Client not informed.
- Violation: §2-i (undisclosed conflict of interest). The financial interest "tends to weaken... standing as unprejudiced adviser."

**Pattern 3: Impractical Project Advice**
- Scenario: Client requests design the architect knows is financially unfeasible. Architect proceeds to avoid losing the commission.
- Violation: §2-c (duty to advise against impractical projects "even if such advice may mean the loss of a prospective commission").

**Pattern 4: Fixed-Sum Construction Contract**
- Scenario: Architect designs building, then agrees to construct it for fixed lump sum.
- Violation: §2-g (architect "shall not undertake, under a fixed contract sum agreement, the construction of any project based on plans prepared by him").

<Tip>**Memory Aid: "FAIR"—Fiduciary, Advised truthfully, Interested parties disclosed, Received only from client.** If a question involves third-party compensation, conflicts of interest, or impractical project advice, apply the FAIR test. Anything that weakens the architect's standing as "unprejudiced and honest adviser" violates §2-i.</Tip>
