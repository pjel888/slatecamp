# Early Christian and Byzantine architecture

## LEA Point

Early Christian architecture adapts the basilica into a processional worship hall. Byzantine architecture turns Christian space toward centralized domes, pendentives, mosaics, and luminous interiors. The safest exam anchors are Old St. Peter's type basilica logic, Hagia Sophia, San Vitale at Ravenna, SS Sergius and Bacchus, pendentives, and mosaic/light programs.

## Source Anchors

- UNESCO Historic Areas of Istanbul anchors Hagia Sophia inside the Byzantine urban and religious context.
- UNESCO Ravenna anchors Early Christian and Byzantine monuments, including San Vitale and mosaic interiors.
- The local Fletcher conversion anchors basilica organization, Byzantine centralized planning, pendentives, Hagia Sophia, San Vitale, and mosaic/light treatment.

## Core Pattern

The basilica is longitudinal: nave, aisles, apse, and processional movement toward the altar. Byzantine design is centralized and vertical: a dome can sit over a square bay through pendentives, turning the interior into a unified sacred volume. Mosaic and reflected light are not just decoration; they organize the spiritual reading of the space.

## Exam Guardrails

- Do not call flying buttresses the defining Byzantine structural device; the high-yield device is the pendentive.
- Do not confuse Old St. Peter's basilican movement with Hagia Sophia's centralized dome system.
- Avoid exact Hagia Sophia dimensions and exact window counts when the source-safe answer only needs dome, pendentive, light, and centralized space.
- Treat icon and image debates cautiously unless a future source map includes theological primary sources.

## Real World Scenario

A board item describes triangular curved surfaces that transfer a circular dome onto a square bay. The source-safe answer is pendentive, and the high-yield building association is Byzantine architecture and Hagia Sophia.
