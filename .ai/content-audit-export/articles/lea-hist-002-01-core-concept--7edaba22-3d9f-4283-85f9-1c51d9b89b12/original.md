## What You Need to Know for the LEA

<Callout>
Early Christian architecture established the **basilica** as the dominant church form, directly influencing all Western religious architecture. Byzantine refinement of this type, especially through the **pendentive dome**, creates structural and design questions on every board exam.
</Callout>

## Core Concept

Early Christian and Byzantine architecture represent the bridge between Roman building traditions and Medieval Europe. Early Christians (4th–5th centuries) inherited Roman **basilica** forms—rectangular halls with colonnaded aisles—but repurposed them as places of worship after Constantine's legalization of Christianity in 313 CE. The altar replaced the Roman magistrate's seat; the longitudinal plan emphasized procession toward the sacred east end.

Byzantine architecture (6th–15th centuries), centered in Constantinople, synthesized Greek refinement, Roman engineering, and Eastern opulence. The defining innovation was the **pendentive dome**—a spherical triangle allowing a circular dome to rest on a square base. This solution created the iconic Byzantine church form: a centralized, domed structure that unified interior space vertically.

<Tip>
**Memory Aid: Basilica vs. Byzantine.** Basilica = Long rectangle (procession east), columns divide space. Byzantine = Square base with dome above (unity under dome), columns are few. Basilica is linear; Byzantine is centralized. This shift is testable.
</Tip>

**Hagia Sophia** (532–537 CE, **Anthemius of Tralles** and **Isidore of Miletus**, Constantinople) remains the supreme example. Its massive dome (31.7 meters diameter) appears to hover weightlessly above a square structure, achieved through pendentives and 40 windows around the dome's base creating a luminous effect. The interior rises to 55.6 meters from floor to dome apex. The interior space flows unified beneath the dome, establishing the Byzantine ideal of spiritual light entering physical form.

<Note>
**Key Clarification:** The pendentive is a specific structural innovation allowing a circular dome to rest on a square base. Do not confuse pendentives with barrel vaults or other vault types. This distinction is essential for understanding Byzantine structural innovation.
</Note>

Early Christian basilicas stayed linear (Old St. Peter's Basilica, Rome, begun 326–333 CE, completed ~360 CE). Byzantine churches evolved toward centralized plans with domes, narthex (entry vestibule), and multiple apses. Icons—painted religious images—became central to Byzantine worship, especially after the Iconoclastic period (726–843 CE) resolved that representational sacred art was theologically acceptable.

<Warn>
**Critical Distinction:** Do not confuse Early Christian and Byzantine periods. Early Christian (4th–5th c.) = linear basilicas; Byzantine (6th–15th c.) = domed, centralized. A 5th-century basilica is not Byzantine. The shift from linear to centralized is a major architectural development tied to theological and liturgical changes.
</Warn>

Byzantine aesthetics emphasize:
- **Luminous interior surfaces** through colored marble, gold tesserae mosaics, and gilt
- **Mystical verticality** drawing the eye upward toward heaven
- **Mathematical precision** in proportions and structural geometry
- **Imperial monumentality** reflecting Constantinople's political power

**Structural Terms:**
- **Pendentive**: A spherical triangle that transitions a square base to a circular dome opening
- **Vault**: Curved ceiling structure (barrel vault, groin vault)
- **Narthex**: Entrance vestibule of a church
- **Apse**: Semicircular or polygonal recess in a church, typically housing the altar
- **Transept**: Cross arm in a cruciform (cross-shaped) church plan

## Key Terms

- **Basilica**: A rectangular church with a nave flanked by aisles; established as the dominant Early Christian and Western church form
- **Pendentive**: A spherical triangle enabling a circular dome to rest on a square base; key Byzantine structural innovation
- **Dome**: Hemispherical or near-hemispherical vault; dominant in Byzantine centralized plans
- **Narthex**: Entrance vestibule of a church; separates secular and sacred space
- **Apse**: Semicircular or polygonal recess typically at the east end of a church, housing the altar
- **Mosaic**: Small colored glass or stone tesserae arranged to form an image; primary Byzantine wall and vault decoration
- **Icon**: A religious image painted on wood or in mosaic; central to Byzantine Orthodox worship
- **Centralized plan**: A church plan organized around a central vertical axis (dome) rather than a linear procession (basilica)
- **Tesserae**: Small glass or stone cubes used in mosaics
- **Transept**: The cross arm of a cruciform church plan
