## What You Need to Know for the LEA
<Callout>

Postmodernism fundamentally disrupted modernism's hegemony by reintroducing historical reference, ornament, symbolism, and contradiction as legitimate design tools. The LEA examines your grasp of key postmodern theorists and architects—Venturi, Jencks, Rossi, Graves, and the deconstructivists—and how their work redefined architectural possibility in the late twentieth century.
</Callout>

## Learning Goal

- Explain how Postmodernism rejected Modernism's reductive purism through Venturi's Complexity and Contradiction (1966) and Learning from Las Vegas (1972).
- Define Jencks's "death of modern architecture" thesis and the demolition of Pruitt-Igoe as symbolic marker.
- Distinguish Deconstructivism from Postmodernism: where Postmodernism plays with historical reference, Deconstructivism fragments and destabilizes form.

## Core Concept

Postmodernism in architecture emerged not as a unified style but as a critical reaction against modernism's reductive purism and its claim to universal, timeless principles. Where modernism sought to strip away historical ornament and craft the "honest" expression of materials and function, postmodernism embraced complexity, irony, historical pastiche, and the semantic richness of symbolic language. Robert Venturi's 1966 manifesto *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture* fundamentally shifted architectural discourse by arguing that modernism's reduction was fundamentally incomplete. "I like elements which are hybrid rather than 'pure,' compromising rather than 'clean,' distorted rather than straightforward, ambiguous rather than articulate," Venturi wrote, inverting modernism's aesthetic hierarchies.

<Tip>Postmodernism's core move: **rehabilitation of ornament and historical reference**. Where modernism said "ornament is crime" (Loos), postmodernism says "ornament is legitimate cultural expression." This single reversal enables everything postmodern—applied symbolism, color, historical allusion. Testable pattern: any question about postmodernism in architecture hinges on this ornament rehabilitation.</Tip>

<Note>Postmodernism is NOT frivolous or anti-intellectual. Venturi's analysis is semiotic and rigorous—he shows HOW meaning operates through decoration and historical quotation. The LEA may test whether students recognize postmodernism as serious critical theory (challenging modernism's philosophical monopoly) rather than mere stylistic whimsy.</Note>

<Warn>Postmodernism collapsed by the 1990s for a crucial reason: if ALL historical references are equally available, how do architects make choices? Pastiche accumulation exhausts itself. Students sometimes think postmodernism "failed"—actually, it succeeded in disrupting modernism's hegemony; its own internal contradictions (how to choose among infinite references?) generated successor movements (deconstructivism, critical regionalism, etc.). Understanding postmodernism's productive failure is crucial to LEA narrative literacy.</Warn>

Charles Jencks, the architecture critic who coined the term "postmodern architecture," positioned postmodernism as architecture's response to broader cultural fragmentation and pluralism. Unlike modernism's monolithic truth claims, postmodernism acknowledged that multiple styles and meanings could coexist within a single building or city. Jencks's concept of "double-coding"—simultaneously addressing an educated architectural elite and a popular audience through layered meanings—became central to postmodern design strategy. Buildings could quote history while being functionally contemporary; they could employ ornament while maintaining rational planning.

Aldo Rossi's *L'architettura della città* (1966) reoriented architecture toward memory and typology rather than functionalist prescription. Rossi argued that cities possess a collective memory embedded in monuments, street patterns, and architectural types that survive economic and political upheavals. His concept of "La Città Analoga" (The Analogous City) suggested that architects should understand buildings as part of an eternal dialogue with historical precedent, drawing analogies between contemporary problems and historical solutions. Rather than inventing forms from first principles, architects should engage in "critical reuse" of archetypal building types—the courthouse, the marketplace, the domestic block. Rossi's own designs, particularly his Gallaratese Housing (Milan, 1969-1976) and San Cataldo Cemetery (Modena, 1971-1984), employed stark geometric forms referencing historical prototypes, creating a poignant tension between modernist abstraction and historical meaning.

Michael Graves emerged as the most commercially successful postmodern architect, translating postmodern principles into fashionable residential and public buildings. Graves's work embraced color, applied ornament, and historical allusion that modernists had categorically rejected. His Portland Building (1982) in Portland, Oregon—a massive postmodern pastiche featuring colorful bands, applied ornament, and classical references rendered in contemporary materials—became the symbol of postmodern architecture's popular emergence. Graves demonstrated that postmodernism could achieve market success; his later work for Disneyland and commercial clients revealed postmodernism's capacity to generate engaging, semantically rich architectural experiences that connected with broader audiences.

Deconstructivism emerged in the 1980s as a more radical critique, translating Jacques Derrida's philosophical deconstruction into architectural form. The 1988 Museum of Modern Art exhibition "Deconstructivist Architecture," curated by Philip Johnson and Mark Wigley, canonized a movement featuring Frank Gehry, Daniel Libeskind, Zaha Hadid, Peter Eisenman, Bernard Tschumi, and Coop Himmelb(l)au. These architects rejected postmodernism's historical quotation as insufficiently critical. Instead, deconstructivism embraced fragmentation, non-Euclidean geometry, and what Wigley called the "violent disruption of stable form." Gehry's Guggenheim Museum Bilbao (1997)—a titanium-clad sculptural mass that appears to defy structural logic—epitomized deconstructivist ambition: architecture as radical formal experimentation liberated from both modernist orthodoxy and postmodern historical pastiche.

Daniel Libeskind's Jewish Museum Berlin (1999) demonstrated how deconstructivism could embody historical trauma and memory. The building's fragmented geometry, absent windows, and symbolic voids (representing the absence of murdered Jewish populations) show how deconstructivist formal language could convey profound emotional and historical content. Libeskind proved that abstraction need not be emotionally cold; fragmentation could be deeply humanistic. Peter Eisenman's theoretical work, particularly his engagement with conceptual grids and palimpsest mapping, established deconstructivism's intellectual foundations, arguing that form need not represent function directly but could explore autonomous spatial and conceptual relationships.

Bernard Tschumi's Parc de la Villette competition design (1983) extended deconstruction into landscape and urban strategy. Tschumi rejected the unified, hierarchically organized park in favor of a disjunctive system where points (follies), lines (paths), and surfaces (programmed zones) operate according to different logics. The red metal "follies" scattered throughout the park—functionally ambiguous structures that refuse conventional typological clarity—became iconic deconstructivist objects. Tschumi's work suggested that architecture and urbanism need not aspire to harmonious unity; productive tensions between contradictory systems could generate spatial richness and experiential complexity.

Contemporary architectural theory has moved beyond postmodernism's historical eclecticism and deconstructivism's radical formalism toward more diverse and globally-inflected approaches. Digital technologies have transformed design methodologies, enabling parametric complexity and algorithmic generation. Sustainability concerns have reoriented practice toward material efficiency and environmental responsibility. Simultaneously, critical regionalism (developed by Kenneth Frampton), phenomenology (following Norberg-Schulz and Peter Zumthor), and feminist and postcolonial perspectives have challenged both modernist and postmodern European dominance, insisting that architecture engage place, embodied experience, and non-Western knowledge systems.

## How It's Tested on the LEA

<ExamTip>1. **Theorist Positioning**: The exam asks where theorists stand relative to modernism and each other. You must understand that Venturi critiques modernism from within architectural humanism, while deconstructivists critique both modernism and postmodernism's historical borrowing as insufficiently radical.</ExamTip>

<ExamTip>2. **Typological vs. Formal Approaches**: Questions distinguish between Rossi's typological method (engage historical building types analogically) and deconstructivism's formal experimentation (liberate form from typological constraint). These represent fundamentally different postmodern strategies.</ExamTip>

<ExamTip>3. **Symbolic vs. Deconstructive Language**: Venturi's architecture speaks through applied symbols (the "decorated shed"), while Libeskind's speaks through deconstructed form itself. Can you explain how each strategy differently mobilizes meaning?</ExamTip>

<ExamTip>4. **Historical vs. Contemporary Contextualization**: Rossi engages history through analogy; contemporary architects engage context through digital responsiveness, sustainability, and cultural specificity. The exam tests your understanding of how architectural method evolves.</ExamTip>

<ExamTip>5. **Architect-Building Attribution**: You must identify which theorist-architect designed which canonical postmodern building. Graves = Portland Building; Gehry = Bilbao; Libeskind = Jewish Museum Berlin.</ExamTip>

## Key Terms

**Double-Coding** — Jencks's term for postmodern architecture that simultaneously addresses elite architectural discourse and popular vernacular understanding through layered symbolic meanings.

**Typology** — In Rossi's theory, recurring building types (the courthouse, marketplace, residential block, tower) that persist across history and cultures, suggesting solutions rooted in collective memory rather than individual invention.

**La Città Analoga** (The Analogous City) — Rossi's concept that contemporary urban problems can be solved by drawing analogies with historical precedents and building types, rather than inventing forms from first principles.

**Deconstruction** — Jacques Derrida's philosophical method of revealing how texts undermine their own stability and meaning; adapted architecturally to disrupt form's apparent stability and coherence.

**Fragmentation** — Deconstructivist strategy of breaking unified form into disjunctive elements, suggesting that coherence is unstable and that productive tensions generate meaning.

**Folly** — Tschumi's term for the non-functional red metal pavilions at Parc de la Villette; structures that refuse conventional typological definition and purpose clarity.

**Parametric Design** — Contemporary methodology using algorithmic and algorithmic relationships to generate form responsively to environmental, programmatic, or conceptual parameters; enabled by digital technology.

**Phenomenology** — Philosophical approach emphasizing lived experience, embodied perception, and the qualitative character of spaces rather than abstract formal or functional properties.

**Critical Regionalism** — Response to postmodern globalization; architecture that respects global modernist principles while remaining grounded in specific place, culture, climate, and material traditions.

**Autonomy of Form** — The notion that architectural form possesses its own logic and meaning independent of functional expression or symbolic reference; central to both deconstructivism and some postmodern theory.
