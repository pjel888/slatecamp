It is 6:30 in the morning in a barangay in Cavite. The Dela Cruz family — father, mother, grandmother, and three children — wake up in a house that is legally classified as a Group A dwelling, a single-family residence. The kitchen is barely large enough for two people to work in simultaneously. The toilet is tucked under the staircase. The youngest child's bedroom gets no direct light from outside; a borrowed window faces a narrow court between the house and the next property. The house was built without a building permit, without a licensed architect, without any reference to the National Building Code of the Philippines.

Three years later, the family is selling the property to buy something bigger. A buyer's due diligence reveals the house was never issued a Certificate of Occupancy. The municipal Building Official inspects the structure and finds that the toilet room's area is only 0.85 square meters — below the 1.20 square meters required by PD 1096. The habitable rooms fail the minimum 6.00 square meter floor area rule. The corner lot coverage is at 92%, exceeding the 90% maximum. The slea falls through. The family loses two months of paperwork, a lawyer's fees, and the buyer's earnest money.

This scenario repeats itself thousands of times across the Philippines every year. The minimum requirements for Group A dwellings under PD 1096, Rule VII, Sections 708-709 — and the room dimension rules under Rule VIII, Sections 805-806 — are not bureaucratic afterthoughts. They are the difference between a home that can be legally sold, refinanced, insured, and occupied versus one that exists in a grey zone of non-compliance. For the LEA candidate, these numbers are not merely memorizable facts; they are the professional obligations you will carry every time you sign and seal a residential plan.

## Core Concepts

### Principle 1: Lot Occupancy Limits — 90% Corner Lot, 80% Inside Lot

Per PD 1096, Rule VII, Sec. 708(a), **a Group A dwelling shall occupy not more than 90% of a corner lot and not more than 80% of an inside lot.**

These percentages refer to the ratio of the building footprint (ground floor area of the structure, including all projections) to the total lot area. Let's be precise about the definitions:

- **Corner lot:** A lot bounded by two intersecting streets. Because it has frontage on two sides, it typically enjoys greater access, visibility, and light, hence the more generous 90% maximum coverage.
- **Inside lot:** A lot bounded by a street on one side only, with neighboring properties on the remaining sides. More constrained, hence the tighter 80% maximum.
- **Lot coverage (or PSO — Percentage of Site Occupancy):** The ratio of the building footprint to the total lot area, expressed as a percentage.

**Why these limits exist:** Lot coverage limits preserve open space within the lot for air circulation, rainwater drainage, emergency access, and the psychological well-being of occupants. In the Philippines' tropical climate, unbuilt lot area also reduces heat island effect, allows for natural drainage (critical given frequent typhoons), and provides buffer space for the structural setback from property lines.

These maximums interact with the **Civil Code of the Philippines**, which Sec. 708(a) explicitly cross-references: the dwelling shall be at least **2 meters from the property line**, subject to Civil Code easements of light and view. This 2-meter setback from the property line creates a mandatory strip of open space around the structure that cannot be built upon. On a narrow lot, this 2-meter setback can significantly reduce the buildable area, potentially making the 80% (inside lot) or 90% (corner lot) maximums academic — the setback constraint may be more binding.

**Calculating buildable area:** If a client owns a 150 sq m inside lot, the maximum building footprint is 150 × 0.80 = 120 sq m. However, the 2-meter setback from all property lines further constrains this. If the lot is 10 m × 15 m, the setback reduces the buildable area to (10 − 4) × (15 − 4) = 6 × 11 = 66 sq m — far less than 120 sq m. The more restrictive constraint governs.

### Principle 2: Minimum Room Sizes for Habitable Rooms — 6.00 sq m, Least Dimension 2.00 m

Per PD 1096, Rule VIII, Sec. 806(1)(a), **rooms for human habitation (bedrooms, living rooms, dining rooms, multi-purpose rooms) require a minimum floor area of 6.00 sq m with a least dimension of no less than 2.00 m.**

Two numbers, two conditions — both must be satisfied simultaneously:

1. **Minimum area:** 6.00 sq m. A room that is 3 m × 2 m = 6 sq m just barely qualifies. A room that is 2.5 m × 2.5 m = 6.25 sq m qualifies on area but must also pass the least-dimension test.
2. **Least dimension:** 2.00 m. This is the shortest side of the room. A room that is 6 m × 1 m = 6 sq m fails because the least dimension (1 m) is below 2.00 m. A corridor-like room with sufficient area but a narrow width is not a habitable room under the Code.

The least-dimension requirement ensures the room is actually usable as a living space and not a glorified hallway. The Philippine minimum of 6.00 sq m represents an absolute floor — it is the smallest room that the Code considers habitable. Most professional practice targets significantly larger dimensions for livability and marketability.

**Referenced provision:** The Sec. 708(e) minimum standards for Group A dwellings explicitly cross-reference Sec. 806 for room sizes. This connection between Rule VII and Rule VIII is frequently tested on board exams in the form of "which section governs the minimum room size for a Group A dwelling?" — the correct answer cites Rule VIII, Sec. 806, invoked by Rule VII, Sec. 708(e).

### Principle 3: Minimum Kitchen Size — 3.00 sq m, Least Dimension 1.50 m

Per PD 1096, Rule VIII, Sec. 806(1)(b), **kitchens require a minimum floor area of 3.00 sq m with a least dimension of no less than 1.50 m.**

Again, both conditions apply simultaneously. A kitchen that is 2 m × 1.5 m = 3.00 sq m qualifies. A kitchen that is 3 m × 1 m = 3.00 sq m fails (least dimension = 1.0 m, below 1.50 m). The 1.50 m minimum width is critical: it ensures a person can stand at the counter and another can pass behind them, which is the ergonomic minimum for a functional kitchen.

**Philippine context:** In informal settlements and socialized housing, kitchen areas are frequently under-sized. Licensed architects designing housing for the National Housing Authority (NHA) or private developers must ensure that even the most compact units meet this 3.00 sq m / 1.50 m minimum. Compliance is not optional regardless of the project's price point.

**Important distinction:** "Kitchen" here means the food preparation space. If a unit has an open-plan layout where the "kitchen" blends into a dining or living area, the Code's minimum still applies to the identifiable kitchen work zone. A pass-through counter in a studio unit does not exempt the kitchen from the minimum area requirement.

### Principle 4: Minimum Bath and Toilet Size — 1.20 sq m, Least Dimension 900 mm

Per PD 1096, Rule VIII, Sec. 806(1)(c), **bath and toilet rooms require a minimum floor area of 1.20 sq m with a least dimension of no less than 900 mm (0.90 m).**

This is the most frequently tested specific number in residential board exam questions. The 1.20 sq m minimum is small — it represents roughly a 1.0 m × 1.2 m footprint, which is the bare minimum needed for a water closet and basin in a Philippine context. The 900 mm least dimension ensures the user can enter, stand, and maneuver without the room being a physical impossibility.

**Practical planning note:** In Philippine residential design, the toilet and bath (T&B) is often one combined room. The 1.20 sq m minimum covers this combined space. If the designer separates the toilet compartment from the bath compartment, each separate space must independently satisfy the minimum — though common sense and the spirit of the Code would require the toilet compartment (at minimum) to meet the 1.20 sq m rule.

**Why 900 mm?** A standard water closet is approximately 350-400 mm wide; clearance in front of the bowl is approximately 500-600 mm. The 900 mm dimension allows for the fixture plus minimum maneuvering clearance. For persons with disabilities (PWD standards under BP 344), the minimum dimensions are much larger (approximately 1.50 m × 1.50 m turning radius), but Section 806 establishes the absolute floor for non-accessible residential design.

### Principle 5: Ceiling Heights — Governed by Rule VIII, Sec. 805

Per PD 1096, Rule VII, Sec. 708(e), ceiling heights for Group A dwellings are governed by **Rule VIII, Sec. 805**. The key provisions of Sec. 805 are:

**For buildings with natural ventilation:**
- Minimum ceiling height: **2.70 m** (floor to ceiling, for all habitable rooms)

**For buildings with artificial/mechanical ventilation:**
- First storey: minimum **2.70 m**
- Second storey: minimum **2.40 m**
- Succeeding storeys (third and above): minimum **2.10 m** (unobstructed head-room clearance)

**For mezzanine floors:**
- Clear ceiling height above and below the mezzanine: not less than **1.80 m**

These ceiling heights reflect the Philippine tropical climate imperative for air circulation. At 2.70 m, a room allows warm air to rise above occupant level, improving thermal comfort in the absence of air conditioning. This is why the 2.70 m minimum is required for naturally ventilated spaces — a lower ceiling in a hot, humid Philippine interior creates a box of trapped hot air that is hazardous to health.

The descending scale for multi-storey buildings with artificial ventilation (2.70 → 2.40 → 2.10) reflects the engineering reality that upper floors in mechanically ventilated buildings can be served by ductwork at lower floor-to-floor heights without sacrificing occupant comfort, since mechanical systems replace the natural convection role of ceiling height.

### Principle 6: Light, Ventilation, and Sanitation Cross-Requirements

Per PD 1096, Rule VII, Sec. 708(b) and (c):

**(b) Light and Ventilation:** Every Group A dwelling must provide adequate light and ventilation per **Sec. 805-811** of Rule VIII. This cross-reference is the formal link between the dwelling requirements of Rule VII and the detailed light/ventilation metrics of Rule VIII.

- Natural ventilation window area: minimum **10% of the floor area** of each room (Sec. 811), with an absolute minimum of **1.00 sq m** regardless of floor area.
- For toilet/bath rooms: window area minimum **1/20 (5%) of floor area**, not less than **240 sq mm**.

**(c) Sanitation:** Every Group A dwelling must have at least:
- **One sanitary toilet**
- **Adequate washing facilities**
- **Adequate drainage facilities**

This minimum sanitation requirement means every Group A dwelling — even a single-room structure — must have a toilet. There is no exception for small structures or temporary use. The "one sanitary toilet" is the absolute minimum; actual household size and BP 344 requirements for larger units may demand more.

### Principle 7: Foundation Requirements — Firm Ground, Adequate Capacity

Per PD 1096, Rule VII, Sec. 708(d), **footings shall be of sufficient size and strength to support the load of the dwelling and shall be built on firm ground.**

While this provision is brief, it carries significant weight in Philippine seismic conditions. The Philippines sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire and experiences frequent earthquakes. "Firm ground" in Philippine practice is interpreted through the National Structural Code of the Philippines (NSCP), which classifies soil types and prescribes foundation depths accordingly. A Group A dwelling built on reclaimed land, soft marine clay, or liquefaction-prone sandy soil requires geotechnical investigation and engineered foundations — the bare requirement of Sec. 708(d) points to this, even if it does not enumerate specific values.

Board exam questions on this provision typically frame the scenario around a dwelling on obviously unsuitable ground (a flood plain, a landslide-prone hillside, reclaimed foreshore land) and ask whether the foundation provision is satisfied. The correct answer invariably invokes the "firm ground" requirement and connects it to the NSCP.

### Principle 8: Section 709 — Requirements for Other Group Occupancies

Per PD 1096, Rule VII, **Sec. 709** extends the principle of minimum occupancy requirements beyond Group A to Groups B through J. While Sec. 709's specific provisions for each group are detailed in the full Code and IRR, the LEA candidate should understand the structural logic:

- **Group A** (Sections 708): Single-family dwellings — the subject of this article.
- **Groups B-J** (Section 709): All other occupancies (residential multi-family, educational, institutional, commercial, industrial, assembly, storage, etc.) have their own minimum requirements, which are generally more stringent in terms of fire resistance, egress, and occupant load calculations.

The principle is hierarchical: Group A (residential, simplest occupancy) has the most permissive minimums. As you move up the occupancy groups, requirements escalate. This hierarchy is a testable concept — knowing that Group A has the most generous minimums compared to other groups helps answer questions about which group triggers a particular requirement first.

|---|---|---|

| **Bath/toilet ventilation** | Window ≥ **1/20 (5%)** of floor area, min **240 sq mm** | IRC R303.3: mechanical ventilation or window ≥ **1.5 sq ft (0.14 sq m)** openable area |

**Key Takeaways from the Comparison:**

1. **Philippine lot coverage is dramatically higher than US norms.** The 80-90% maximum lot coverage allowed in the Philippines for residential dwellings is 1.5 to 3 times the typical 30-60% coverage permitted in US residential zoning. This reflects the different land use pressures in densely populated Philippine urban areas versus lower-density US suburban development patterns.

2. **US habitable room minimum is slightly larger.** The IRC's 70 sq ft (6.50 sq m) minimum habitable room is slightly more than the Philippine 6.00 sq m. More significantly, the IRC requires at least one room to be 120 sq ft (11.15 sq m) — a requirement that has no direct equivalent in PD 1096, which sets a uniform 6.00 sq m minimum without a "primary room" larger-room requirement.

3. **Philippine ceiling heights are higher for naturally ventilated spaces.** The Philippine 2.70 m ceiling for naturally ventilated rooms significantly exceeds the IBC's 2.29 m (7'6") and the IRC's 2.13 m (7'0"). This is a deliberate climate-driven design standard — higher ceilings in tropical conditions improve passive cooling and air circulation.

4. **Philippine window area requirement is larger than IBC but smaller than IRC.** PD 1096 requires 10% of floor area in natural ventilation openings. IBC Section 1203 requires only 4% (for commercial buildings). The IRC requires 8% for habitable rooms. PH's 10% reflects the tropical reliance on natural cross-ventilation in the absence of widespread air conditioning.

5. **US provides a mandatory larger room; PH provides uniform minimums.** The IRC R304.1 requirement for at least one 120 sq ft (11.15 sq m) room ensures that even the most compact US dwelling has one genuinely livable space. PD 1096 does not have this "primary room" concept — all habitable rooms are subject to the same 6.00 sq m minimum.

## Cross-Referencing

### Connection to Rule VIII — Ceiling Heights, Room Sizes, Air Space & Ventilation (Sec. 805-811)

Rule VII, Section 708 is explicitly written as a cross-reference document. It mandates compliance with Group A dwelling requirements and then points to Rule VIII for the technical standards. This architecture in the Code's structure is important: **Rule VII tells you WHAT is required; Rule VIII tells you HOW MUCH.**

- Sec. 708(b) → Sec. 805-811 (light and ventilation details)
- Sec. 708(e) → Sec. 806 (room sizes) and Sec. 805 (ceiling heights)

The LEA candidate must be fluent in navigating between these rules. A board question about a Group A dwelling's kitchen minimum may cite Sec. 708 as the source of the requirement but expects you to cite Sec. 806 for the specific dimensions. A question about ceiling height in a Group A bedroom expects Sec. 805 as the answer, not just Sec. 708.

**Air Space Requirement (Sec. 807):** While not directly cited in Sec. 708, the air space per person requirement of Sec. 807 applies to habitable rooms in Group A dwellings. Habitable rooms require **14.00 cubic meters of air space per person.** This is a three-dimensional constraint — if your room meets the 6.00 sq m floor area and 2.70 m ceiling height, it contains 6.00 × 2.70 = 16.20 cu m, which supports one occupant under Sec. 807 (14.00 cu m needed) with minimal margin. A room housing two people would need 28 cu m, requiring either a larger floor area or a higher ceiling.

### Connection to Rule VII — Location on Property (Sec. 704)

Article 11 of this series covered exterior wall fire resistance and opening limitations under Sec. 704. The 2-meter setback from the property line required by Sec. 708(a) interacts with Sec. 704's requirements: a dwelling that is set back 2 meters from the side property line may or may not need fire-rated exterior walls on that side, depending on the Secretary's prescribed fire separation thresholds and the applicable construction type. The setback is a minimum for easement compliance, not a guarantee of no fire-rating requirement.

The lot coverage maximums (80% inside lot, 90% corner lot) also connect to the location rules: a building that maximizes its lot coverage will have minimal unbuilt area around it, which affects both the fire separation distances of Sec. 704 and the available space for the required 2-meter setback. On small lots, the mathematics of coverage percentage + setback minimum can produce a net buildable area much smaller than the headline coverage percentage suggests.

### Connection to Rule V — Fire Zones (Sec. 501-507)

The fire zone designation of the lot determines the allowable construction type for the Group A dwelling. A single-family residence in a Non-Fire Restricted Zone may be built of bamboo, sawali, nipa, and wood (Types I and II Construction). The same dwelling in a Fire Restrictive Zone must meet at least 1-hour fire-resistive construction standards (Types II-IV). In a Highly Fire Restrictive Zone, the house must be Types IV or V Construction — reinforced concrete or structural steel — with fire-resistive exterior walls.

This creates a tiered cost structure for Group A dwellings based on their location. A family in a rural barangay (Non-Fire Restricted Zone) can legally build a nipa hut under PD 1096. A family in a dense urban barangay (Highly Fire Restrictive Zone) must build in concrete and steel. Both are "Group A dwellings" under Rule VII, Sec. 708, but their required construction standards differ radically due to Rule V.

### Connection to BP 344 — Accessibility Law

Batas Pambansa Bilang 344 (Accessibility Law) and its Implementing Rules impose minimum space standards that typically exceed the PD 1096 minimums. For accessible toilet rooms, BP 344 requires a turning radius of approximately 1.50 m × 1.50 m — far larger than the 1.20 sq m minimum of Sec. 806. While BP 344 formally applies to public buildings and facilities, any Group A dwelling that claims accessible design must meet BP 344 standards, which supersede the more permissive PD 1096 minimums for those specific elements.

The LEA candidate should recognize that PD 1096 establishes absolute minimums, not design targets. Professional practice — and compliance with related laws like BP 344 — consistently demands spaces well above these minimums.

### Connection to the Civil Code of the Philippines

Section 708(a) explicitly states the 2-meter setback is "subject to Civil Code easements of light and view." The Civil Code's Articles 667-673 establish the right of an adjoining property owner to light and view, including the prohibition against openings in walls directly on the property boundary and the required minimum distances for windows looking into neighboring property. These Civil Code provisions operate as an **independent legal constraint** on top of the building code requirements.

A wall built exactly 2.0 m from the property line may comply with Sec. 708(a) but still violate Civil Code Article 670, which requires windows in a wall less than 3 meters from the boundary to be equipped with a fixed iron grating and wire screen to protect the neighbor's privacy. The architect must satisfy both bodies of law simultaneously.

### Connection to NSCP (National Structural Code of the Philippines)

The foundation requirement of Sec. 708(d) — "firm ground" and "sufficient size and strength" — is technically implemented through the NSCP, which provides the engineering standards for soil bearing capacity, foundation depth, and seismic design requirements. In a country with the Philippines' seismic and volcanic hazard profile, "firm ground" is a loaded phrase. The NSCP classifies soil into Site Classes (from rock to soft clay) and mandates different foundation depths and types for each. Compliance with Sec. 708(d) requires, at a minimum, knowledge of NSCP foundation provisions and, in many cases, geotechnical investigation reports.

## Mnemonic Devices

### Mnemonic 1: "6-3-1.2" — The Three Room Minimums, In Order

> **6** — Habitable room minimum: **6.00 sq m**
> **3** — Kitchen minimum: **3.00 sq m**
> **1.2** — Bath/Toilet minimum: **1.20 sq m**

Read it as a descending sequence: six, three, one-point-two. Each number is exactly half the previous one (6 → 3 → 1.5... close enough for memory purposes, given 1.20 ≈ 40% of 3). This sequence is easy to recall because it counts down by roughly halving. Pair each number with its minimum least dimension: **2.00 m / 1.50 m / 900 mm.**

Combined recall chain: **"6 by 2, 3 by 1.5, 1.2 by 0.9"** — habitable, kitchen, toilet. Once you drill this sequence, no board question on room minimums will catch you off guard.

### Mnemonic 2: "CLOCK" — What a Group A Dwelling Must Have

> **C** — **Coverage** limited (90% corner / 80% inside lot)
> **L** — **Light and ventilation** per Sec. 805-811
> **O** — **One sanitary toilet** minimum (plus washing + drainage)
> **C** — **Ceiling heights** per Sec. 805 (2.70 / 2.40 / 2.10)
> **K** — **Kind of ground** must be firm; foundations must support loads

"CLOCK" captures the five substantive requirements of Sec. 708: coverage, light/ventilation, one toilet, ceiling heights, and kind-of-ground (foundation on firm ground). A dwelling that passes the CLOCK checklist satisfies the core structure of Sec. 708.

## Exam Trap Learts

**TRAP 1:** "A habitable room that is 3.0 m × 2.0 m = 6.0 sq m satisfies the minimum requirements for a Group A dwelling bedroom."

**TRUTH:** The area check (6.00 sq m) is satisfied, but you must also verify the least-dimension check. A 3.0 m × 2.0 m room has a least dimension of **2.00 m** — which exactly meets the 2.00 m minimum. So this room actually **does** comply, but only barely. The trap version of this question often presents a 4.0 m × 1.5 m room (6.00 sq m area, but least dimension 1.5 m < 2.00 m required) — that room fails. Both conditions must be met: **area AND least dimension.** Never apply just one criterion.

---

**TRAP 2:** "The minimum ceiling height for a Group A dwelling bedroom is 2.40 m, as stated in Section 805."

**TRUTH:** The 2.40 m minimum applies specifically to the **second storey** of a multi-storey building with **artificial ventilation** (mechanical/air conditioning). For a naturally ventilated room — the default in a simple Group A dwelling — the minimum ceiling height is **2.70 m** per Sec. 805. Board exams frequently offer 2.40 m as an answer option knowing that candidates confuse it with the general minimum. The 2.40 m figure is correct only in a specific context (second storey, artificial ventilation). For a typical naturally ventilated house, 2.70 m is the answer.

---

**TRAP 3:** "A Group A dwelling on a 100 sq m inside lot can have a building footprint of 80 sq m (80% lot coverage) and be placed anywhere on the lot."

**TRUTH:** The 80% maximum lot coverage means the footprint cannot exceed 80 sq m. However, Sec. 708(a) additionally requires the dwelling to be **at least 2 meters from the property line** (subject to Civil Code easements of light and view). On a 100 sq m inside lot (e.g., 8 m wide × 12.5 m deep), the 2-meter setback on all sides reduces the buildable footprint to (8 − 4) × (12.5 − 4) = 4 m × 8.5 m = **34 sq m** — far less than the 80 sq m coverage maximum. The two requirements (coverage limit and setback minimum) are independent constraints; the more restrictive one governs. Exam questions that present only the coverage percentage and ask for the maximum footprint are trapping candidates who ignore the setback.

## Active Recall Questions

**Question 1**

A licensed architect is designing a single-family dwelling on a 120 sq m inside lot in a suburban barangay. The client wants to maximize the building footprint. Per PD 1096, Rule VII, Sec. 708(a), what is the maximum lot coverage allowed for this dwelling?

- A. 72 sq m (60% of 120 sq m)
- B. 84 sq m (70% of 120 sq m)
- C. 96 sq m (80% of 120 sq m)
- D. 108 sq m (90% of 120 sq m)

> **Answer: C — 96 sq m (80% of 120 sq m)**
>
<Note>

Per PD 1096, Rule VII, Sec. 708(a), a Group A dwelling on an inside lot shall occupy not more than 80% of the lot area. 120 sq m × 80% = 96 sq m. The 90% coverage (Option D) applies to corner lots, not inside lots. Option A (60%) and Option B (70%) have no basis in Sec. 708. The candidate must distinguish between corner lot (90%) and inside lot (80%) — this distinction is the primary exam test point in this provision. Note also that the 2-meter setback requirement may further reduce the achievable footprint below 96 sq m depending on lot dimensions.

</Note>

Per PD 1096, which of the following correctly describes the minimum dimensions for a kitchen in a Group A dwelling?

- A. Minimum 6.00 sq m with a least dimension of 2.00 m
- B. Minimum 3.00 sq m with a least dimension of 1.50 m

**Question 3**

A developer is constructing a two-storey Group A dwelling with mechanical air conditioning throughout. Per PD 1096, Rule VIII, Sec. 805, what are the minimum ceiling heights required for the first and second storeys, respectively?

- A. First storey: 2.40 m; Second storey: 2.10 m
- B. First storey: 2.70 m; Second storey: 2.10 m
- C. First storey: 2.70 m; Second storey: 2.40 m
- D. First storey: 2.40 m; Second storey: 2.40 m

> **Answer: C — First storey: 2.70 m; Second storey: 2.40 m**
>
<LawCallout code="pd1096" section="Rule VII - Ceiling Heights">


Per PD 1096, Rule VIII, Sec. 805, for buildings with artificial ventilation: first storey minimum = 2.70 m; second storey minimum = 2.40 m; succeeding storeys = minimum 2.10 m unobstructed headroom clearance. This is one of the most exam-tested provisions in Rule VIII. Option A is wrong on both storeys (first storey must be 2.70 m, not 2.40 m). Option B has the first storey correct but applies the third-storey clearance (2.10 m) to the second. Option D applies 2.40 m to the first storey, which is incorrect — first storey must be 2.70 m regardless of ventilation type. The first storey standard (2.70 m) applies equally to naturally and artificially ventilated buildings; only the second storey gets the reduced 2.40 m allowance.
