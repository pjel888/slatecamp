## What You Need to Know for the LEA

The three classical urban models — Concentric Zone, Sector, and Multiple Nuclei — explain how cities grow and organize spatially. Each model identifies the theorist, the year, and the underlying mechanism. These are the most-tested models in the LEA and the most commonly swapped by mistake.

## Learning Goal

Distinguish the Concentric Zone (Burgess, 1925), Sector (Hoyt, 1939), and Multiple Nuclei (Harris & Ullman, 1945) models by theorist, year, spatial mechanism, and Philippine application.

## Core Concept

**Urban structure models** are theoretical frameworks that explain how cities develop, grow, and organize land use over time. The three foundational classical models emerged in the early 20th century and remain the basis for understanding city form.

The **Concentric Zone Model** (**Ernest W. Burgess, 1925**) proposes that cities grow outward in concentric rings from a central business district (CBD). Based on Chicago's development, Burgess argued that cities expand through ecological succession—like ripples from a stone dropped in water. Each ring represents a distinct land-use zone and income level, with lower-income residential areas farther from the CBD. The CBD is the commercial core; moving outward, you find light manufacturing, working-class residential, middle-class residential, and finally commuter suburbs.

The **Sector Model** or **Wedge Model** (**Homer Hoyt, 1939**) challenges the concentric rings concept by proposing that growth follows transportation corridors as pie-shaped sectors radiating from the CBD. Rather than uniform rings, high-income neighborhoods cluster along premium transportation routes (streetcar lines, highways), and once a sector is established as high-rent, it tends to remain so. Lower-income and industrial uses fill remaining sectors. Hoyt's model explains why cities look more like a pie chart than a bullseye.

The **Multiple Nuclei Model** (**Chauncy Harris AND Edward Ullman, 1945**) rejects the single-CBD assumption entirely. Real cities, they argue, have multiple centers or nuclei, each specialized for different functions. A retail nucleus, a governmental nucleus, a port nucleus, and residential clusters develop independently based on locational requirements—some activities need agglomeration (like finance), others need waterfront access (like shipping), others need cheap land (like warehousing). The city becomes polycentric, a collection of clusters rather than rings or wedges.

**Supporting models** refine and extend this thinking:

- **Central Place Theory** (**Walter Christaller, 1933**) describes a hierarchy of urban centers based on range (the distance people travel) and threshold (the minimum market size needed). Smaller towns provide basic goods; larger cities provide specialized services. Trade areas form hexagonal patterns—a foundational framework for understanding regional systems.

- **Von Thünen Rings** (**Johann Heinrich von Thünen, 1826**) precede Burgess and model agricultural land use, showing how intensity decreases with distance from a market town. Concentric rings of different crops reflect transport costs. This model profoundly influenced Burgess's thinking about Chicago.

- **Rank-Size Rule** (**George Kingsley Zipf, 1949**) predicts that in a balanced urban system, the second-largest city should be 1/2 the population of the largest, the third city 1/3, and so on. The Philippines dramatically violates this rule—Metro Manila is roughly 10 times larger than Cebu City, creating a primate city pattern.

- **Primate City** (**Mark Jefferson, 1939**) describes a city that is _disproportionately_ large and dominates the urban system far beyond statistical prediction. Manila is the classic Philippine example—it concentrates national commerce, government, education, and culture in a way that violates the rank-size expectation. Primacy is not just being the largest; it is being dramatically oversized.

## Philippine Context

Metro Manila exemplifies all three models simultaneously. The CBD (Makati-Taguig) generates concentric rings of middle-income to commuter zones. Premium residential clusters follow transport corridors (Sector Model). And the metropolis contains multiple specialized nuclei—government (Quezon City), finance (Makati), heritage (Intramuros), entertainment (Ortigas), and light manufacturing zones. Understanding these models helps planners diagnose why development follows certain patterns and how to guide future growth.

## How It's Tested

1. **Attribution questions:** "Which theorist proposed the Concentric Zone Model?" Direct recall—must answer Burgess (1925), not Hoyt.

2. **Mechanism questions:** "Cities following the Sector Model grow as [pie-shaped sectors / concentric rings / multiple centers]?" Requires understanding the spatial mechanism.

3. **Application questions:** "The Multiple Nuclei Model best explains [Metro Manila's mix of CBD, port district, and government center / Quezon City's radial growth / suburban sprawl]?" Must match the mechanism to the phenomenon.
