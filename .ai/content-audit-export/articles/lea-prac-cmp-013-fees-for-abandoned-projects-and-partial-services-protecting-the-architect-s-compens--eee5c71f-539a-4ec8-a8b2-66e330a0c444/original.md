## Fees for Abandoned Projects and Partial Services — Protecting the Architect's Compensation


## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- Describe the architect's right to compensation for abandoned projects.
- Explain how partial services are compensated when a project is discontinued.
- Identify the rules protecting the architect's compensation.
- Apply abandonment compensation rules to exam scenarios.

### Definition of Abandoned Projects

An abandoned project is one where the client discontinues the project before completion. The client may abandon during Schematic Design before committing to design direction; during Design Development before finalizing the scope and cost estimate; after Contract Documents are prepared but before construction begins; or during construction. The point of abandonment determines what design work has been completed and what compensation the architect has earned.

### Compensation for Work Completed at Abandonment

The architect's fee is typically structured as milestone-based payments tied to phase completion. If the client abandons during Schematic Design, the architect receives 15% of total fee (the Schematic Design percentage) for work completed. If abandonment occurs after Design Development, the architect receives 15% + 20% = 35% of total fee. If abandonment occurs after Contract Documents, the architect receives 90% of total fee per SPP 202 Section 7.7.

The key principle is that the architect receives compensation for completed design phases, not for projected future phases. The contract must clearly establish this milestone-based compensation structure so both parties understand what percentage of the total fee the architect earns if the project is abandoned at different points.

### Reimbursable Expenses at Abandonment

When projects are abandoned, the architect has typically incurred reimbursable expenses (consultant fees, travel costs, permit application fees) before learning the project will not proceed. These expenses are legitimate project costs that the architect advanced on the client's behalf; the client should reimburse actual expenses regardless of project abandonment.

The contract should clearly establish that all reimbursable expenses become due and payable upon project abandonment. A client may argue "the project failed, so we shouldn't pay for consultants who didn't produce final deliverables," but this contradicts the principle that reimbursable expenses are actual costs incurred. If the client authorizes a geotechnical survey costing 50,000 pesos, and the project is abandoned before that survey is used, the client nonetheless incurred that cost and should reimburse the architect for advancing the payment.

### Change Order Procedures at Abandonment

The contract should specify what happens to any authorized change orders at the time of abandonment. If the client had requested and authorized additional scope (adding an adjacent building, expanding the original design), and the architect was designing these additions when the client decided to abandon, the architect should be compensated for change order work completed to the point of abandonment, following the same milestone-based percentage applied to the original scope.

### Partial Services — Design Development Only

Some clients contract for architectural services limited to a specific phase. A client might retain an architect for Schematic Design and Design Development only, planning to find another architect for Contract Documents and Construction Administration. This is legitimate partial services, not abandonment; the architect should clearly understand the engagement scope and be compensated only for services provided.

If the contract specifies "Design Development services only," the architect provides Schematic Design and Design Development (15% + 20% = 35% of a full SPP 202 design project) and receives that 35% fee. The remaining 65% (Contract Documents, Bidding, Construction Administration) is not the architect's responsibility or compensation.

Partial services engagements must be clearly documented in the contract. The engagement letter should specify: (1) what phases are included and what phases are excluded, (2) what deliverables the architect will provide at each phase, (3) what coordination with future architects or consultants is the current architect's responsibility, and (4) what documentation will be transferred to the next architect.

### Handoff Documentation for Partial Services

When an architect provides partial services and another architect will continue, the first architect should provide comprehensive documentation to facilitate handoff. If the first architect completes Design Development and a second architect will prepare Contract Documents, the first architect should provide: complete Design Development drawings and specifications, consultant coordination documentation, site investigation reports, cost estimates, program documentation, and any design notes or rationale that explain design decisions.

This handoff documentation allows the second architect to proceed efficiently without redesigning work the first architect completed. The contract should address whether the first architect provides this documentation as part of their contracted scope (typically yes) or whether the client must pay additional fees for extensive documentation preparation (if documentation beyond standard deliverables is required).

### Preventing Abandonment Disputes

To protect the architect's interests at abandonment, the contract should:

1. Clearly establish milestone-based compensation percentages per SPP 202, with 90% of fee due if Owner fails to implement plans (Section 7.7).

2. Specify that the architect receives payment for completed phases upon abandonment, not just upon final project completion.

3. Establish that reimbursable expenses are due and payable upon abandonment regardless of project status.

4. Define abandonment circumstances (client decision, financing failure, force majeure) and establish compensation for each scenario.

5. Specify what documentation the architect provides at abandonment (partial deliverables, notes, design rationale) to facilitate potential future continuation.

6. Establish payment timing: typically, the architect invoices for completed work within a specified period after abandonment notification, and the client pays within the contract's standard payment terms.

### Partial Services — Design Only, No Construction Administration

Some architectural engagements provide design services (Schematic through Contract Documents) but exclude Construction Administration. This is common when: the client expects to manage construction themselves, a different architect will provide Construction Administration, or the client plans to use a design-build contractor who will not require ongoing architect involvement.

When Construction Administration is excluded, the contract should specify that the architect provides services through Contract Documents and receives 90% of SPP 202 fee per Section 7.7 if no construction implementation. The remaining 10% (Construction Administration) is not part of the engagement.

The contract should clarify the architect's responsibilities at completion of Contract Documents: does the architect respond to RFI (requests for information) during bidding, or does the client manage this? Does the architect provide as-built drawing updates if the contractor makes changes during construction, or is this outside the engagement scope? Clear specification prevents disputes about what post-Contract Documents services the architect provides.

### Force Majeure and Project Suspension

Projects may be suspended (not abandoned, but temporarily halted) due to force majeure (natural disasters, pandemics, wars) or financing delays. The contract should address suspension versus abandonment: if a project is suspended for an extended period, does the architect continue to be paid retainer fees to maintain availability, or does the engagement pause? If suspension extends indefinitely, at what point does it become abandonment requiring architect compensation for work completed?

Clear contract language distinguishing suspension (temporary halt with expected resumption) from abandonment (permanent discontinuation) protects both parties and establishes compensation expectations.

### Client Communication and Expectation Setting

During proposal and contract negotiation, the architect should discuss and document abandonment and partial services provisions. Many clients don't anticipate project abandonment and may not realize the architect earned fees for completed phases even if the project doesn't proceed to completion. Transparency about what happens at abandonment, what the architect is compensated for, and what documentation is provided prevents disputes if abandonment occurs.
