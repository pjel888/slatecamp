## 2. Core Concepts with Mnemonic

### The Governing Provision

**PD 1096, Chapter 17 (Basic Law, 1977):**
Establishes the fundamental requirements for sheet metal paint spray booths covering construction, fire protection, lighting, and ventilation. The basic law sets the performance intent; it delegates specific dimensional and material details to the Secretary of Public Works.

**2004 Revised IRR, Rule XVII, Sections 1701–1704:**
Operationalizes the basic law with exact dimensions, materials, and clearances. The IRR adds the critical expansion that booth separation from other operations may be increased beyond 91 cm at the discretion of the **Local Fire Service Marshall** — a provision not explicitly stated in the 1977 basic law text. All numerical requirements cited below are from the 2004 Revised IRR unless otherwise noted.

---

### The Five Key Principles of Rule XVII

**Principle 1 — Steel Containment (Section 1701)**
The spray booth is a steel box designed to contain flammable vapors and combustible overspray. The minimum steel gauge is **No. 18 U.S. gauge** — heavier than ordinary sheet metal roofing. The booth area is doubly limited: it shall not exceed **150 sq.m** AND shall not exceed **10% of the basic area permitted for the major use of the building** under its Occupancy Group. Both caps apply simultaneously; whichever is smaller governs.

**Principle 2 — Floor and Interior Finish (Section 1701)**
If the floor within or beneath the booth is combustible, it must be covered with a **non-combustible, non-sparking material** that facilitates safe cleaning and residue removal. Interior surfaces must be **smooth and continuous without outstanding edges** to prevent paint residue buildup in crevices that would create ignition reservoirs.

**Principle 3 — Fire Barrier and Separation (Section 1702)**
Two distinct requirements apply simultaneously: (a) a **fire curtain or metal deflector** at least **100 mm deep** is required at the upper outer edge of any open booth front if the opening exceeds **1.00 sq.m** and the booth has no doors; and (b) every booth must be separated from other operations by at least **91 centimeters**. These are independent requirements — the curtain does not substitute for the clearance distance.

**Principle 4 — Isolated Illumination (Section 1703)**
Lighting is isolated from the spray zone by **hammered wire or heat-treated glass panels**. The panel must (a) be non-combustible or protected against breakage, (b) effectively isolate the spraying area from the lighting area, and (c) be positioned to minimize paint spray deposit on the panel surface. Normal paint accumulation on the panel must not reach dangerous temperatures through radiation or conduction from the light source.

**Principle 5 — Forced Mechanical Exhaust (Section 1704)**
Ventilation is non-optional and non-passive. Air must move through the spray area at **≥30 lineal meters per minute**, directed by mechanical means to the exterior. The exhaust system has strict material and placement rules for blades, motor, and duct discharge to eliminate ignition sources within the vapor-rich airstream.

## 4. In-Depth Technical Analysis

### Section 1701 — Sheet Metal Paint Spray Booths: Construction

#### Steel Shell
The **No. 18 U.S. gauge** steel requirement (approximately 1.27 mm nominal thickness) represents the lightest sheet metal that can resist deformation from an internal pressure wave in a minor deflagration event. Thinner gauges — No. 22 or No. 24 commonly used in HVAC ductwork — would perforate or rupture under the same conditions, allowing flammable vapor and burning residue to escape the containment. The code specifies U.S. gauge specifically, consistent with the Philippine practice of referencing ASTM standards for steel products.

#### Area Limitations — The Double Cap
The 150 sq.m hard cap and the 10% of basic permitted area requirement work together. Consider a Group G factory building on a lot where the basic permitted floor area is 800 sq.m. Ten percent of 800 sq.m is 80 sq.m — which controls over the 150 sq.m cap. The spray booth in that building cannot exceed 80 sq.m regardless of how large it is designed. The rationale: a spray booth is a high-hazard accessory use; it must remain subordinate in area to the building's primary function. Allowing a spray booth to consume half a building's floor area would effectively reclassify the entire building as hazardous.

The 10% cap echoes the logic used in Rule VII for accessory occupancies — hazardous uses are tolerated only when they remain incidental to the primary occupancy. This connection to Rule VII, Section 701, is a common board exam cross-reference.

#### Floor Covering
The requirement for a non-combustible, non-sparking floor covering addresses two simultaneous hazards: (1) overspray residue that settles on the floor over time and (2) foot traffic, rolling equipment, and dropped tools that can create sparks. Materials that satisfy both requirements include concrete (bare or with epoxy non-sparking coating), quarry tile set in non-combustible mortar, and non-sparking safety grating. Bare wood plank flooring fails on both counts — it is combustible and can cause sparks from metal tool drops.

#### Interior Surface Geometry
The requirement for **smooth and continuous surfaces without outstanding edges** is a hygiene-and-safety provision. Paint spray residue accumulates in corners, on ledges, in crevices, and behind structural members. Once dry, accumulated residue is highly combustible. A booth with exposed fasteners, angle iron stiffeners, or internal ductwork ledges becomes increasingly dangerous as residue builds up. Smooth-walled booths are also easier to clean manually or with solvents.

---

### Section 1702 — Fire Protection

#### Fire Curtain / Deflector
The fire curtain or metal deflector is required when **both conditions are met simultaneously**:
1. The open front elevation exceeds **1.00 sq.m**, AND
2. The booth is **not equipped with doors**.

If either condition is absent — if the booth has doors, or if the opening is 1.00 sq.m or less — the curtain is not required. The curtain must hang from the **upper outer edge** of the booth opening and must extend at least **100 mm deep** (downward). Its function is to deflect a fireball or pressure wave upward and outward rather than forward into the work area, and to help contain overspray drift.

The comparison with US practice is instructive: NFPA 33 triggers the fire curtain requirement at openings exceeding **9 sq.ft (approximately 0.84 sq.m)**, while California Title 8 Section 5446 uses the same 9 sq.ft threshold. The Philippine code threshold of 1.00 sq.m (10.76 sq.ft) is slightly higher — meaning small booths in the Philippines may not require a curtain even though they would under California OSHA rules.

#### Separation Distance
The minimum **91 cm** separation between spray booths and adjacent operations must be maintained as a clear, unobstructed zone. The 2004 Revised IRR explicitly expands this requirement: the Local Fire Service Marshall may require **greater distance, or a partition/wall**, based on site-specific hazard assessment. This is a significant expansion over the basic law, which states only the 91 cm minimum. In practice, the LFSM has authority to require full fire-rated separation walls if the adjacent operations present elevated ignition risk.

The 91 cm figure (approximately 910 mm) aligns remarkably closely with the OSHA 29 CFR 1910.107 requirement of **3 feet (914 mm)** — a difference of only 4 mm, almost certainly reflecting the metric conversion of the same source standard.

---

### Section 1703 — Light

#### Why Isolated Lighting Is Critical
Spray finishing operations generate flammable vapor-air mixtures that can reach explosive concentrations within seconds of operation. Standard electrical fixtures are ignition sources: lamp heat can ignite paint deposits on the fixture surface, and electrical arcing (even in sealed fixtures not rated for explosive atmospheres) can ignite the ambient vapor. The Philippine code's requirement for glass panel isolation creates a physical air gap between the ignition-capable fixture and the flammable atmosphere of the spray zone.

#### Glazing Material Selection
**Hammered wire glass** (also called wired glass) is traditional: wire mesh embedded in the glass prevents the panel from shattering and falling into the spray zone when heated. **Heat-treated (tempered) glass** is the modern alternative: it is four to five times stronger than annelead glass and, when it does break, shatters into small blunt pieces rather than sharp shards. Both materials satisfy the code's requirement for panels that are either non-combustible or protected against breakage.

The code's requirement that normal residue accumulation "shall NOT be raised to dangerous temperature by radiation or conduction from the light source" establishes a performance requirement that effectively limits the wattage and proximity of lighting fixtures. High-intensity discharge lamps or bare incandescent lamps immediately behind a paint-encrusted panel can create a thermal ignition risk even without electrical arcing. In Philippine practice, fluorescent or LED fixtures with proper thermal ratings are the preferred compliant solution.

---

### Section 1704 — Ventilation

#### The 30 m/min Standard
The **30 lineal meters per minute** (approximately 0.5 meters per second) air velocity requirement applies to any cross-section through the spray area — not just at the exhaust grille. This means the booth must be sized and the exhaust system engineered so that the minimum velocity is maintained across the **largest cross-sectional area** the air must traverse. A booth that is wide at the opening but narrows to a small exhaust plenum can easily create stagnant zones where flammable vapor accumulates. The designer must calculate air velocity at the most unfavorable cross-section.

The NFPA 33 requirement of 100 fpm (feet per minute) converts to approximately **30.5 m/min** — essentially identical to the PH standard and confirming a common technical basis.

#### Non-Ferrous Fan Blades
Ferrous (iron or steel) fan blades can generate sparks when they contact the duct wall, a foreign object, or each other during mechanical failure. In a vapor-laden airstream, a single spark is sufficient to initiate ignition. Non-ferrous materials — aluminum, bronze, stainless steel (in some interpretations), or fiber-reinforced polymer — do not produce incendiary sparks on contact. This is one of the most frequently tested specifics on the board exam: the material is **non-ferrous**, not simply "metal" or "stainless."

#### Motor Outside Booth and Duct
Placing the motor outside the spray booth and its duct system is the single most effective safeguard against motor-ignited fires. Electric motors produce heat, can arc at brushes and commutators, and produce electromagnetic fields — all potential ignition sources in flammable atmospheres. The code is explicit: the motor is prohibited from being located inside the **booth** AND inside the **duct system**. Belt drives, which transmit power from the external motor to the internal fan, must be **enclosed where they pass through the booth or duct wall** to prevent belt-generated static electricity and friction sparks from entering the hazardous atmosphere.

#### Exhaust Duct Discharge Clearances
The two clearance requirements for the exhaust duct discharge point address different risks:

- **≥2.00 m from combustible construction:** Exhaust gases contain flammable vapor and aerosolized paint particles. At the discharge point, these are diluted but still potentially above the lower explosive limit. Maintaining 2.00 m (approximately 6.56 ft) of clearance ensures the gases disperse below ignition concentration before reaching combustibles such as wood siding, roofing boards, or stored materials.

- **≥8.00 m from exterior wall openings:** Windows, louvers, doorways, and ventilation openings in adjacent walls can allow exhaust gases to re-enter the building and accumulate in non-spray areas where unrated electrical fixtures, HVAC equipment, or open flames may be present. The 8.00 m buffer is substantially larger than most buildings' footprints; on dense urban sites, this requirement drives booth placement to building perimeters or rooftop exhausts.

#### Waterwash Booth Exception
Waterwash spray booths — where exhaust air is scrubbed by a water curtain before discharge — are explicitly exempted from the discharge location regulations of Section 1704. The water scrubbing process removes flammable paint particles and partially absorbs solvent vapors, producing exhaust that is non-flammable under normal operating conditions. This exception reflects the established principle in both Philippine and US codes (NFPA 33 contains a comparable exception) that engineering controls can justify relaxed distance requirements.

#### Exhaust Duct Material
Section 1704 requires exhaust ducts to be constructed of **steel**, with thickness in accordance with the Secretary's specifications. Steel is specified — not galvanized sheet metal, not aluminum flex duct — because (a) steel resists the chemical attack of solvent vapors and paint residue better than coated metals whose galvanizing can be stripped by aggressive solvents, (b) steel maintains structural integrity in an internal fire event long enough for heat to dissipate and for suppression to act, and (c) steel does not propagate flame. In practice, the specific gauge for exhaust ducts is referenced through SMACNA (Sheet Metal and Air Conditioning Contractors' National Association) duct construction standards, which are the most widely adopted specifications in Philippine mechanical engineering practice.

The requirement that ducts be cleaned periodically — implied by the smooth interior surface requirement of Section 1701 for the booth itself — is operationalized through RA 9514 fire safety inspection requirements, which check for paint residue accumulation in exhaust ducts as a fire hazard.

## 5. PH vs. USA Comparative Analysis

### Where Philippine and US Standards Converge

The convergence of the Philippine and US spray booth standards is striking. The three most critical technical requirements — steel gauge (No. 18), ventilation rate (≈30 m/min / 100 fpm), and non-ferrous fan blades — are essentially identical. The motor-outside-booth requirement is identical. This alignment reflects the fact that PD 1096 and its IRR drew heavily from US model codes (particularly BOCA, UBC, and NFPA standards) when establishing Philippine building requirements. The spray booth provisions are among the most technically harmonized sections of PD 1096.

The 91 cm / 914 mm separation distance is so close as to be practically identical — a rounding artifact of metric conversion from the 3-foot OSHA standard.

### Where Philippine Code Is More Restrictive

**Area cap:** The PH code's absolute 150 sq.m cap and 10% of basic area rule is more prescriptive than NFPA 33's approach. NFPA 33 limits booth size through construction type, sprinkler status, and fire separation — a performance-based approach that can permit larger booths when fire suppression is provided. The Philippine code imposes a hard cap regardless of suppression.

**Duct discharge from combustible construction:** The PH requirement of ≥2.00 m (approximately 6.56 ft) exceeds the NFPA 33 minimum of ≥6 ft (1.83 m) by approximately 170 mm. This makes the Philippine standard slightly more restrictive for this specific clearance.

### Where US Standards Are More Restrictive

**Fire curtain trigger:** NFPA 33 and California Title 8 require fire curtains for openings exceeding **0.84 sq.m (9 sq.ft)**. The Philippine code triggers the requirement at **1.00 sq.m** — meaning a booth with an 0.90 sq.m opening requires a curtain in the US but not in the Philippines.

**Automatic fire suppression:** NFPA 33 requires automatic fire protection systems for spray booths with open areas exceeding 9 sq.ft — typically wet-pipe sprinkler or dry chemical systems. PD 1096, Rule XVII does not mandate automatic suppression within Sections 1701–1704, though Rule XII, Section 1212 (Fire-Extinguishing Systems) may apply based on building occupancy and size. This is a notable gap in PH prescriptive requirements relative to US practice.

**IBC Occupancy Classification:** The IBC classifies spray operations under Group H-2 or H-3 — the High-Hazard occupancy group — which imposes construction type limitations, required fire separations, and site placement restrictions more stringent than those applied to PH Group G occupancies. Philippine Group G, while classified as hazardous, does not carry the full suite of IBC H-Occupancy constraints.

### Design Implications for Philippine Projects

On Philippine projects, the architect and fire safety engineer must navigate Rule XVII alongside RA 9514 (Revised Fire Code of the Philippines) and the Philippine Mechanical Engineering Code (for exhaust system sizing). The Local Fire Service Marshall's authority to impose additional separation requirements (per Section 1702) means the code minimum of 91 cm is a floor, not a ceiling. In densely occupied industrial estates — common in Metro Manila, Cebu, and Davao — the LFSM may effectively require fire-rated partitions between spray booths and adjacent manufacturing lines.

### Tropical Climate Considerations

The Philippine tropical climate introduces specific design considerations not addressed in US model codes. High ambient humidity — common in coastal and rainy-season conditions — can affect spray equipment performance, solvent evaporation rates, and the flammability characteristics of paint-air mixtures within the booth. Spray booths in high-humidity environments may require dehumidification of supply air to maintain consistent spray quality and predictable flammable vapor concentrations. The ventilation standard of 30 m/min remains constant regardless of season; the mechanical engineer must size for worst-case conditions. Additionally, the prevleance of typhoon conditions in the Philippines means exhaust discharge points must be designed to prevent rain ingress and wind-driven reversal of the exhaust airstream — conditions that could re-introduce flammable vapors into the booth.

## 6. Exam Traps

### Trap Leart 1 — "The separation distance replaces the fire curtain"

**Wrong answer:** A candidate selects that a 1.5 sq.m open-front booth with 91 cm clearance to adjacent operations is compliant — reasoning that the separation distance provides fire protection equivalent to the curtain.

**Why it's wrong:** Section 1702 imposes **two independent requirements**. The 91 cm separation distance applies to ALL booths regardless of opening size. The fire curtain requirement applies to ANY open-front booth with an opening >1.00 sq.m. Meeting one does not satisfy or substitute for the other. The booth in this question needs both the 91 cm clearance AND a fire curtain/deflector ≥100 mm deep at the upper outer edge of the opening.

**Correct answer:** The booth requires both 91 cm separation AND a fire curtain/deflector of at least 100 mm depth per Rule XVII, Section 1702.

---

### Trap Leart 2 — "Non-ferrous blades OR motor inside the duct is acceptable"

**Wrong answer:** A candidate identifies only the non-ferrous blade requirement and misses that the motor is located inside the duct plenum — concluding the system is compliant because the blades are aluminum.

**Why it's wrong:** Section 1704 requires that (a) fan blades are non-ferrous AND (b) the motor is NOT in the spray booth OR the duct system. Both are mandatory. A non-ferrous blade paired with a motor inside the duct still violates the code, because the motor is an ignition source within the flammable exhaust stream regardless of blade material.

**Correct answer:** The motor must be located entirely outside both the spray booth and the duct system. Belts, where used to transmit power from the exterior motor, must be enclosed where they enter the booth or duct per Rule XVII, Section 1704.

---

### Trap Leart 3 — Confusing the Two Discharge Clearances

**Wrong answer:** A candidate applies the 2.00 m clearance to exterior wall openings, concluding a discharge point 3.00 m from a neighbor's window complies.

**Why it's wrong:** Section 1704 prescribes **two distinct clearances** at the discharge point: ≥2.00 m from **combustible construction** (e.g., wood walls, combustible cladding) and ≥8.00 m from **adjoining exterior wall openings** (windows, doors, louvers). These are different requirements for different conditions. A discharge point 3.00 m from a window fails the 8.00 m window/opening clearance — even if 2.00 m from the nearest wood wall is satisfied.

**Correct answer:** The exhaust duct discharge point must maintain **both** clearances simultaneously: ≥2.00 m from any combustible construction, AND ≥8.00 m from any adjoining exterior wall openings (Rule XVII, Section 1704). Exception: waterwash spray booths are not subject to these discharge location requirements.

## 7. Board-Prep Mnemonics

### Mnemonic 1: **"2 and 8 from the Gate"** — Exhaust Discharge Clearances

> **2** meters from combustible construction.
> **8** meters from exterior wall openings.
> Both from the exhaust **gate** (discharge point).

The number sequence — **2 and 8** — doubles from one requirement to the next. If you remember "2 and 8," you will never mix up the two discharge clearances. Add the qualifier: **"from the gate"** to remind you this applies to the discharge point (the end of the exhaust duct), not to the booth itself.

## 8. Active Recall Questions

### Question 1

A spray booth has an open front elevation of 1.10 sq.m and no doors. The booth is separated from adjacent painting operations by 95 cm. Under Rule XVII of PD 1096 (2004 Revised IRR), which statement is correct regarding fire protection compliance?

**A.** The booth is compliant — the 95 cm separation exceeds the 91 cm minimum, satisfying all fire protection requirements.
**B.** The booth requires a fire curtain or metal deflector at least 100 mm deep at the upper outer edge of the opening, in addition to maintaining the separation distance.
**C.** The booth requires a fire curtain only if it is not equipped with a fire-extinguishing system.
**D.** The 1.10 sq.m opening is below the threshold — only booths with openings exceeding 1.50 sq.m require a fire curtain.

> **Correct Answer: B**
> Section 1702 imposes two independent requirements. The fire curtain/deflector is triggered when the open front elevation exceeds **1.00 sq.m** AND no doors are present — both conditions are met here (1.10 sq.m, no doors). The deflector must be ≥100 mm deep at the upper outer edge. The 95 cm separation independently satisfies the ≥91 cm requirement, but it does not substitute for the curtain. Option D states an incorrect threshold (1.50 sq.m); the actual threshold is 1.00 sq.m.
> **[LEA Area 2 — Utilities & Fire Protection | Rule XVII Sec. 1702]**

The exhaust system of a conventional (non-waterwash) paint spray booth discharges at a point 1.90 m from the nearest wood-framed wall of an adjacent structure and 5.00 m from the nearest window of that structure. Which Philippine code requirement is violated?

**A.** The distance from combustible construction only — must be ≥2.00 m.
**B.** The distance from exterior wall openings only — must be ≥8.00 m.
**C.** Both the distance from combustible construction AND from exterior wall openings.

### Question 3

Under PH Rule XVII (Sec. 1701) and US NFPA 33 (Chapter 7), which of the following statements CORRECTLY compares the spray booth requirements of both jurisdictions?

**A.** NFPA 33 requires a minimum of No. 16 U.S. gauge steel, while PH requires No. 18 U.S. gauge — making the US standard more restrictive.
**B.** The Philippines imposes a hard 150 sq.m area cap on spray booths, while NFPA 33 uses a performance-based approach that can permit larger booths when automatic fire suppression is provided.
**C.** The Philippine ventilation standard of 30 m/min is significantly more stringent than NFPA 33's 50 fpm requirement.
**D.** NFPA 33 prohibits motors inside the spray booth but permits them inside the exhaust duct, unlike the Philippine code which prohibits both.

> **Correct Answer: B**

## 9. Cross-Referencing Section

### Rule VII, Section 701 — Occupancy Classification

The connection between spray booth requirements and occupancy classification is fundamental. Section 701 classifies "paint shops and spray painting rooms" under **Group G, Division 2** — Moderate Hazardous. Division 1 (Highly Hazardous) may apply when flammable finishes are used in quantities exceeding the threshold for "moderate" hazard. The architect must determine the correct Division before applying Group G area and construction type standards: a Group G-2 spray room in a larger Group E (Business) or Group F (Industrial) building will have different setback and fire-resistance requirements than a standalone Group G-1 facility.

### Rule IV — Types of Construction

The steel shell requirement (No. 18 gauge) in Section 1701 aligns with Rule IV's classification of Types II–V construction as requiring non-combustible primary structural elements. A spray booth is effectively a Type II or Type III accessory structure — its steel construction must be continuous and unbroken. Rule IV establishes that the construction type of the **building** (not just the booth) determines the fire-resistance ratings of the walls enclosing the booth within the larger structure.

### Rule V — Fire Zones

Group G occupancies are restricted to Fire Zone 2 and 3 under Rule V. Spray painting operations in dense urban cores (often within Fire Zone 1 commercial districts) may require conditional permits or alternative compliance paths. The Local Fire Service Marshall's authority under Section 1702 to impose additional separation requirements reflects this fire zone sensitivity.

### Rule VI — Fire-Resistive Construction

The walls surrounding a spray booth area (not the booth itself) must achieve fire-resistance ratings appropriate to the building type and occupancy separation requirements under Rule VI. While Section 1701 specifies only the booth's own steel walls, the enclosing structural fire compartment must meet Rule VI requirements. A Group G spray room within a Group F building must be separated by fire-rated construction per Rule VII, Section 701, and Rule VI.

### Rule XII, Section 1212 — Fire-Extinguishing Systems

The building-level automatic fire suppression requirement under Section 1212 may independently apply to buildings containing spray booths based on occupancy group and floor area. While Rule XVII itself does not mandate automatic suppression within the booth, a Group G building of sufficient size will require a fire suppression system under Rule XII. Per US practice (NFPA 33), automatic suppression is standard within spray booths — this represents a gap in Philippine prescriptive requirements that the LFSM may address through administrative orders.

### RA 9514 — Revised Fire Code of the Philippines

The Fire Code of the Philippines (RA 9514, 2008, and its 2019 IRR) governs fire prevention in industrial occupancies including spray painting operations. Key RA 9514 requirements relevant to spray booths include: (a) fire safety inspection certificates for industrial facilities, (b) fire safety plan requirements, (c) storage of flammable liquids, and (d) training of fire brigade members. The Fire Code and PD 1096 are complementary — PD 1096 governs construction and physical plant, while RA 9514 governs operational fire safety.

### Philippine Mechanical Engineering Code

Section 1704's exhaust system requirements must be designed in detail per the Philippine Mechanical Engineering Code (R.A. 8495), which governs HVAC and exhaust system design, including fan selection, duct sizing, static pressure calculations, and motor specifications. The architect specifies the performance requirement (30 m/min minimum air velocity); the Mechanical Engineer of Record produces the system design that meets it.

### DOLE Administrative Order on Occupational Safety

The Department of Labor and Employment (DOLE) Department Order 13 (Construction Safety and Health) and related DOLE regulations on occupational safety in spray painting operations require that workers in spray booths use appropriate respiratory protective equipment, that the booth is maintained in accordance with OSHS standards, and that regular air quality monitoring is conducted. These operational requirements work alongside the physical plant standards of PD 1096, Rule XVII.
