# Code of Ethical Conduct

## Board Exam Quick Check

The Code of Ethical Conduct organizes an architect's duties by relationship. It is not only a personal morality statement. The checked source ties conduct to the public, the client, the contractor, manufacturers and suppliers, colleagues, and subordinates.

## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- Identify which relationship is being tested in an ethics scenario.
- Apply client-good-faith duties about scope, fees, doubtful projects, estimates, and outside returns.
- Apply contractor-facing duties about clear documents, fair administration, and rejection of nonconforming work.
- Apply supplier and colleague duties without inventing newer adoption rules not in the checked source.

## Relationship Map

The Code begins by requiring integrity, judgment, business capacity, artistic and technical ability, unprejudiced advice to the client, impartiality between client and contractor, moral responsibility to associates and subordinates, and public responsibility.

Section 1 addresses responsibility to the people. It includes civic and urban service, safety, health, community well-being, professional organization participation, compliance with law and ethical standards, restraint in advertising, and care not to mislead the public about specializations unless the qualifications are well-known facts or sanctioned by professional consensus and experience.

Section 2 addresses responsibility to the client. It includes defining services and charges at the start, advising against questionable projects even if the advice loses a commission, explaining the conditional character of estimates, charging professional fees commensurate with the work and prescribed minimum fee standards, refusing outside returns from interested sources, disclosing business interests that affect the client, and including arbitration in the client agreement.

Section 3 addresses responsibility to the contractor. The architect should provide clear, definite, and consistent information in contract documents, avoid forcing the contractor to pay for the architect's document errors, promptly reject nonconforming materials or workmanship, refuse substantial gifts or favors from contractors, and inspect completed phases when requested.

Section 4 addresses manufacturers, dealers, and agents. Technical exchange is allowed, but services or favors that create obligations harmful to the client are not. Discounts, commissions, gifts, or favors from firms handling materials or equipment are prohibited when they place the architect in a reciprocal frame of mind, except market discounts that are credited to the client.

Section 5 addresses colleagues and subordinates. It covers compensation, competition, prior commissions, work on structures by another architect, professional reputation, doubtful enterprises, signing and sealing work prepared by others, fair treatment and mentoring of employees, and sharing technical information.

## Real World Scenario

A tile supplier offers the architect a private discount if its product is specified. The source-safe answer is not to keep a hidden benefit. Under the supplier section, commissions, discounts, fees, gifts, or favors that create reciprocal obligation are prohibited. A market discount may be accepted only if credited to the client. Under the client section, the architect should also avoid outside returns and disclose business interests that affect client interests.

## Exam Traps

- Treating every ethics question as one generic honesty answer instead of identifying the relationship section.
- Keeping supplier discounts personally instead of crediting a permitted market discount to the client.
- Guaranteeing cost estimates even though the code requires explaining their conditional character.
- Signing and sealing documents not done under the architect's direct personal supervision.
