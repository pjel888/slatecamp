## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal

Identify wood preservation methods (pressure treatment, surface application, thermal modification) and explain how each method protects against decay, termites, and fire in the Philippine tropical climate.
## Core Concept

Untreated wood decays when exposed to moisture, temperature, and biological attack (fungi, termites, beetles, marine borers). Chemical preservatives prevent colonization by these organisms. The preservative must penetrate the wood surface deeply enough to protect for the intended service life, typically 20—50 years depending on use category.

Wood preservation involves applying protective chemicals through pressure treatment (most effective), brush/spray application (surface treatment only), or internal modification (kiln drying, thermal modification). The choice depends on wood species, exposure environment, and durability requirements.

## Preservative Types and Standards

### Pressure-Treated Lumber: Overview

Pressure treatment uses a vacuum and pressure cycle to force preservative deep into the wood. The wood is placed in a sealed cylinder, the air is extracted (vacuum phase), and the preservative is pumped in under 100—200 psi (high-pressure phase). This achieves deep penetration and long service life, superior to surface treatment.

AWPA (American Wood Protection Association) Standard U1 specifies retention levels (pounds of preservative per cubic foot) for each use category. AWPA is the only ANSI-accredited standards developer for treated wood.

### Chromated Copper Arsenate (CCA)

CCA was the dominant preservative worldwide from the 1970s through early 2000s. It is a copper-based fungicide combined with chromium and arsenic for comprehensive protection against decay and insects.

<Note>CCA is now restricted or banned in many countries due to arsenic content and environmental concerns. In the Philippines and many developed nations, CCA-treated wood for residential use (ground contact, play structures) is restricted. CCA remains approved for marine and non-residential applications in some jurisdictions, but new specifications generally exclude it.</Note>

**Properties:** CCA develops high retention levels (0.4 lb/ft³ for UC3 exposure, 0.6 lb/ft³ for ground contact). The wood acquires a greenish tint. Fasteners must be corrosion-resistant because the copper oxidizes standard steel.

### Alkaline Copper Quaternary (ACQ)

ACQ replaced CCA in the 2000s as the primary pressure-treatment preservative. It combines copper with a quaternary ammonium compound (didecyl dimethyl ammonium chloride), which is the insecticide/biocide. ACQ provides excellent protection against decay and termites with no arsenic.

**Properties:** ACQ-treated wood has a greenish tint similar to CCA. ACQ is approximately 5 times more corrosive to common steel than CCA, requiring fasteners meeting ASTM A 153 Class D or better (hot-dipped galvanized minimum; stainless preferred). Retention levels are similar: 0.4—0.6 lb/ft³ depending on use category.

**Limitation:** ACQ has higher cost than copper azole alternatives and slightly higher corrosiveness to fasteners.

### Copper Azole (CA-B and CA-C)

Copper azole is a modern preservative combining copper with an azole (biazole or triazole) co-biocide. The azole enhances decay protection and reduces the copper retention needed, lowering cost.

**Properties:** Copper azole develops adequate protection at lower retentions (0.25—0.4 lb/ft³), making it more economical. The wood has minimal tint or is naturally colored, depending on product formulation. Fastener corrosion is less severe than ACQ, though galvanized fasteners are still recommended.

**Market Position:** CA-B and CA-C are increasingly common in residential and decking applications where cost and appearance favor lower-retention treatments.

### Borate Treatments

Boron-based preservatives (sodium tetraborate pentahydrate, disodium octaborate tetrahydrate) are diffusion treatments suitable for above-ground, non-exterior exposure. Borates penetrate slowly and leach out in wet environments, making them unsuitable for ground contact or exposed outdoor applications.

**Application:** Interior framing, attics, and protected locations. Borate-treated wood is not suitable for decks, fencing, or exposed structural applications in the Philippines due to rainfall and humidity.

## Surface Treatment Methods

### Brush Application

A thin coating of preservative is applied with a brush. This method achieves only surface penetration (less than 1/4 inch), making it suitable only for touch-up of cut ends and damage in otherwise treated lumber, or for interior non-exposed wood.

**Limitations:** Brush application does not provide adequate protection for new lumber and is not recognized as a stand-alone treatment method for structural applications.

### Spray Application

Spraying forces preservative mist onto wood surfaces. Like brushing, it provides only surface coverage and is used for remedial treatment of joints, cut ends, and damage in treated lumber.

**Limitations:** Spray is not accepted as a complete treatment for new lumber and is labor-intensive if applied to large volumes.

### Dip Treatment

The wood is immersed in preservative solution for a brief period (minutes to hours). Dip treatment provides moderate surface and shallow subsurface penetration, suitable for above-ground lumber or as a secondary treatment to improve retention in pressure-treated wood.

**Application:** Exterior millwork, siding, and industrial lumber. Dip is faster than brush/spray but less effective than pressure treatment.

## Wood Drying Methods

### Kiln Drying

Wood is dried in a heated chamber under controlled temperature and humidity. Kiln drying reduces moisture content to 12—15%, the optimal level for most construction and woodworking applications.

**Benefit:** Dry wood is stronger, more stable, and resists decay better than green wood because fungi and termites require high moisture (above 20%) to thrive. Kiln-dried wood also accepts glue better and experiences less warp and checking during service.

**Rate:** Kiln drying takes 2—6 weeks depending on wood thickness and species.

### Air Drying

Wood is stacked outdoors or in ventilated sheds and allowed to dry naturally by wind and sun. Air drying is slower and less controllable but less expensive than kiln drying.

**Moisture Content:** Air-dried wood typically reaches 15—20% moisture content at equilibrium with the local climate. In humid tropical regions (like the Philippines), air-dried wood may not drop below 18—20%, which is still above the optimal gluing and strength benchmark.

**Limitation:** Air drying in tropical climates is less effective; kiln drying is preferred to achieve target moisture levels and minimize decay risk.

## Tropical Wood-Decay Organisms in the Philippines

### Termites (Isoptera)

Philippine termites include subterranean species (which build mudtubes from soil to wood and attack structural timbers) and drywood species (which live entirely within wood). Termites feed on cellulose and can reduce structural timbers to hollow shells within 2—3 years if untreated.

Pressure-treated wood with copper-based preservatives (ACQ, CA-B) provides reliable protection. Borate treatments are insufficient because borates leach in wet tropical conditions. Periodic inspection and chemical re-treatment of vulnerable areas (mud tubes, damaged finishes) are recommended.

### Powder-Post Beetles (Anobiidae)

Small beetles lay eggs in cracks and cut surfaces. Larvae tunnel through wood, creating a fine powder as they emerge. These beetles attack softwoods and some hardwoods, though less commonly than termites in the Philippines.

Preservative treatment and surface finishes (paint, varnish) that seal wood and prevent oviposition provide protection. Kiln drying before construction also helps because the beetles' larvae cannot develop in very dry wood.

### Wood Decay Fungi

Fungal decay (wet rot and dry rot) causes softening and discoloration of wood. In the Philippines' high-humidity environment, fungal decay occurs rapidly on exposed or inadequately sealed timber.

Prevention includes proper drainage, ventilation, and application of fungicidal preservatives. Regular maintenance and early repair of damaged finishes prevent deep fungal colonization.

## Philippine Regulations and DENR Standards

The Philippines Department of Environment and Natural Resources (DENR) regulates timber harvesting and export. Native Philippine hardwoods (narra, molave, yakal) are protected species with restricted harvesting and export quotas.

For construction, imported pressure-treated lumber (primarily ACQ-treated softwoods from North America or Australia) is widely available and conforms to AWPA U1 standards. Locally treated lumber is less common but is occasionally produced under AWPA or equivalent specifications.

NSCP 2015 Section 2401 requires that structural lumber in exposed or high-decay environments be preservative-treated. The standard references AWPA Use Category (UC) designations:

- UC1: Interior, always dry
- UC2: Interior, subject to wetting
- UC3A: Exterior, not in contact with ground (residential)
- UC3B: Exterior, not in contact with ground (commercial/industrial)
- UC4A: Ground contact, in-service (residential/general)
- UC4B: Ground contact, heavy-duty (industrial/marine)

Most Philippine residential construction uses UC3A or UC4A treated lumber for sills, rim boards, and decking.

## Application in Philippine Construction

In tropical Philippines, wood degradation is rapid due to year-round warmth, humidity, and insect activity. Guidelines for practical application:

1. Specify pressure-treated lumber for all exterior and ground-contact applications
2. Use ACQ or copper azole (CA-B, CA-C); avoid CCA for residential applications
3. Specify stainless steel or ASTM A 153 Class D galvanized fasteners (standard steel corrodes faster than the treated wood)
4. Ensure adequate drainage and ventilation around timber to prevent moisture accumulation
5. Apply finish coats (exterior paint or stain) to maintain moisture barrier and UV protection
6. Inspect annually for signs of decay, termite tubes, or paint failure; repair immediately

## How It's Tested

The LEA expects you to:

1. Identify preservative types and their active ingredients
2. Match preservatives to use categories (CCA—UC4, ACQ—UC3 and UC4, borate—UC1 only)
3. Explain why CCA is restricted and name approved replacements
4. Describe the difference between pressure treatment and surface treatment in terms of service life
5. Recognize tropical wood-decay organisms and preservation methods that protect against them
6. Apply DENR and NSCP 2015 requirements for timber in Philippine buildings
7. Determine fastener material requirements for treated wood (galvanized vs. stainless)

Typical questions present a building application (exterior deck, ground-contact beam, interior wall) and ask what preservative treatment and fastener type are appropriate, or ask you to explain why an existing treatment is inadequate.

<Tip>Always consider climate and exposure: tropical Philippines demands more aggressive treatment and fastener corrosion resistance than temperate regions. Never specify standard steel fasteners in treated wood; always require hot-dipped galvanized minimum (stainless preferred).</Tip>
