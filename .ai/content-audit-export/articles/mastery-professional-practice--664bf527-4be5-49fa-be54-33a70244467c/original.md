# Professional Practice & Jurisprudence — Mastery & Connections

Professional Practice & Jurisprudence has a clear internal logic. Once you see the chain — from the law that authorizes the profession, to the standards that define how it is practiced, to the ethics that govern how practitioners behave — the subject stops feeling like a scattered collection of rules and starts feeling like a coherent professional framework.

This article maps that internal logic and the connections to other LEA subjects. If you are already comfortable with the foundational content, this is where you build the integrated understanding that the harder LEA questions demand.

## The Internal Chain: RA 9266 → Everything Else

The logical chain of Professional Practice runs like this:

**RA 9266 establishes the profession.** It defines what architecture is, who may practice it, how practitioners are qualified and registered, what they are responsible for, and what happens when they fall short. Everything else in Professional Practice is downstream of this law.

**Registration establishes the practitioner.** The Board of Architecture, operating under the PRC, administers the LEA, evaluates qualifications, and issues the Certificate of Registration and Professional Identification Card. Without registration, there is no legal authority to practice. RA 9266 Section 20 makes this explicit: no person shall practice architecture in the Philippines without a certificate of registration.

**Ethics governs how the registered architect behaves.** The UAP Code of Ethical Conduct and the ethical provisions of RA 9266 set the standards for professional conduct: honesty with clients, protection of the public interest, avoidance of conflicts of interest, fair dealing with colleagues. Ethics is not a separate topic — it is the behavioral layer that sits on top of every professional interaction.

**Services define what the architect does.** The SPP documents translate the general authorization to practice (from RA 9266) into specific definitions of what services architects provide, how those services are structured in phases, and what the architect delivers at each stage.

**Fees define how the architect is compensated.** SPP 301 sets the standard fee scleas for those services. The fee methods (percentage of PCC, lump sum, time-based, per unit) are different ways of translating the value of architectural services into a number both parties can agree on.

**Contracts define the legal relationship.** The owner-architect agreement and the owner-contractor agreement are the instruments through which services and fees are formalized into enforceable commitments. Construction administration is where the architect's contractual obligations to the owner meet the contractor's contractual obligations to the owner — and where the architect must act with both loyalty to the client and fairness to all parties.

<Callout>The chain runs in one direction for most purposes: law → registration → ethics → services → fees → contracts. But the LEA can test any link in the chain and any connection between links. A question about an architect's fee dispute involves services, fees, and contracts simultaneously. A question about professional misconduct involves ethics, registration, and RA 9266 simultaneously. Train yourself to see the whole chain.</Callout>

## The SPP Document Chain in Detail

The SPP documents form their own internal sequence. Understanding how they relate to each other makes it easier to recall which document governs which situation.

**SPP 200** is the master document — it defines architectural services in general and establishes the framework within which all the specific service documents operate.

**SPP 201** (Pre-Design Services) covers the work that happens before the design begins: programming, feasibility studies, site selection, project management. These are often overlooked but are increasingly common in large or complex projects.

**SPP 202** (Regular Design Services) is the core document — the standard design-bid-build service delivery model. It defines the five phases: Pre-Design, Schematic Design, Design Development, Contract Documents, and Construction Phase. Most fee computation questions are rooted here.

**SPP 203** (Specialized Architectural Services) covers specialized services beyond the standard design scope: acoustic design, interior architecture, historic preservation, landscape architecture, urban design.

**SPP 204** (Architect's Role in Construction Management) covers the architect's services when the delivery method is construction management rather than design-bid-build. The architect's role during construction is different in this model — more advisory, less authoritative over the contractor.

**SPP 205** (Post-Construction Services) covers services after project completion: facilities management consulting, post-occupancy evaluation, as-built documentation.

**SPP 206** (Comprehensive Architectural Services) covers the full spectrum of services from pre-design through post-construction, typically for large institutional or government clients.

**SPP 207** (Architectural Interior Design Services) covers interior design services as a distinct service category.

**SPP 208** (Design-Build Services) covers the architect's role when the delivery method is design-build — where the architect and contractor are on the same side of the contract, working for the owner as an integrated team.

**SPP 301** (Standard Fee Guidelines) is the fee document — the standard percentage scleas, the computation methodology, and the guidelines for non-standard fee arrangements.

<ExamTip>LEA questions about SPP documents often describe a service scenario and ask which SPP document governs it. The key is knowing what makes each document distinct: SPP 201 is pre-design, SPP 202 is regular design, SPP 208 is design-build, SPP 301 is fees. When in doubt, ask: what phase? what delivery method? what type of service?</ExamTip>

## Connections to Laws & Codes

Professional Practice and Laws & Codes share RA 9266 as a common reference, but they test it differently.

**In Professional Practice**, RA 9266 questions tend to focus on:
- The scope and limitations of architectural practice
- The registration process and qualifications for licensure
- The grounds for disciplinary action
- The roles of the Board of Architecture and the PRC

**In Laws & Codes**, RA 9266 questions tend to focus on:
- Specific statutory provisions and their exact language
- The penalties for unauthorized practice
- The interaction of RA 9266 with other professional regulations (engineering laws, contractors' law)
- The legal liability of registered architects for their professional work

The same section of the law can be the basis for a Practice question (what does this section mean for how you run your practice?) and a Laws question (what is the exact penalty specified in this section?). When you study RA 9266, read it from both angles.

Other laws that bridge Professional Practice and Laws & Codes:

**PD 1096 (National Building Code):** In Practice, the relevant angle is the architect's responsibility to ensure designs comply with the code. In Laws, the relevant angle is the specific permit requirements, penalties, and liabilities the code creates.

**RA 9184 (Government Procurement Reform Act):** In Practice, relevant to architects competing for government projects (procurement methods, bidding procedures, JV rules). In Laws, relevant to the legal framework governing public procurement and the liability for violations.

**EO 398 (Contractors' License Law) / RA 4566:** In Practice, relevant to understanding the contractor's obligations and the boundary between architectural and contracting services. In Laws, relevant to the specific licensing requirements for contractors.

## Connections to Architectural Design

The connection between Professional Practice and Architectural Design is most visible in **project delivery** and **construction administration**.

**Project delivery method shapes design decisions.** In design-build, the architect and contractor must coordinate from the earliest stages — which encourages integration of structural and systems decisions early in the design process. In traditional design-bid-build, the design is largely complete before contractor input is sought — which places more responsibility on the architect to fully coordinate the design before the documents go out to bid.

**Construction administration is design in implementation.** The architect's construction phase services — reviewing shop drawings, responding to RFIs, issuing clarifications, reviewing substitution requests — are exercises in design judgment applied to the real conditions of a building under construction. The decisions an architect makes during construction administration can significantly affect the quality of the built work.

**Fee structure reflects design scope.** The percentage-of-PCC fee model means that more complex, more expensive buildings generate higher fees — which is meant to reflect the greater design effort they require. When a scope changes significantly during construction, the architect's compensation should adjust accordingly. Understanding fee structure helps you understand why scope management is a design issue as well as a business issue.

<Tip>When studying construction administration, think of it as the final phase of design — not a separate activity. The RFI, the shop drawing review, the change order: each one is a moment where the design intent meets construction reality, and the architect's job is to ensure the intent survives the collision.</Tip>

## Integration Patterns the LEA Tests

**The scenario with multiple relationships.** LEA questions often describe a project with an owner, an architect, a contractor, and sometimes a subcontractor or a third-party consultant. The question asks about the architect's obligation in a specific situation — a payment dispute, a defective work claim, a change in scope. Working through these scenarios requires knowing the contractual chain (who has a contract with whom), the SPP service framework (what the architect's obligations are), and the ethical framework (what the architect owes to each party).

**The ethics-meets-business scenario.** Questions where a client offers an architect a referral fee, or where an architect is asked to recommend a contractor who has given them gifts, or where an architect is tempted to certify work they have not inspected — these combine ethical obligations with practical practice knowledge. The UAP Code and RA 9266 are both relevant, but the question is testing judgment, not just recall.

**The fee computation with a twist.** A standard fee computation with a complication: a change of scope, a suspension of services, a partial services arrangement, or a fee for a specialized service layer on top of regular services. These require you to apply the standard methodology and then adjust for the specific conditions.

**The registration or CPD question.** Who is required to obtain a license? What are the CPD requirements for renewal? What happens if an architect allows their license to lapse and continues to practice? These questions test the administrative framework of the profession alongside its practice standards.

Professional Practice rewards the candidate who takes it seriously as a coherent system — not a list of rules to memorize, but a framework for understanding what it means to be a licensed architect in the Philippines. Study it with that frame, and the details will organize themselves around the principles.
