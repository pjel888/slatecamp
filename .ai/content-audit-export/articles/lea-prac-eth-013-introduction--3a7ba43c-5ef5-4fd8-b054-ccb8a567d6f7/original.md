## Introduction
## Learning Goal

After reading this article, you should be able to:

- Explain the general rule that fee competition among architects is legal and ethical.
- Identify prohibited anti-competitive practices: collusive fee-fixing, predatory pricing, and deceptive fee practices.
- Distinguish ethical fee discounting from unethical practices that compromise professional standards.
- Apply fee competition rules to LEA scenarios involving minimum fee schedules, competitive bidding, and professional standards.

The relationship between professional ethics and fair competition in architecture creates tension: architects must maintain professional standards while competing fairly with one another for projects and fees. This article examines the ethical limits on fee competition, restrictions on setting minimum fees, and the distinction between competitive fee structures and unethical cost-cutting.

The Code of Ethical Conduct and RA 9266 prohibit fee agreements that restrict competition (collusion on pricing) while also establishing professional standards that fee-discounting cannot undermine. LEA candidates must understand when fee competition is ethical, when it crosses into anti-competitive practice, and how professional standards limit cost-cutting.

## Fundamental Principle: Fee Competition is Legal and Ethical

The legal and professional framework in the Philippines allows architects to compete on fees while maintaining professional standards:

<LeartBox />

## Category 1: Anti-Competitive Fee Practices (Prohibited)

### Type 1A: Collusive Fee-Fixing Agreements

**Definition:** Two or more architects agree to charge the same (or minimum) fees, eliminating competition on price.

**Scenario 1:** Architects in a region meet informally and agree to charge minimum fees for different project types: residential design at 5% of construction cost, commercial at 4%, etc. Each architect agrees to not bid below these minimums.

**Why it's prohibited:**
- **Eliminates fee competition:** Clients cannot seek lower fees from competing architects
- **Fixes prices:** Clients are denied price discovery and competitive bidding benefits
- **Violates competition law:** Collusive pricing agreements violate Philippine Competition Act (RA 10667)
- **Professional violation:** Code of Ethics Section 4 (honest professional relationships); RA 9266 Section 23

**Consequences:**
- **Professional discipline:** Suspension 2-3 years; possible revocation
- **Criminal liability:** Potential prosecution under RA 10667 (competition violations can result in fines and imprisonment)
- **Civil liability:** Clients affected by price-fixing may sue for overcharges

<ExamTip />

### Type 1B: Recommended Fee Schedule Agreements

**Definition:** Professional organizations (like UAP) publish "recommended" fee schedules that operate as de facto minimum fees because architects coordinate around them.

**Ethical/Legal Status:**

In many countries, recommended fee schedules have been eliminated due to anti-competitive concerns. In the Philippines:

- **Publishing suggested fees:** A professional organization may publish educational information about typical fees
- **Coordinating around the schedule:** If architects use the "recommendation" as a floor below which they won't bid, this functions as price-fixing
- **Enforcement of recommendations:** If the professional organization polices members who undercut the recommendation, this is anti-competitive

**Analysis:**

The key distinction is:
- **Informational:** "Architects in the region typically charge 4-6% for residential design" (educational, not collusive)
- **Coordinated:** "We all agree to charge at least 4%" (collusive, anti-competitive)

Even if not formally agreed, if architects use a published schedule as a coordination point to avoid competing below the recommended minimum, it functions as price-fixing.

**Consequences:** Same as collusive pricing agreements—professional discipline and potential criminal liability

### Type 1C: Market Division or Customer Allocation Agreements

**Definition:** Competing architects divide markets or agree not to compete for certain clients or project types.

**Scenario 2:** Architects A and B agree that Architect A will focus on government projects while Architect B will focus on private clients. Neither will compete for work in the other's "territory."

**Why it's prohibited:**
- **Eliminates competition:** Clients in each territory have no competitive choice among architects
- **Divides market:** Reduces effective competition
- **Restricts business:** Each architect agrees not to compete for certain projects
- **Violates competition law:** Market allocation agreements are per se violations of RA 10667

**Consequences:** Professional discipline and criminal liability

### Type 1D: Bid-Rigging Agreements

**Definition:** Architects agree in advance which architect will submit the lowest bid and win a project, rotating the "winning" bids among themselves.

**Scenario 3:** Three architects submit competitive bids for a government project. Unknown to the client, they agreed in advance which architect would bid lowest. The bids are designed to appear competitive but are actually pre-determined.

**Why it's prohibited:**
- **Defrauds clients:** Client believes bids are competitive; actually they are predetermined
- **Eliminates genuine competition:** Client doesn't receive benefit of true competitive bidding
- **Divides revenue:** Architects take turns "winning" projects
- **Violates competition law:** Bid-rigging is per se violation of RA 10667

**Consequences:** Serious—professional discipline (possible revocation), criminal prosecution, potential imprisonment

## Category 2: Ethical Fee Competition (Permitted)

### Type 2A: Independent Fee Determination

**Ethical approach:** Each architect independently determines fees based on:

- **Project complexity and scope**
- **Architect's experience and specialization**
- **Market rates in the region**
- **Firm's cost structure and profit expectations**
- **Client's budget and ability to pay**
- **Value provided (design quality, innovation, expertise)**

**Analysis:**

Multiple architects will likely arrive at similar fee ranges through independent analysis. This is **not collusion**—it's natural market operation where competent professionals analyzing the same factors reach similar conclusions.

**Example:** Five architects independently analyze a 10,000 m² commercial building project and determine fees of:
- Architect 1: 3.5% of construction cost
- Architect 2: 3.8% of construction cost
- Architect 3: 4.0% of construction cost
- Architect 4: 4.2% of construction cost
- Architect 5: 4.5% of construction cost

These varied fees are **not collusive**. Each architect arrived at the fee independently, and clients can choose among the options. The existence of a range demonstrates competition.

### Type 2B: Competitive Bidding for Design Services

**Ethical approach:** Clients solicit competitive bids from multiple architects, and architects submit independent fee proposals.

**Standard practice:**
1. Client publishes RFQ (request for qualifications) or RFP (request for proposals)
2. Multiple architects respond with fee proposals
3. Client evaluates proposals on fee, qualifications, and approach
4. Client selects architect based on best value (not necessarily lowest fee)

**Ethical considerations:**
- Architects submit fees **independently**, without coordination
- Each architect presents **authentic approach** (not copying others)
- Architects may **lose bids** to competitors with lower fees or better value
- **Low bidders** must still deliver professional-quality services (no cost-cutting)

<ExamTip />

### Type 2C: Value-Based Fee Approaches

**Ethical approach:** Instead of percentage-of-construction-cost fees, architects negotiate fees based on value delivered:

- **Fixed fees** for defined scope
- **Hourly rates** for specialized services
- **Negotiated fees** based on project value and complexity
- **Performance-based fees** tied to project outcomes (cost savings, schedule efficiency)

**Example:** Rather than charging 4% of construction cost, Architect negotiates:
- Basic design services: $50,000 fixed fee
- Value engineering: Additional $10,000 if architect's design innovations save >5% on construction cost
- Expedited schedule: Additional $5,000 per month saved

This approach allows **fee flexibility** while tying architect compensation to **value delivered**, creating healthy competition on both price and quality.

### Type 2D: Competitive Advantage Through Efficiency

**Ethical approach:** Architects compete on fees by:

- **Improving efficiency** (faster design process)
- **Developing specialization** (economies of scale in specific building types)
- **Leveraging technology** (BIM, prefabrication expertise, digital tools)
- **Building reputation** (higher-quality designs command higher fees)

**Example:** Architect develops specialization in modular residential design, achieving 30% faster design time through standardized systems. This allows fees 15% lower than generalist architects while maintaining profitability. This is **ethical competition** based on efficiency and expertise.

## Category 3: The Limits of Fee Competition—When Low Fees Violate Professional Standards

### Prohibition: Designing Below Professional Standards to Compete on Price

**Definition:** An architect wins work through aggressive fee discounting but then provides inadequate design services to compensate for the low fee.

**Scenario 4:** Architect C wins a complex commercial project by underbidding competitors significantly (offering design services at 2% instead of typical 4%). To deliver design at this price point, Architect C:

- Provides minimal site investigation
- Skips specialized consultant input (structural, MEP)
- Reduces drawing quality and clarity
- Provides minimal construction administration
- Fails to verify code compliance

**Why it's prohibited:**
- **Violates standard of care:** Code of Ethics Section 1 requires "honesty of purpose and entire impartiality"
- **Breaches fiduciary duty:** Client is entitled to professional-quality services at the fee price
- **Endangers public safety:** Inadequate design compromises building safety and performance
- **Harms profession:** Low-fee/low-quality work damages profession's reputation

**Consequences:**
- **Professional discipline:** Client complaints lead to investigation
- **Negligence liability:** Building deficiencies result in client claims
- **Reputational harm:** Word spreads about inadequate design quality

<LeartBox />

### Prohibition: Coercive Low-Fee Arrangements

**Definition:** A larger firm or client pressures architects to accept below-cost fees as condition of future work.

**Scenario 5:** Large developer requires all architects bidding for projects to accept fees at 50% of market rates, threatening to hire competitors if any architect refuses. Architects feel compelled to accept the below-cost fees to maintain their business relationship.

**Why it's prohibited:**
- **Coercion:** Developers cannot compel professionals to work below-cost through threat of exclusion
- **Predatory practice:** Deliberately underbidding to eliminate competition, then raising prices after competitors are eliminated
- **Labor exploitation:** If below-cost fees require architects to work uncompensated overtime, they violate employment standards
- **Professional harm:** Systematic below-cost work undermines the profession's economic viability

**Consequences:**
- The developer's conduct may violate RA 10667 (competition law) by coercing below-cost bids
- Architects should refuse coercive below-cost arrangements and seek other clients
- Reporting coercive practices to professional associations or authorities is appropriate

### Prohibition: Predatory Pricing Intended to Eliminate Competition

**Definition:** An architect deliberately prices services far below cost to drive competitors out of business, intending to raise prices once competition is eliminated.

**Scenario 6:** Large multinationals architectural firm enters a regional market and bids projects at substantial losses to capture market share and drive out local competitors. Once competitors are eliminated, the firm plans to raise prices.

**Why it's prohibited:**
- **Anti-competitive predatory practice:** Violates RA 10667 (Philippine Competition Act)
- **Market manipulation:** Intended to monopolize market, not to serve client interests
- **Harm to profession:** Eliminates local competition and disrupts market
- **Unsustainable:** Below-cost work cannot be sustained indefinitely

**Consequences:**
- **Competition authority action:** RA 10667 authorities (PCC) may investigate
- **Professional discipline:** PRBoA may discipline for conduct prejudicial to profession
- **Client litigation:** Clients harmed by predatory pricing may seek damages

## Category 4: Professional Standards as Limits on Fee Reduction

The Code of Ethical Conduct establishes that professional standards cannot be compromised for fee reduction. This includes:

### Standard 1: Investigation and Research Phase

**Requirement:** Architect must conduct adequate site investigation, client needs assessment, and code/regulatory research before design.

**Fee pressure problem:** An architect with inadequate fee might skip or minimize this phase to reduce costs.

**Professional minimum:** Even with low fees, architect cannot avoid:
- Site visit and assessment
- Client needs confirmation
- Code and zoning verification
- Competitive analysis if relevant
- Regulatory requirement research

If the fee is insufficient for these investigations, the architect should:
- Negotiate higher fee, or
- Decline the engagement

**Discipline consequence:** Skipping investigations due to fee pressure constitutes negligence, subject to discipline

### Standard 2: Design Development and Coordination

**Requirement:** Architect must coordinate building systems, verify structural viability, and produce clear drawings.

**Fee pressure problem:** Low fees might incentivize incomplete design or minimal coordination.

**Professional minimum:** Even with low fees, architect cannot avoid:
- Structural coordination and engineer consultation
- MEP systems coordination
- Clear, complete drawing sets
- Technical specification
- Code compliance review

**Discipline consequence:** Inadequate design coordination due to fee pressure constitutes negligence

### Standard 3: Construction Administration

**Requirement:** During construction, architect must conduct inspections and verify code compliance.

**Fee pressure problem:** Low overall project fees is offset by minimizing CA time and inspections.

**Professional minimum:** CA commitment cannot be reduced below standard. If the overall fee is insufficient for adequate CA, architect should:
- Increase CA fees, or
- Limit scope (e.g., design-only, no CA), or
- Decline engagement

**Discipline consequence:** Inadequate CA due to insufficient fees is negligence

## Category 5: Permissible Fee Variations

### Variation 1: Reduced Fees for Simplified or Repetitive Projects

**Ethically permissible:** An architect may charge lower fees for:

- **Standardized designs:** If a design is largely copied from prior projects
- **Repetitive buildings:** Multiple similar buildings (e.g., housing units, retail modules)
- **Limited scope:** If client limits services (e.g., design-only, no CA)
- **Negotiated discounts:** Client requests lower fee for large volume or long-term relationship

**Key requirement:** Even with reduced fees, architect must deliver professional-quality services within the reduced scope.

**Example:** Architect develops a modular residential design for a developer building 50 units. Design services for Unit 1 are $50,000 (full design development). Units 2-50 are adapted variations at $5,000 each (modified from Unit 1). This is **ethically permissible** because:
- Each unit receives professional design quality appropriate to its scope
- The fee reduction reflects reduced design effort (variation vs. full design)
- The client benefits from efficiency and volume pricing

### Variation 2: Cross-Subsidization Among Project Types

**Ethically permissible:** An architect may offer lower fees for some projects to subsidize lower fees for others, as long as all projects receive professional-quality services.

**Example:** Architect charges 5% fee for large commercial projects (high volume, lower relative design time) and 3% for small residential projects (lower volume). Cross-subsidization allows competitive pricing on smaller projects while maintaining profitability overall.

**Key requirement:** Each project's fee must be sufficient for professional-quality services within that project's scope.

### Variation 3: Geographic or Market-Based Fee Differences

**Ethically permissible:** Architects may charge different fees in different markets based on local cost of living, competitive factors, and market rates.

**Example:** Architect charges 4% in Manila (competitive market) and 5% in provincial markets (less competition). This reflects local market conditions.

**Key requirement:** Fees must be independently determined, not result from collusive agreements with competitors in each market.

## Category 6: Special Cases and Fee Ethics

### Case 1: Government Project Competitive Bids

**Context:** Government projects often require competitive sealed bids at specified fee levels.

**Ethical considerations:**
- Architects must submit **independent** bids, not coordinate with competitors
- Fees must be **sufficient** for professional-quality services at the bid level
- If specified fee is **below-cost**, architect should either:
 - Accept the risk and negotiate contingencies, or
 - Decline to bid

**Example:** Government RFP specifies design fee at 3.5% of estimated construction cost. Architect's normal minimum is 4.5%. Architect must decide:
- Accept 3.5% if achievable, or
- Decline to bid

**What architect cannot do:** Coordinate with competitors to all bid 3.5% (collusion) or submit artificially low bid intending to demand change orders later (fraud).

### Case 2: Design-Build Services Fee Pressures

**Context:** In design-build projects, contractors often pressure designers to accept lower design fees (since the contractor profits from construction).

**Ethical considerations:**
- Designer must **resist pressure** for fees insufficient for professional services
- **Fixed design fees** should be separated from contractor profit to maintain design independence
- **Subcontracting architects** should ensure adequate compensation for quality work

**Example:** Contractor offers to hire Architect as subconsultant for design-build project at 50% of normal design fees. Architect should:
- Negotiate adequate design fee, or
- Decline if the contractor won't allow adequate design compensation

**What architect cannot do:** Accept inadequate design fee and then provide low-quality design to minimize losses.

### Case 3: Public Interest Work and Pro Bono Services

**Context:** Architects sometimes provide pro bono or below-cost services for public interest projects (low-income housing, nonprofits, government agencies with budget constraints).

**Ethical status:** **Permissible and commendable**, with conditions:

- **Voluntary choice:** Architect elects to reduce/eliminate fees for public interest reason
- **Maintained quality:** Pro bono work still receives professional-quality services
- **Sustainability:** Architect must ensure pro bono work doesn't undermine firm's financial viability
- **Transparency:** Client should understand the pro bono nature of the arrangement

**Example:** Architect agrees to design low-income housing for nonprofit at 50% of normal fees. This is ethically commendable if:
- Architect voluntarily chooses to reduce fees
- Design quality is professional
- Firm can afford the reduced revenue
- Client understands the subsidized arrangement

**What architect cannot do:** Agree to pro bono work but deliver inadequate design to minimize loss.
