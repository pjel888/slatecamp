## Learning Goal

Understand the five-phase architectural design process (Schematic Design through Construction Administration) and the deliverables, approvals, and stakeholder roles at each phase.

## Core Concept

Architecture is not created in isolation. The **architectural design process** is the systematic progression through which an architect translates client needs and project requirements into a complete building design. This process unfolds in distinct phases, each with specific deliverables, stakeholders, and decision-making criteria. Understanding these phases is essential to the Philippine architecture licensure examination, as the LEA repeatedly tests candidates on how architects organize their work and communicate with clients and contractors.

The standard design process, codified by the American Institute of Architects and adopted internationally, typically encompasses five major phases: schematic design, design development, construction documents, bidding and negotiation, and construction administration. Each phase builds on the previous one, progressively refining the design from concept to reality.

**Schematic Design** marks the beginning of the designer's formal engagement with the project brief. During this phase, the architect translates the client's stated wishes, budget constraints, site conditions, and programmatic requirements into preliminary design concepts. The work typically consists of sketches, preliminary floor plans, site analyses, building sections, and three-dimensional renderings or models. The goal is not to finalize every detail, but to establish the big-picture concept: the general layout of spaces, the overall form and mass of the building, the relationship between interior and exterior, and the approximate scale before investing time in detailed specifications. The schematic phase concludes when the client approves the general design direction.

**Design Development** commences once the schematic concept is approved. This phase transforms the architect's design intent into increasingly detailed drawings and specifications. If the project requires structural engineers, mechanical engineers, or other consultants, they typically join the design team at this stage. The architect develops and presents the client with specific material selections for both exterior and interior finishes, mechanical systems are outlined, structural approaches are defined, and the building's performance characteristics begin to be quantified. Construction costs are estimated more precisely, and the design is refined in response to both aesthetic and practical considerations. The completion of design development results in drawings and specifications sufficiently detailed to indicate the project's final form and systems, though not every construction detail has been resolved.

**Construction Documents**, sometimes called technical design, constitute the phase where design becomes constructible reality. The architect produces comprehensive sets of drawings and technical specifications that specify every material, dimension, connection, and operational detail of the final design. These documents translate the design development drawings into a format that enables a contractor to actually build the project. All necessary technical information is finalized, including building code compliance verification, structural calculations, mechanical and electrical system layouts, material specifications, and construction methodologies. The final set of drawings and specifications must contain all information required to obtain building permits and to bid the project competitively. These documents serve as the primary contract document between the owner and contractor.

**Bidding and Negotiation** occurs when the project is released to prospective contractors. The architect typically assists the owner in soliciting competitive bids, evaluating contractor qualifications, and negotiating contract terms. This phase ensures that the project is understood consistently across the construction industry and that costs are competitive. The architect's role here is primarily consultative—advising the owner on contractor selection and contract structure.

**Construction Administration** begins when construction commences and continues until substantial completion. During this phase, the architect's primary role shifts from creative design to project management and quality assurance. While the architect does not physically manage the construction site, they make regular site visits to verify that the contractor is executing the work in accordance with the approved drawings and specifications. The architect reviews shop drawings submitted by the contractor, interprets design intent when field conditions differ from anticipated conditions, processes change orders if modifications become necessary, and certifies payment requests. The architect serves as the primary communication channel between the design intent and the construction reality, ensuring design integrity throughout the building process.

These five phases are not rigidly sequential. On smaller projects, phases may overlap or be compressed. On design-build projects where a single firm both designs and constructs the building, the phases may be organized differently. On highly complex projects, additional phases may be added, and stakeholder engagement may occur throughout the entire process. Regardless of the specific project delivery method, the fundamental progression from concept to construction remains the architect's systematic approach to design problem-solving.

## Key Terms

**Schematic Design** — The earliest design phase in which the architect develops preliminary design concepts, site analysis, and spatial organization in response to client requirements and program.

**Design Development** — The phase following schematic design approval, in which the architect refines the design, selects systems and materials, and produces drawings sufficiently detailed to indicate final design intent.

**Construction Documents** — The comprehensive set of drawings, specifications, and schedules produced for construction, containing all information required for bidding and building permit approval.

**Bidding** — The procurement process in which contractors submit competitive bids based on Construction Documents, and the owner selects a contractor.

**Construction Administration** — The phase during which the architect conducts site visits, reviews contractor submissions, interprets design intent, and certifies compliance with Construction Documents.

**Design Development Drawings** — Detailed drawings produced during Design Development showing spatial relationships, material selections, systems, and design intent at greater precision than schematic drawings.

**Shop Drawings** — Detailed drawings submitted by contractors or suppliers showing how specific components will be fabricated and installed, requiring architect review and approval.

**Request for Information (RFI)** — A formal inquiry from the contractor to the architect seeking clarification or interpretation of the design documents during construction.

**Change Order** — A document modifying the original contract scope, cost, or schedule, typically issued during Construction Administration when conditions differ from documented expectations.

**Design Intent** — The architect's conceptual and technical vision for the project, communicated through drawings, specifications, and decisions that guide construction.
