## Project Management in an Architectural Firm—Scope, Schedule, Budget, and Communication Control

Project management is the discipline of planning, coordinating, and controlling project variables—scope, time, cost, and quality—to deliver projects successfully. Architects who manage projects well earn client loyalty, maintain profitability, and build reputation. Poor project management leads to cost overruns, missed deadlines, scope disputes, and professional liability. This article addresses project management practices specific to architectural service delivery in the Philippines, governed by Republic Act No. 9266 and the Standards of Professional Practice.

## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- Define project management within an architectural firm.
- Describe the four pillars: scope, schedule, budget, communication.
- Explain the architect's role as project manager vs. design lead.
- Identify project management tools and techniques relevant to practice.

### Project Scope Definition and Change Control

Under the UAP Standards of Professional Practice (SPP Documents 201-209), the scope of architectural services must be clearly defined to prevent disputes. A clear scope statement in the Owner-Architect Agreement outlines: (1) project phases (programming, schematic design, design development, contract documents, construction administration); (2) deliverables per phase (number of design options, presentation materials, construction document sets); (3) included services (site investigation, consultations, meetings); (4) excluded services (sustainability consulting, cost estimating, specialized engineering); and (5) client responsibilities (timely decisions, providing information).

The service agreement is the binding contract. Ambiguity about scope creates disputes—the client believes services are included; the architect believes they are additional and chargeable. Once scope is defined, any changes require formal change orders signed by both parties. Change orders should describe the additional work, explain the reason, and specify the additional fee or schedule impact.

Architects should resist scope creep—the gradual addition of services without compensation. Common scope creep includes: the client requesting a new design alternative during design development; asking for detailed cost estimating when not contracted; requesting ongoing consulting after project completion. Architects should politely but firmly direct these requests to change order procedures as mandated by SPP guidelines.

### Schedule Management and Milestone Tracking

Schedules establish expected completion dates for each project phase and identify dependencies. A typical architectural project takes 6–18 months from programming through construction documents, depending on project complexity and client decision speed. The architect should establish interim milestones—schematic design completion by date X, design development review by date Y—and communicate these clearly to the client.

Delays typically result from slow client decisions (approvals, information), design complexity requiring rework, or regulatory reviews (building permit, environmental approval). The architect cannot control all delays but should proactively identify bottlenecks and communicate the impact on the schedule. If environmental review takes 4 months (as required by regulatory agencies), the schedule should reflect this from the outset.

The architect should document when delays are client-caused (late decisions, delayed information) versus architect-caused (design rework). This documentation protects the architect if the final schedule is extended and the client claims the architect is responsible.

### Budget Management and Fee Collection

As outlined in SPP Document 202 (Regular Design Services), architects are typically compensated through standard methods such as: percentage of Project Construction Cost (PCC), Multiple of Direct Personnel Expenses, Professional Fee Plus Expenses, Lump Sum or Fixed Fee, Per Diem/Honorarium, or Mixed Methods of Compensation.

Percentage fees align architect and client interests but require clear boundaries if the project scope expands. Lump-sum fees lock the architect's cost; if the project proves more complex, the architect absorbs cost overruns. The architect should track hours and costs throughout the project. If a lump-sum contract is used, monitoring actual hours against budgeted hours reveals whether the project is on financial track. If hours are exceeding budget, the architect can request a change order or discuss project scope adjustment.

Furthermore, Republic Act No. 9266 (The Architecture Act of 2004), specifically **Section 36 (Collection of Professional Fees)**, legally mandates and protects the registered and licensed architect's right to collect remuneration for services rendered. Fee collection discipline is essential to firm profitability. Invoices should be submitted promptly; payment terms should be stated in the contract (usually net 15 or 30 days). If a client delays payment, the architect should follow up promptly. Non-payment can damage firm cash flow and project morale.

### Communication Management: Meetings, Decisions, and Documentation

Clear communication prevents misunderstanding and protects the architect. The architect should: (1) document all design decisions and the rationale; (2) maintain meeting minutes summarizing decisions, action items, and responsible parties; (3) submit design and construction documents in clear, professional format; and (4) respond promptly to client inquiries.

Design decisions should be documented through sketches, memos, and presentation materials. If the client later claims a feature was not what they wanted, documentation showing the design decision was approved protects the architect. Meeting minutes should note attendees, topics discussed, decisions made, and next steps. Email correspondence confirming decisions creates a written record.

For virtual projects or remote teams, communication discipline is even more critical. Architects should use project collaboration platforms (shared document repositories, messaging systems) that maintain records and avoid information loss.

### Quality Control and Constructability Review

The architect must verify that construction documents are complete, coordinated, and compliant with codes and standards (such as the National Building Code of the Philippines). A constructability review—ideally with contractor input—helps identify build-ability issues, cost-saving opportunities, and construction sequence challenges. Rework during construction is costly and may extend the schedule.

Internal quality control processes include: design reviews by senior staff, code compliance checklists, consultant coordination reviews (MEP, structural, landscape integration), and final document review before issuance. These processes catch errors before they reach the client or the contractor.

### Consultant Coordination and Agreement Management

Under RA 9266 and the SPP, the architect is the prime professional responsible for the overall design. Architects typically engage allied professionals and consultants (structural engineers, MEP engineers, landscape architects, specialty consultants).

Clear agreements with consultants must specify their scope, deliverables, coordination obligations, insurance requirements, and fee arrangements. The architect acts as the lead coordinator—ensuring consultants submit work on schedule, review each other's work for conflicts, and issue coordinated documents. Disputes among consultants (structural vs. MEP design conflicts, for example) are the architect's responsibility to manage and resolve through integrated design. The architect should establish clear coordination protocols, conduct regular design meetings, and use BIM or other coordination tools to catch conflicts early.

### Risk Management and Professional Liability

Project risks include: client default on payment, design errors causing construction cost overruns, regulatory disapproval delaying the project, and consultant errors. The architect should identify these risks early and develop mitigation strategies. Professional liability insurance protects against design errors; contract language should specify who bears risk for regulatory delays or client-initiated changes.

The architect must maintain complete project files including drawings, correspondence, meeting minutes, and design calculations. These files document the work performed and are essential if disputes arise or if a PRC Board of Architecture investigation occurs. Under the Civil Code of the Philippines (Article 1723), the architect holds a 15-year liability for the collapse of a building due to defects in plans or specifications.

### Post-Project Review and Continuous Improvement

After project completion, the architect should conduct a brief post-project review. Was the project completed on schedule? Within budget? Did the design meet the client's needs? What went well? What could improve? This reflection helps the firm learn and improve processes for future projects.

Client feedback, whether positive or critical, is valuable. A client survey or exit interview can identify areas for improvement. Firms that systematically review projects and implement improvements build strong reputations and attract repeat clients.
