# Project Management in an Architectural Firm: Scope, Schedule, Budget, and Communication Control

## Exam scope

Project management in an architectural office is practical control of scope, budget, schedule, quality, coordination, and documented decisions. The exam-relevant core is that projects are controlled by scope, budget, and schedule, and that the project manager plans and controls execution through team authority, design reviews, constructability checks, document control, and risk management.

## Scope, budget, and schedule

The project-management source defines projects by scope, budget, and schedule. Scope describes the required work and performance of the deliverables. Budget is the funding and cost-control constraint. Schedule gives the defined beginning, end, activity sequence, and critical-path relationships.

Scope refinement is normal as better project information becomes available. Scope creep is different: it is a serious change in scope after the project is underway and may enlarge the facility, require redesign, increase budget, or delay the schedule. In architectural firm practice, this distinction is why client-requested additions should be documented as service and scope changes instead of absorbed informally.

## Project manager authority

The source handbook states that project execution is planned and controlled by the project manager, who needs adequate authority to form and manage the team supporting the project. The manager also needs prior relevant experience or equivalent support. In an architectural office, that authority is practical rather than ornamental: someone must own scope decisions, consultant coordination, review dates, cost-estimate checkpoints, and the record of decisions.

<Tip>
**Five essential quality control processes:** (1) design reviews by senior staff; (2) code compliance checklists; (3) consultant coordination reviews (MEP, structural, landscape); (4) final document review before issuance; (5) constructability review with contractor input. These catch errors before they reach the client or contractor.
</Tip>

## Design reviews and quality control

Design reviews are formal control points used to verify deliverables such as studies, final drawings, technical specifications, and construction bid documents. The handbook identifies common review points at design criteria, conceptual, preliminary engineering or 30 percent, 60 percent, 90 percent, 100 percent, and bid-document stages.

The review objectives are source-specific: adherence to design criteria, design quality, errors and omissions, building-code compliance, operational and functional objectives, discipline coordination, cost-estimate adherence to budget, constructability, interface compatibility, and final contract-document compliance with criteria, codes, and regulations.

Quality control in design requires a quality management plan, documented design-change procedures, sign-off and document control. Calculations, drawings, and specifications should be checked by qualified personnel, and the design should be verified against the scope.

## Constructability, risk, and communication control

Constructability review is not a style critique. Its purpose is to eliminate impossible or impractical construction requirements and to verify access, utilities, site constraints, work space, durations, QA/QC requirements, outage procedures, available construction methods, public impacts, staging, storage, and environmental mitigation. The handbook places constructability reviews before completion of final design and preferably shortly after 60 percent design.

The source handbook distinguishes design/construction risks, such as weather, contractor inability, unforeseen site conditions, and permitting delays, from financial risks, such as revenue shortfalls, project cash demand changes, and interest-rate changes. Communication control means recording decisions, comments, changed assumptions, and accepted or rejected review comments so scope, budget, and schedule remain traceable.

## Philippine contract context

UAP Document 401-402 ties the architect service agreement to schematic design, design development, contract documents, construction drawings, plans, designs, specifications, bidding assistance, change orders, shop-drawing review, site visits, payment certificates, and substantial/final completion inspections. Use that contract context together with project-management controls: the firm manages deliverables, decisions, and coordination so the professional service can remain within the agreed scope and documented changes.
