# Al-Andalus (Islamic Spain) Architecture

## LEA Point
Al-Andalus architecture is the Islamic architecture of Spain and the western Mediterranean. For the LEA, read it through Cordoba and Granada: hypostyle mosque space, horseshoe arches, reused/adapted classical elements, intimate palace courts, water, stucco, tile, and refined surface geometry.

## Source Anchors
The Great Mosque of Cordoba is the safest mosque anchor. The source map supports Cordoba as an Andalusian monument where repeated arches, columned prayer space, and a reused sacred urban setting matter more than later palace vocabulary.

The Alhambra is the safest palace anchor. UNESCO and official Alhambra sources support it as a Nasrid palace-city and garden ensemble in Granada. Use it for court sequences, water, gardens, slender supports, carved plaster/stucco, tile surfaces, inscriptions, and controlled movement from public to private space.

Fletcher and the local HOA module support the exam contrast: Moorish or Andalusian architecture combines Islamic building needs with local Spanish, North African, and classical resources. The answer is cultural adaptation, not simple importation from Damascus, Baghdad, or Istanbul.

## Exam Pattern
If the item mentions Cordoba, a column forest, red-and-white arches, a hypostyle hall, or a horseshoe arch, answer Great Mosque of Cordoba or Andalusian mosque architecture.

If the item mentions Granada, Nasrid palaces, Court of the Lions, Court of Myrtles, water channels, stucco, tile, and poetic interior courts, answer Alhambra or Al-Andalus palace architecture.

## Guardrails
Do not make every Islamic building in Spain a mosque. The Alhambra is tested primarily as a palace and court sequence, even though it sits within a fortified complex.

Do not treat water as only climate control. In the source-safe exam pattern, water also organizes movement, reflection, sound, and court identity.

## Real World Scenario
On an image item, a red-and-white hypostyle arch field points to Cordoba. A narrow palace court with water, light, plaster, tile, and delicate columns points to the Alhambra and Nasrid Granada.
