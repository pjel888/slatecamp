## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal

Identify the key characteristics of Indus Valley civilization architecture — grid-plan cities, standardized brick sizes, advanced drainage systems, the Great Bath of Mohenjo-Daro — and explain how urban planning and water management reveal sophisticated civic organization.
## Core Concept

The Indus Valley Civilization flourished c. 2600–1900 BCE across the Indus River valley (modern Pakistan and northwestern India). This culture is remarkable for its sophistication, planning discipline, and mysterious decline. Unlike Mesopotamian and Egyptian civilizations with visible monumental architecture and royal tombs, the Indus civilization built cities of standardized proportions, absence of hierarchical display, and unknown spiritual beliefs. Written script survives but remains undeciphered, limiting our understanding of governance, religion, and purpose.

**Urban Planning and Grid Layout:** The signature Indus achievement was rational city planning. **Mohenjo-daro** (meaning "Mound of the Dead"), the largest known Indus site, covered approximately 250 acres (100 hectares) and housed 35,000–41,250 people. The city was divided into distinct zones: the citadel (raised platform), lower town (residential and commercial areas), and specialized districts (possibly craft areas). Streets were laid out on a grid pattern, with main streets running north-south and east-west at regular intervals. This orthogonal planning resembles much later Hellenistic cities (designed by **Hippodamus of Miletus** in the 5th century BCE), suggesting independent discovery of rational urban organization.

**Standardized Brick:** Indus builders employed standardized mud brick at a ratio of 1:2:4 (height to width to length). A typical brick measured approximately 7 × 14 × 28 cm (some sources cite 6.5 × 13 × 26 cm for earlier buildings). This standardization allowed rapid construction, easy replacement of damaged units, and visual consistency. Bricks were laid in regular patterns—often a "running bond" (alternating orientation)—creating stable walls and uniform appearance. No monumental stone temples or palaces are evident, suggesting either religious ideology that eschewed monumental display or a theocratic system without kingship requiring display tombs.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Note" status="preserved"]

**Domestic Architecture:** Indus houses were largely uniform in scale and plan. Individual dwellings typically featured an internal courtyard (likely for shade and water collection in a hot climate), rooms arranged around the courtyard, and flat roofs. Larger houses (likely merchant or administrator residences) featured multiple courtyards and additional rooms, but even these lacked the ostentatious display of contemporary Near Eastern palaces. This suggests either strong central planning (preventing inequality) or an economy and society with limited hierarchical distinction.

**Public Structures:** The **Great Bath** at Mohenjo-daro is the most famous Indus public building. Measuring approximately 40 × 23 feet (12 × 7 metres) and sunk 8 feet (2.4 metres) below ground level, it featured brick stairs descending on all sides and a sophisticated drainage system with an overflow outlet. The bath was waterproofed with bitumen. Its ritual or ceremonial purpose remains debated—possibly for ablution (religious purification), public bathing, or cold-water immersion therapy. The sophistication of its drainage system is remarkable: Indus architects engineered gradients allowing water to flow to central drains without stagnation.

**Drainage and Sanitation:** Indus cities featured the most advanced sanitation systems of the ancient world. Every house, apparently, had a latrine. Waste ran through covered drains under streets, connecting to larger drain channels leading outside the city. This hygiene infrastructure suggests either exceptionally sophisticated understanding of disease prevention or a cultural/religious emphasis on cleanliness. Compare this to contemporary cities: Mesopotamian cities lacked comparable drainage; Classical Greek cities did not standardize sewage systems. Indus civilization's sanitation predates modern understanding of germ theory yet achieved superior public health outcomes.

**The Citadel:** Raised platforms (likely defensive) crowned each major Indus city. At Mohenjo-daro, the citadel contained larger structures, possibly administrative or religious buildings. **Granaries** have been identified—large structures with ventilation systems, suited for grain storage. This infrastructure suggests centralized economic control or redistribution systems, though without written records, governance remains speculative.

**Building Materials and Aesthetics:** Indus builders worked with mud brick, stone (less commonly), and timber (wood structural elements have not survived). Unlike Egyptian monumental stone or Mesopotamian relief decoration, Indus architecture was utilitarian and undecorated. Wall surfaces were plain, save for mortar lines. This aesthetic restraint—or lack of monumental tradition—distinguishes Indus architecture from contemporary civilizations and suggests different values: pragmatism, order, and functionality rather than divine display or royal monument.

**Mysterious Decline:** Around 1900 BCE, Indus civilization declined. Evidence suggests environmental factors (possibly Indus River course change, reducing agricultural productivity), climate change (decreased monsoon rainfall), or earthquake. Invasion by Indo-European peoples (Aryans) is sometimes proposed but unproven. No evidence of warfare, weaponry, or conquered cities has been found. The civilization appears to have gradually abandoned, with population migration to the Ganges valley or other regions. This peaceful decline, rare in ancient history, adds to Indus civilization's uniqueness.

## Key Terms

**Grid layout (or orthogonal plan):** A city plan with streets intersecting at right angles, creating regular city blocks; characteristic of Indus cities and later Hellenistic cities.

**Standardized brick:** Mud brick following a consistent size and ratio, facilitating rapid construction and replacement; Indus bricks followed a 1:2:4 proportion.

**Citadel:** A raised platform or fortified area within a city, typically containing important structures (administrative, religious, or defensive); each major Indus city featured one.

**Internal courtyard:** An open space surrounded by a house's rooms, providing shade, water collection, and ventilation; standard in Indus domestic architecture.

**The Great Bath:** A sunken public structure at Mohenjo-daro with stepped descent, bitumen waterproofing, and sophisticated drainage; purpose debated (ritual purification, bathing, or medical use).

**Bitumen:** A naturally occurring asphalt-like material used for waterproofing; the Indus Valley Civilization obtained it from natural seeps or traded for it, applying it to seal the Great Bath.

**Terracotta pipe:** Fired clay tubes used in Indus drainage systems; allows water flow while preventing surface contamination.

**Granary:** A large ventilated storage structure for grain; evidence of centralized food storage and economic redistribution or control.

**Latrine:** An indoor toilet or waste-disposal facility; virtually all Indus houses featured one, connected to covered drainage systems.

**Ablution:** Ritual washing or purification, particularly in religious contexts; possibly the purpose of the Great Bath.
