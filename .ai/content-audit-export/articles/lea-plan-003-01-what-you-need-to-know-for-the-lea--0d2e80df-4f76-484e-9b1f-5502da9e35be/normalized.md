## What You Need to Know for the LEA

Philippine housing law operates through a layered system of buyer protection, developer regulation, and socialized housing standards. Five core laws establish the framework: PD 957 controls market-rate subdivisions and condominiums; BP 220 sets minimum area standards for economic and socialized housing; RA 7279 (UDHA) mandates eviction protections and balanced housing; RA 6552 (Maceda Law) provides installment buyer safeguards; and RA 10884 quantifies the balanced housing percentage. Understanding which law governs which housing type and which provision belongs to which law is the foundation of housing law competency.

## Learning Goal

Distinguish the five core Philippine housing laws (PD 957, BP 220, RA 7279, RA 6552, RA 10884) by their scope, key provisions, and housing type coverage, and apply eviction protection rules and balanced housing requirements to given scenarios.

## Core Concept

PD 957 is exclusively about **market-rate residential projects** — subdivisions and condominiums sold to individual buyers. It does not govern socialized housing, informal settlements, or government-built projects.

**Developer Registration and Licensing**
Before selling even a single lot or condominium unit, a developer must register with DHSUD (Department of Human Settlements and Urban Development, formerly HLURB). This registration is mandatory and non-negotiable. The developer must prove financial capacity, provide proof of land ownership or right to develop, and submit a detailed project description. Registration protects buyers by ensuring an accountable entity stands behind the project.

**30% Open Space Requirement**
Every residential subdivision developed under PD 957 must allocate a minimum of 30% of the gross area as open space. This open space includes parks, playgrounds, community facilities, roads, and other non-buildable land. The requirement balances density with livability — the developer cannot use the entire parcel for revenue-generating lots. This is reinforced by PD 1216 (Open Space Requirement in Residential Subdivisions).

**Title Delivery and Developer Accountability**
The developer is obligated to deliver the Certificate of Title (TCT) to the buyer once full payment is received. The developer cannot withhold the title as leverage or security. Additionally, the developer is liable to complete all promised amenities and facilities — if the project brochure promises a swimming pool, clubhouse, or gym, these must be built. The developer also cannot mortgage or pledge the buyer's lot without written consent after the purchase contract is signed.

**What PD 957 Does NOT Cover**
PD 957 is silent on socialized housing (BP 220 governs), raw land subdivision without residential use, eviction procedures, or government-built housing. It is a buyer-protection and developer-regulation law, not a social housing mandate.

### BP 220: Economic and Socialized Housing (1982)

BP 220 establishes the physical design standards that make affordable housing genuinely affordable. Unlike PD 957, which regulates market transactions, BP 220 prescribes the exact lot sizes and floor areas below which housing cannot legally be called "socialized" or "economic."

**Socialized Housing Standards**

Socialized housing serves families earning below **₱12,000** per month (current DHSUD threshold). BP 220 distinguishes two scenarios:

1. **New Subdivision (Greenfield Development)**
   - Minimum lot area: **72 square meters**
   - Minimum floor area: **22 square meters**

   These tight standards reflect the scarcity of land near employment centers. A 72 sq.m. lot in an organized subdivision with utilities and services is considered more livable than informal settlers' improvised shelters on smaller irregular lots.

2. **In-Fill Site (Existing Urban Area)**
   - Minimum lot area: **28 square meters**
   - Minimum floor area: **22 square meters**

   In-fill developments are permitted to go smaller than greenfield because they leverage existing neighborhood infrastructure and services. A 28 sq.m. lot with adjacent schools, markets, and transport is more viable than an isolated 28 sq.m. parcel.

**Economic Housing Standards**

Economic housing serves families earning **₱12,001–₱45,000** per month (current DHSUD bands).

- Minimum lot area: **100 square meters**
- Minimum floor area: **42 square meters**

These larger standards allow for better livability — more space for family functions, room for a small yard or storage, and reduced density that permits better circulation and services.

**Road Standards Under BP 220**

Local roads in socialized housing subdivisions must have a minimum right-of-way (ROW) of **6 meters**. This is significantly narrower than the standard DPWH local road width of 10 meters. The reduction is intentional — narrower roads reduce land lost to circulation and lower development costs, which translates to lower housing costs for socialized buyers. The tradeoff is accepted because socialized housing values affordability over spaciousness.

**BP 220 Is NOT PD 957**

This is the most critical distinction in housing law testing. PD 957 does not specify minimum lot or floor areas for any housing type. BP 220 specifies them for socialized and economic only. If a question presents a lot area standard, ask: "Which law mentions lot areas?" The answer is BP 220, not PD 957.

### RA 7279: Urban Development and Housing Act (UDHA) (1992)

RA 7279 reframes housing from a market commodity to a human right. It mandates balanced housing, protects evictees, and defines the role of government in ensuring housing access.

**Balanced Housing Requirement**

Developers of open-market and medium-cost housing projects must produce socialized housing equivalent to the market housing. The requirement ensures that every luxury subdivision or mid-range residential project generates affordable units. Options include:

- On-site development: Build socialized units within the same project
- Off-site development: Develop an equivalent project elsewhere
- Joint venture with NHA: Partner with the National Housing Authority
- Bond contribution: Pay a contribution to NHA if on-site or off-site are infeasible

RA 10884 (2016) later quantified this as **15–20% of project cost**, giving developers a specific target.

**Eviction Protection — The Three-Requirement Rule**

The most tested provision of RA 7279 is its eviction protection. An eviction of informal settlers is lawful ONLY if ALL three conditions are met simultaneously:

1. **30-day written notice** to occupants (not a verbal order, not posted on a tree — formal written notice delivered to the occupant)
2. **Court order** (specifically a Writ of Demolition from the Regional Trial Court; administrative demolition is illegal)
3. **Certified adequate resettlement site** available and accessible to the occupants BEFORE eviction occurs

All three must be satisfied. Missing even one renders the eviction illegal and subject to restraining orders and habeas corpus petitions.

**Danger Areas — Where Relocation May Be Waived**

RA 7279 designates certain areas as hazardous, where occupants may be evicted without waiting for resettlement:

- Within **3 meters** of esteros (urban drainage canals); **20m/40m** of natural waterways (per PD 1067)
- Within **50 meters** of active railroad tracks
- Under bridges or overhead structures
- Within power line easements
- Within active earthquake fault zones (per PHIVOLCS mapping)

Even in danger areas, the 30-day notice is still required. What changes is that the government is not required to provide permanent resettlement before clearing the danger zone — immediate relocation to safety is permitted. But the notice period is not waived.

**Adequate Resettlement Standards**

When resettlement is required (outside danger areas), the relocation site must meet these criteria:

- Accessible to livelihood opportunities (not in an isolated rural zone if the occupant's work is urban)
- Near schools and basic health services
- Water, electricity, and sanitation services available
- Minimum floor area: **22 square meters** per housing unit (same as BP 220 socialized housing), reflecting the understanding that resettled families deserve slightly better standards to compensate for displacement.

### RA 6552: Realty Installment Buyer Protection Act (Maceda Law) (1972)

The Maceda Law protects buyers who finance real property purchases from the developer (seller-financing), not from banks. It is named after Senator Ernesto Maceda.

**Refund and Grace Period Based on Years Paid**

The law creates a sharp threshold at 2 years:

| Years of Installments Paid | Buyer's Rights Upon Cancellation |
|---------------------------|----------------------------------|
| Less than 2 years | 60-day grace period from date of last installment; NO refund of payments |
| 2 years or more | **50%** of total payments refunded + 5% per additional year **above 5 years** (capped at 90% refund)

Example: A buyer who paid for 3.5 years is entitled to a 50% refund. A buyer who paid for 7 years is entitled to 50% + (5% × 2 years) = 60% refund.

**Grace Period Rule**

Separately, the buyer receives a grace period of **1 month for every year of installments paid**. If a buyer paid for 4 years, they receive 4 months of grace after missing a payment before the seller can cancel. This grace period allows the buyer to catch up on overdue payments without losing their entire investment.

**Coverage and Exclusions**

The Maceda Law covers residential real property on installment — subdivision lots and condominium units sold on installment by the developer. It does NOT cover industrial property, commercial lots (offices, shops), or housing financed by banks. Bank-financed housing is governed by the General Banking Law and credit agreements, not the Maceda Law.

### RA 10884: Balanced Housing Development Program (2016)

RA 10884 updated RA 7279's balanced housing requirement by specifying percentages:

Developers of open-market housing projects must produce socialized housing equivalent to **15–20% of the project cost**. This quantifies the social obligation — it is not vague, it is not discretionary, and it is enforced through the DHSUD licensing system (developers who do not meet the requirement cannot obtain licenses for new projects).

---

### PD 1216: Open Space Requirement in Residential Subdivisions (1977)

PD 1216 reinforces PD 957's 30% open space rule. It mandates that 30% of the gross area must be allocated to non-buildable uses: parks, playgrounds, community facilities, and roads. The 30% cannot be shrunk by claiming that roads are separate; roads count toward the 30%.
