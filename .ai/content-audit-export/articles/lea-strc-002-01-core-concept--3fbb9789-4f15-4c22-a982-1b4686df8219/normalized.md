## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal
By the end of this article, you will be able to:

- Define a bearing wall system and explain its load path
- Identify which structural elements resist gravity loads and which resist lateral loads in a bearing wall system
- Describe the height and slenderness limitations of bearing wall construction
- Compare bearing wall systems to moment frames and shear wall systems

Structural systems classify buildings into two primary categories: bearing wall systems (where walls carry gravity loads) and frame systems (where skeletal frameworks carry loads). This distinction determines load paths, member sizing, and lateral bracing strategies.

## Core Concept

A **bearing wall system** relies on walls to resist both gravity and lateral loads. The walls transfer vertical loads directly to the foundation through compression, while also functioning as shear walls for lateral resistance. Bearing walls in masonry or reinforced concrete are common in low-to-mid-rise construction because they are economical and inherently stiff.

A **frame system** uses a skeletal arrangement of columns and beams connected by rigid or pinned joints. The frame carries gravity loads through bending in the beams and axial compression in the columns. Lateral loads are resisted through frame bending (moment-resisting frames) or by separate shear walls and diaphragms. Frame systems allow more open floor plans and are preferred for mid-to-high-rise buildings.

**Key distinction:** In bearing walls, the gravity and lateral load paths are integrated into the same structural element. In frames, they are often separated—gravity is handled by the frame skeleton, and lateral is handled by shear walls or frame rigidity.

The NSCP Section 202 defines these system types to guide member design, component selection, and response modification factors for seismic analysis. Choice of system affects not only structural efficiency but also constructability, fire rating, and architectural flexibility.

## Key Provisions

| Aspect | Bearing Wall System | Frame System |
|--------|-------------------|--------------|
| **Primary load path** | Walls carry gravity directly to foundation | Columns/beams distribute loads to foundation |
| **Lateral resistance** | Walls act as shear walls | Shear walls, diaphragms, or frame moment resistance |
| **Typical materials** | Masonry, reinforced concrete, light-gauge steel studs | Steel, reinforced concrete, wood |
| **Typical height range** | 1–6 stories (economical) | 5+ stories (more efficient at height) |
| **Architectural flexibility** | Limited (walls are structural) | High (columns can be spaced widely) |
| **Seismic response** | Stiffer, lower displacement | More flexible, higher displacement if not braced |
| **Cost typical applications** | Residential, low-rise commercial | Commercial, office, tall buildings |

## Philippine Practice Context

Bearing wall systems dominate residential and mixed-use construction in the Philippines due to material availability, labor skills, and lower initial cost. Concrete shear walls are standard for multi-unit residential blocks. Frame systems (steel or RC moment-resisting frames) are used in commercial office towers and buildings in Seismic Zone 2, where R factors reward rigid lateral systems.

## How It's Tested

**Remember-level:** Identify whether a given structural system is bearing wall or frame based on a building description.

**Understand-level:** Explain why a bearing wall system is economical for low-rise buildings but frame systems are preferred for mid-to-high-rise.

**Apply-level:** Given a building in Seismic Zone 2 with 10 stories, justify why a frame system with moment-resisting capability would be chosen over a bearing wall approach.
