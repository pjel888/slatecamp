# MEPF Coordination With Architecture

## Architect's Coordination Duty

RA 9266 IRR frames the architect as professionally responsible for total design and describes practice as the orderly coordination of processes needed for a complete building. Its scope includes schematic design, design development, contract documents, construction phases, and the architectural layouting of mechanical, electrical, electronic, sanitary, plumbing, communications, and other utility systems.

## Three-Phase Framework

Use three coordination moments. In pre-design and schematic work, reserve zones for service entrance, transformer or switchgear rooms, vertical risers, shafts, equipment rooms, plumbing stacks, ducts, sprinklers, lighting, and telecommunications. In design development and contract documents, coordinate the architectural drawings with structural, electrical, plumbing and sanitary, mechanical and air-conditioning, civil work plans, specifications, and bid documents. During construction, use shop drawing review, regular coordination meetings, inspections, and records to resolve conflicts before installation.

## Space And Access Implications

Utility planning has physical consequences. Electrical service starts at service conductors and service equipment; large buildings may need transformer vaults, switchgear rooms, main switchboards, feeders, panelboards, and riser diagrams. Telephone systems need service entrances, main terminal cabinets, riser conduits or shafts, and central locations relative to served floor areas. These are architectural space-planning issues as well as engineering drawings.

## LEA Takeaway

For exam scenarios, the architect does not replace the engineer. The architect coordinates the building-wide spatial fit and documentation of MEPF systems so that specialty work, code compliance, and construction sequencing remain aligned.
