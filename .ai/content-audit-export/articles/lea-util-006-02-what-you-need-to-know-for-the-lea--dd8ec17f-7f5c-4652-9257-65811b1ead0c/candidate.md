## What You Need to Know for the LEA

Reverberation is the prolongation of sound in an enclosed space after the source has stopped. RT60 is the time required for the sound level to drop by 60 dB. In architectural acoustics, this is the quick test for whether a room is too live for speech, too dry for music, or appropriately controlled for its use.

The key relationship is simple: reverberation time depends on room volume and total absorption. If volume increases while absorption stays the same, sound takes longer to die out. If total absorption increases while volume stays the same, sound dies out faster.

The handout gives the Sabine form as RT60 = 0.049 V / a when volume is in cubic feet and total absorption is in sabins. Total room absorption is assembled from surface area times absorption coefficient or NRC: a = sum of S times alpha. This means the LEA answer should not use area alone; the material coefficient matters.

Material choice changes the acoustic result. Fabric, carpet, fiberboard, and acoustic board are listed as highly absorptive. Concrete, masonry, brick, wood, and metals are listed as highly reflective. Absorptive wall panels, ceiling clouds, baffles, and related acoustic materials reduce reverberation and echoes, which improves speech intelligibility in rooms such as offices, classrooms, and conference spaces.

For exam calculations, keep units consistent and solve for the unknown. If the target RT60 is given, rearrange the formula to find required total absorption. If existing absorption is known, subtract it from the required total to get the added absorption. If a surface material is proposed, multiply its area by its coefficient before comparing options.

## LEA Memory Anchors

- RT60 is the 60 dB decay time.
- RT60 rises with room volume and falls with total absorption.
- Sabine in the checked handout uses RT60 = 0.049 V / a for cubic feet.
- Sabins come from surface area times absorption coefficient or NRC.
- Absorptive finishes control reverberation; reflective finishes sustain it.
