## Learning Goal

Identify expansion joint types and locations in buildings — structural, thermal, seismic, construction joints — and explain how each joint type accommodates movement while maintaining water-tightness, fire rating, and structural integrity.
## Core Concept

Window operations are classified by movement type and operator mechanism. Fixed windows do not open and provide no ventilation. Sliding windows (horizontal or vertical) use tracks and wheels, requiring minimal operator effort but prone to binding if tracks are dirty. Casement windows swing outward on hinges (typically one or two) with a crank operator on the interior, providing full opening and unobstructed ventilation. Awning windows hinge at the top and swing outward at the bottom, automatically shedding rain and allowing ventilation during light rainfall. Hopper windows hinge at the bottom and swing inward, typically used in basements. Pivot windows rotate on pivots at the top and bottom, ideal for large or frameless glass panels. Double-hung windows have two vertically sliding sashes balanced by counterweights (historic) or spring mechanisms (modern).

Casement operators use a mechanical crank or electric motor to convert rotational input into linear motion that opens the sash. The crank arm is typically 10 to 12 inches long, requiring approximately 15 lbf of force to operate. Awning operators function similarly but must support the weight of the sash at any opening angle. Friction stays are mechanical hinges that maintain position at any angle without additional hardware; friction is temperature-sensitive and may weaken in hot climates.

Window locks and latches secure sashes in the closed position. Standard sash locks (or sash fasteners) are friction devices that allow slight racking but are adequate for most residential applications. Keyed locks prevent unauthorized opening and are required in commercial buildings. Espagnolette bolts (or multipoint locks) engage at the top and bottom of the sash, distributing load and preventing sash separation during high winds.

Weatherstripping seals the gap between sash and frame, reducing air leakage and water infiltration. Common materials include vinyl (flexible, durable), rubber (excellent compression), felt (economical but less durable), and silicone (high-performance but expensive). AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440 (NAFS) standards specify air leakage limits; Gold Label windows are limited to 0.30 cfm (cubic feet per minute) at 25 mph wind speed.

AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440 (NAFS) establishes performance grades based on structural strength and water resistance. Structural Resistance is measured by positive and negative wind pressure testing; Gold Label windows resist at least 94 mph wind pressure. Water Resistance is measured by resistance to water infiltration at 32 mph; windows must pass without water entering the building interior.

## Philippine Construction Context

The Philippines experiences wind speeds exceeding 150 mph during Category 4-5 typhoons, particularly in coastal and northern regions. AAMA Gold Label windows (94 mph rating) are insufficient for high-wind zones; upgraded specifications or storm-resistant glazing and framing are necessary. Window hardware corrosion in salt spray and high humidity requires stainless steel or coated aluminum; standard painted steel corrodes rapidly. Casement and awning windows are preferable to sliding windows in the Philippines due to better rain shedding and easier cleaning from the interior. Friction stays are problematic in hot climates; in some cases, they weaken under sustained heat and fail to hold the window open. Weatherstripping durability is critical; vinyl and rubber deteriorate faster in tropical heat than in temperate climates and may require replacement every 5-7 years instead of 10-15 years. Many residential projects omit weatherstripping to reduce cost, leading to high air-conditioning loads and water infiltration during monsoons.

## How It's Tested

**Exam Pattern 1: Window Type Selection.** A residential project in a coastal typhoon zone requires windows that automatically shed rain and allow ventilation. What window type is most appropriate? Answer: Awning windows (hinge at top, swing outward at bottom, shedding rain).

**Exam Pattern 2: AAMA Rating Application.** A commercial building in Manila specifies standard (non-Gold Label) AAMA windows with 0.45 cfm air leakage at 25 mph. Is this adequate for a typhoon-prone region? Answer: No; air leakage of 0.45 cfm exceeds AAMA Gold Label limits (0.30 cfm), and structural resistance is lower than required for high-wind areas.

**Exam Pattern 3: Friction Stay Temperature Effect.** A friction stay has a nominal strength of 50 lbf at 70°F. With a temperature coefficient of -0.75 percent per °F, what is the holding force at 95°F? Answer: 50 lbf × (1 - 0.0075 × 25°F) = 50 × 0.8125 = 40.6 lbf (approximately 19 percent loss).

**Exam Pattern 4: Weatherstripping Specification.** A window assembly leaks air at 0.50 cfm. After installing high-quality weatherstripping that reduces air leakage by 40 percent, what is the new air leakage rate? Answer: 0.50 × (1 - 0.40) = 0.30 cfm (Gold Label compliant).
