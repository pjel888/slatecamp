## What You Need to Know for the LEA

Physical and aesthetic considerations are the starting point for site planning. Before you design, manage, or maintain a site, you must understand the terrain, its visual qualities, and how sun, wind, and existing views shape what's possible. This article focuses on reading the land itself—not on design choices, which belong to other articles.

## Learning Goal

Analyze a site's physical conditions—topography, sun orientation, wind patterns, views, soil constraints, and existing utilities—to determine development opportunities and constraints before design begins.

### Reading the Terrain: Topography and Contours

**The Critical Rule You Cannot Reverse**

Extracted summary:

- Text: Closely spaced contour lines = **STEEP** terrain. Widely spaced contour lines = **FLAT** or gently sloping terrain. This is one of the most-tested distinctions in site planning. Reversing this rule = automatic wrong answer.
[/SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK]

When contour lines crowd together, the elevation changes rapidly over a short distance—that is steepness. When they are far apart, elevation changes slowly—that is flatness.

**Reading Slope Percentage**

Slope is often expressed as a percentage or a ratio (rise:run). A 1:4 slope means the land rises 1 meter for every 4 meters of horizontal distance. A steeper slope might be 1:2. The steeper the slope, the more difficult it is to build on, and the more site preparation (cutting, filling, terracing) will be required.

For site planning:
- **0–5% slope:** Nearly flat; minimal site work
- **5–15% slope:** Moderate; standard building techniques work
- **15–25% slope:** Steep; retaining walls, terracing common
- **>25% slope:** Very steep; specialized design, expensive to develop

### Sun Orientation and Shadows

The sun's path across the site changes with latitude and season. In the Philippines:
- Sun rises in the east, sets in the west
- Sun is highest in the sky at solar noon (12:30 PM, accounting for daylight saving)
- In summer (June), the sun is higher; in winter (December), it is lower

**What Planners Analyze**

1. **Shadow patterns at different times of year** — morning shadows are longer and point west; afternoon shadows are shorter and point east
2. **East–west building orientation** — long facades facing east or west will have intense morning or afternoon sun exposure
3. **North–south orientation** — more favorable in tropical climates; allows cross-ventilation and shade
4. **Overhang design** — horizontal overhangs block summer sun while allowing winter sun penetration (in cooler climates; in the Philippines, year-round shading is often preferred)

In the Philippine context, strong tropical sun and high humidity make shade and ventilation critical. Sites exposed to intense afternoon sun (west-facing) will be hot unless shaded or elevated for air flow.

### Wind Patterns and Ventilation

Wind direction and speed affect comfort, natural ventilation, and building energy use.

**Prevailing Winds in the Philippines**
- **Northeast Monsoon (amihan, October–April):** Cool, relatively dry
- **Southwest Monsoon (habagat, May–September):** Warm, moisture-laden, brings rain

**What to Analyze on Site**

1. **Existing wind patterns** — topography, vegetation, and neighboring buildings channel or block wind
2. **Natural ventilation paths** — gaps and corridors allow breezes through; buildings should allow cross-flow
3. **Wind hazard zones** — typhoon-prone areas require wind-resistant design and orientations
4. **Pedestrian comfort** — wind at street level can be uncomfortable; high winds near tall buildings (wind tunnel effect) require mitigation

Tropical sites benefit from northeast winds during the dry season. Buildings should be oriented to capture prevailing breezes while blocking harsh afternoon sun.

### Views and Visual Quality

The site's visual setting includes what is _seen from_ the site and what is _seen on_ the site.

**Views From the Site**
- Landmarks, water bodies, mountains, skylines visible from the site
- These views are assets to be preserved or framed in development
- Tall buildings, walls, or vegetation may obstruct views

**Views On the Site**
- How the site appears from the street, from neighboring buildings, from approaching paths
- Building facades, materials, street trees, signage all contribute
- Visual continuity with the neighborhood

**Visual Hierarchy and Focal Points**
- Key buildings or landscape features should be visually prominent
- Secondary buildings should not compete visually with primary structures
- Monotonous streetscapes (all same height, material, fenestration) feel dull; variety maintains visual interest

### Height-to-Width Ratios and Enclosure

The ratio of building height to street width (H:W ratio) affects how enclosed or open the street feels.

**Key Ratios**

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0002 component="Note" status="preserved"]
Extracted summary:
- Props: type="warn"
- Text: **H:W = 1:2 is considered comfortable enclosure.** The building is half as tall as the street is wide. This feels human-scale and welcoming. **H:W = 2:1 (building twice as tall as street is wide) is oppressive—canyon-like and uncomfortable.**
[/SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK]

Ratios explained:
- **1:4** — Very open, exposed feeling; feels like a parking lot
- **1:2** — Comfortable; plaza-like
- **1:1** — Still comfortable but more defined
- **2:1** — Oppressive; feels like an urban canyon
- **3:1 or higher** — Very oppressive; minimal sky visible

The H:W ratio is one of the most tested distinctions in urban design. Always remember: **1:2 is ideal; 2:1 is oppressive.**

### Soils and Foundation Constraints

Soil type, bearing capacity, and stability affect building feasibility and cost.

**What Planners Note**

1. **Soil classification** — clay, silt, sand, or mixed; affects drainage and compaction
2. **Bearing capacity** — how much weight the soil can support per unit area; measured in kPa (kilopascals)
3. **Subsidence risk** — in areas with soft clay or peat, the ground may settle over time
4. **Liquefaction risk** — near coast or low-lying areas with high water table; loose saturated sand may lose strength in earthquakes
5. **Excavation difficulty** — rock layers or groundwater table depth affect digging costs

In the Philippines, coastal sites and Metro Manila have high water tables and soft clay layers. Sites near fault lines or in mountainous areas may have rocky soils and complex geotechnics.

### Existing Utilities and Infrastructure

The site's connection (or lack thereof) to water, power, sewage, and telecom affects design and cost.

**What to Assess**
- Proximity to main water lines and their capacity
- Proximity to electrical substations and grid capacity
- Distance to sewage treatment plants or septic tank feasibility
- Road access and utility trenching costs
- Underground vs. overhead lines

Vacant or remote sites often require lengthy utility runs, increasing development costs.
