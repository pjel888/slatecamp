# Postmodern Architecture

## LEA Point
Postmodern Architecture challenged the universal, austere language of mid-20th-century Modernism by reintroducing history, ornament, color, symbolism, and popular imagery. For LEA recall, the safe cue is not "anything historical"; it is self-conscious reference, irony, contradiction, and communicative form after Modernism.

## Source Anchors
- Michael Graves' Portland Building is source-safe as a 1982 Portland municipal office building and an award-winning example of Postmodern architecture.
- Robert Venturi's Vanna Venturi House is source-safe as an early Post Modern house whose simplified domestic front, oversized elements, and deliberate contradictions challenge modernist purity.
- Philip Johnson and John Burgee's AT&T Corporate Headquarters, now 550 Madison Avenue, is source-safe as a postmodern skyscraper with a broken-pediment crown.
- Charles Moore's Piazza d'Italia is source-safe as a Postmodernist public plaza using theatrical Italian references and urban symbolism.

## Exam Pattern
Postmodernism questions usually test the difference between earnest 19th-century eclecticism and late-20th-century quotation. The correct answer usually names Robert Venturi for "Less is a bore," Michael Graves for the Portland Building, Johnson/Burgee for AT&T/550 Madison, or Charles Moore for Piazza d'Italia.

## Guardrails
Do not say Postmodernism rejects all historical reference. Do not flatten it into simple revivalism. Keep it distinct from Critical Regionalism: Postmodernism often uses historical or popular references as signs, while Critical Regionalism works through place, climate, materials, and cultural memory.

## Real World Scenario
When reviewing a civic facade that pairs a modern office tower with an exaggerated classical pediment or bright applied ornament, identify the exam-safe logic as Postmodern historical quotation and irony, not International Style functionalism or Deconstructivist fragmentation.

## Related Articles
Postmodernism is a bridge topic for International Style critique, Deconstructivism, Critical Regionalism, and the Pritzker-era shift toward broader architectural values.
