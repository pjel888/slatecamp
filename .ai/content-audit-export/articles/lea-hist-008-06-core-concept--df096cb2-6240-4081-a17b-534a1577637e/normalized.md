Identify the defining characteristics of Postmodernism — historical reference, ornament, color, irony, contextualism, 'less is a bore' — and recognize key architects (Venturi, Graves, Moore, Stirling, Gehry) and works (Vanna Venturi House, Piazza d'Italia, AT&T Building).
## Core Concept
**Postmodernism** exploded onto architecture in the 1970s–80s as a deliberate repudiation of modernism's dogma. Where modernism pursued reduction, universal principles, and "form follows function," Postmodernism embraced contradiction, historical reference, applied color, and irony. **Robert Venturi's** slogan "Less is a bore" became the movement's rallying cry against modernist austerity.

Postmodernism retrieved architectural history after decades of modernist amnesia. Buildings quoted Renaissance pediments, Art Deco motifs, Neoclassical columns—but in exaggerated, mismatched, or parodic ways. The **Portland Building** (1982, **Michael Graves**, Portland) is the American apotheosis: a small municipal building clad in colorful glazed brick with oversized geometric ornament, a monstrous stylized face, and satirical references to classical and kitsch aesthetics. It announced that architecture need not apologize for color, ornament, historical reference, or popular culture.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Note" status="preserved"]

**Philip Johnson and John Burgee's AT&T Building** (1984, New York) took a different path, inserting a massive Chippendlea-inspired broken pediment atop a sleek glass tower—combining modernist rationality with Rococo whimsy. The building rejected the notion that a skyscraper must be a neutral box. Context, place, and historical consciousness mattered.

The movement's philosophy held that modernism had become dogmatic and inhuman, denying architects and clients the richness of historical memory and the pleasure of ornament and color. Postmodernism reclaimed architecture's capacity to mean, to reference, to communicate beyond pure function. This did not mean returning to traditional eclecticism; instead, Postmodernists mixed codes deliberately, generating unexpected and often humorous juxtapositions.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0004 component="Warn" status="preserved"]

Postmodernism also democratized architectural reference. Modernism's elitism—its claim to universal, rational principles accessible only to enlightened designers—gave way to Postmodern populism. Vernacular architecture, commercial strip aesthetics, and mass-media imagery entered the canon. **Venturi's Vanna Venturi House** (1964, Philadelphia) is modest, asymmetrical, and explicitly references a child's drawing of a house—making the banal and provincial suddenly architectural.

By the 1990s, Postmodernism's playfulness had exhausted itself in the eyes of many critics, and its buildings aged awkwardly. Yet its insistence on meaning, memory, context, and the death of universal aesthetic principles proved permanent. It opened architecture to pluralism and irony that Deconstructivism and parametricism would inherit.

## Key Terms
- **Postmodernism**: Architectural movement (1970s–80s) rejecting modernism's universalism in favor of historical reference, ornament, color, irony, and contextual meaning.
- **Historicism**: The use of historical architectural forms, proportions, or references in contemporary design; central to Postmodern practice.
- **Applied ornament**: Decoration applied to the surface of a building (paint, color, relief) rather than integral to structural or material expression.
- **Irony**: The use of contradiction between form and function or between quotation and context to generate meaning; a Postmodern strategy.
- **Pastiche**: The imitation or quotation of historical styles or codes, often in mixed or exaggerated combination; characteristic of Postmodern aesthetics.
- **Contextual design**: Architecture responding to and respecting the existing urban or landscape context, including historical memory.
- **Populism**: The inclusion of vernacular, commercial, or mass-culture imagery in architecture, elevating the banal to the canonical.
- **Pediment**: A triangular gable above a door, window, or facade; a classical form frequently quoted and exaggerated by Postmodernists.
- **Semantic architecture**: Buildings designed to mean or communicate through form, ornament, and historical reference beyond mere functional expression.
- **Anti-functionalism**: The philosophy that architecture need not subordinate all design decisions to functional requirements; rejecting "form follows function."
