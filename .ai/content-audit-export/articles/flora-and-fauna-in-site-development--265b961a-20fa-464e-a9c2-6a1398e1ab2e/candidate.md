# Flora and Fauna in Site Development

## Board Exam Quick Check

Treat flora and fauna as site-planning controls, not decoration. The checked local sources connect vegetation, habitat corridors, open spaces, watershed protection, and erosion control to climate resilience and safer land development.

## Source-Grounded Core

A resilient site starts with the ecological system already present. The climate-resilience reference tool says adaptation must be designed for local risks, vulnerabilities, built conditions, natural environment, and adaptive capacity. It lists ecosystem management actions such as maintaining wetlands and urban green spaces, watershed and reservoir management, reducing habitat fragmentation, maintaining genetic diversity, and community-based natural resource management.

For site plans, the same source directs planners to protect public open-space networks, operate from a ridge-to-reef or integrated ecosystems framework, and delineate protected areas, prime agricultural areas, key biodiversity areas, critical habitats, heritage areas, and ancestral domains. For LEA purposes, this means that vegetation, wildlife movement, and hydrology belong in the first site analysis pass, before footprint, access, and grading decisions are finalized.

## What To Protect

- Existing drainage lines and waterways should be altered as little as practicable.
- Vegetation clearing should be minimized.
- Viable ecological corridors for wildlife movement should be retained or provided.
- Natural topography and the amount of excavation and filling should be minimized.
- Natural-hazard risk should not be increased by the site layout.

Those performance outcomes are stated in the climate-resilience reference tool's neighborhood design code discussion. They turn landscape features into reviewable planning criteria.

## Planting As Risk Reduction

Vegetation supports climate adaptation in several ways. The reference tool lists green infrastructure such as shade trees, green roofs, ecological restoration, soil conservation, afforestation, reforestation, mangrove conservation and replanting, and ecological corridors. It also gives site and district examples: increasing tree cover, strategic green-space locations, vegetation buffers, green roofs, green walls, street trees, canopy cover, and open spaces used as breezeways and air paths.

For flood and erosion contexts, landscape is also a protective system. The same tool cites renatured river easements for erosion control and stabilization, restored riparian forests, increased upland tree cover, watershed protection measures, planting trees on hillsides, vegetation buffers, bioswales, rainbeds, and pervious pavement.

## Sitework Interface

The Siteworks sources support the technical side of landscape protection. Riprap is described as a stone layer or facing used to prevent erosion, scour, or sloughing. Gabion baskets are wire-mesh units filled with riprap and anchored to slopes. Geotextiles and geosynthetics are used for reinforcement, separation, filtration, drainage, erosion control, sediment control, slope stabilization, and channel protection.

## LEA Answer Pattern

When a question asks for the best flora-fauna site decision, prefer the answer that preserves ecological function while reducing climate risk: protect habitat and drainage corridors, limit clearing and earthwork, use vegetation for shade and stormwater control, and combine planting with engineered erosion or drainage systems where the site risk requires it.
