### Classification by Human Need

**Architectural elements** are components of buildings—physical parts that can be named, studied, and understood in terms of their purpose. Rather than memorizing lists of random terms, students must understand that every element serves at least one of four primary human needs.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0002 component="Tip" status="preserved"]

### Shelter: Enclosure and Protection

**Shelter** is architecture's primary purpose—protection from climate, pests, hazards, and unwanted observation. Shelter elements include:

**Walls** form vertical planes that enclose space, resist lateral wind and seismic forces, and provide privacy and thermal mass. In tropical climates like the Philippines, walls often include deep windows and louvers that permit ventilation while blocking direct sun. Wall thickness, material thermal mass, and surface finish all contribute to climate performance.

**Roofs** shed water, reflect or absorb solar radiation, and often provide the strongest visual expression of a building's form. A **mansard roof** is a four-sided roof where each side has two slopes—the lower slope much steeper than the upper—creating additional ceiling height and floor space within the attic. Historically, in 18th-century France, property taxes exempted attics from taxation, making the mansard's additional volume economically valuable. Today it serves both functional (additional space) and aesthetic (distinctive silhouette) purposes.

A **dome** is a curved vault spanning a circular or polygonal opening, often hemispherical, that distributes weight outward and downward. Domes require sophisticated structural understanding—the **Pantheon** in Rome (built 126 CE) remains the world's largest unreinforced concrete dome, demonstrating Roman mastery. Domes often define important gathering spaces (government buildings, religious structures, civic halls) and create dramatic interiors with central light.

**Floors** provide horizontal surfaces for activity, distribute loads to supporting structure below, and contribute thermal mass. Floor finish materials (tile, wood, concrete, stone) affect acoustics, foot comfort, durability, and aesthetic character.

**Fenestration** — the organization and design of windows in a building facade — controls the relationship between inside and outside. A building with large south-facing windows in a cold climate may overheat; the same window in a shaded tropical courtyard may bring needed light and view without thermal penalty. Fenestration design must integrate structure (window frames must be rigid), climate control (glass provides poor insulation), and social responsibility (windows on street sides permit natural surveillance and reduce crime; windows to adjoining properties must respect privacy).

### Circulation: Movement Through Space

Circulation systems organize how people move through buildings—getting from entrance to destination, ascending and descending levels, navigating emergency exits. **Circulation** serves both functional (efficient movement) and spatial (framing views, creating sequences, establishing hierarchy) purposes.

**Stairs** are the primary vertical circulation element, moving people between levels. Stair design is highly regulated—riser height (typically 150–180 mm), tread depth (typically 250–300 mm), and handrail requirements affect safety and ease of use. **Balustrades** are railings composed of small vertical members (balusters) and a continuous handrail, typically running along stairs or balconies to prevent falls. Balustrades also communicate status—a delicate rococo balustrade signals luxury and refinement differently than an austere modern cable railing.

**Ramps** provide gradual sloped access (typically 1:12 slope or shallower) accessible to people using wheelchairs, crutches, or push carts, and to anyone carrying heavy loads. Universal design demands ramps alongside stairs, not as afterthought.

**Elevators** serve vertical circulation in tall buildings, transporting people, goods, and wheelchairs silently and efficiently. An elevator shaft is also structural—it often contains bracing that resists lateral loads and strengthens the building's core.

**Corridors** and hallways distribute people horizontally, connecting spaces. Their length, width, ceiling height, lighting, and connection to windows and views shape the experience of moving through a building. A long, narrow, windowless corridor feels institutional; a corridor with abundant glazing to exterior courts feels open and connected.

**Vestibules** are transition spaces—typically small rooms connecting the street or external environment to the building's interior. A vestibule buffers temperature changes, screens direct weather, and creates a threshold that marks arrival. In Roman architecture, the vestibule of a basilica or public building was a formal entry portico. A well-designed vestibule is neither wasted space nor grand excess; it is essential to performance and ceremonial function.

**Arcades** and **porticos** are covered walkways with a colonnade—columns supporting a roof or vault—that protect circulation from weather while remaining semi-open. In hot climates, arcades provide cooling shade and encourage walking. In cities like Venice or Bologna, extensive arcades shape public life and comfort.

### Environmental Control: Climate and Comfort

Environmental control systems manage temperature, humidity, light, air quality, and sound to create conditions suitable for human activity.

**Windows** (covered under fenestration) control daylighting and views while admitting solar heat. In tropical climates, deep window reveals (recessed walls) and **louvers** — horizontal or vertical slats that block direct sunlight while allowing air and light to penetrate — are essential. Louvers can be fixed (permanent angle) or operable (adjustable to season and time of day), providing both aesthetic character and functional performance.

**Vents** and **ventilation shafts** provide air movement. Natural ventilation through operable windows, cross-ventilation through opposite walls, and stack ventilation (warm air rising and exiting through roof vents) can passively cool buildings without mechanical systems. In humid tropical climates, ventilation is critical to prevent mold and discomfort.

**Thermal mass** — materials with high heat capacity (concrete, masonry, water) — absorbs solar heat during the day and radiates it at night, moderating temperature swings. Philippine traditional architecture used thick earth walls and raised stone foundations as thermal mass.

**Shading devices** — overhangs, pergolas, vegetation — reduce solar heat gain. A roof overhang positioned correctly can block summer sun (when the sun is high) while allowing winter sun (when the sun is low) to penetrate and warm interior spaces.

**Acoustics** — controlling sound transmission and reverberation — becomes critical in offices, schools, hospitals, and concert halls. Hard surfaces (tile, concrete, glass) reflect sound and create reverberation; soft surfaces (fabric, cork, fiberglass) absorb sound.

### Structure: Load Resistance

**Structure** is the skeleton that allows buildings to stand and resist forces. Every shelter and circulation element rests on structural support.

**Foundations** transfer building loads into the earth. Foundation type depends on soil bearing capacity, frost depth, water table, and seismic risk. A shallow foundation (concrete slab on grade) works on firm soil; deep foundations (piles driven into stable layers) are required on weak or unstable soil.

**Columns** are vertical structural members that resist compression. In classical architecture, columns defined **colossal order** — giant columns extending through multiple stories, creating visual unity across a tall facade. The Palazzo Farnese in Rome exemplifies colossal order, with columns spanning two or three stories and reinforcing the building's hierarchical and monumental character. Columns can be functional (truly supporting load) or purely aesthetic (structural support provided by hidden members while visible columns are decorative).

**Beams** are horizontal structural members that resist bending, spanning between columns or walls to carry floor and roof loads. A deep beam can span a greater distance; a shallow beam requires closer supports.

**Arches** and **vaults** distribute loads outward and downward, permitting large spans without interior columns. An arch is a curved structural member; a vault is a three-dimensional arch. Roman engineers mastered arches and vaults, enabling the creation of vast interior spaces like the **Pantheon**.

**Shells** are thin curved surfaces (concrete or other material) that gain strength through curvature, much like an egg or boat hull. Contemporary architects like Félix Candela have used shells to create dramatic, efficient structures. A shell dome or barrel vault can be remarkably thin, light, and economical compared to solid construction.

### Compound Elements: Cortile, Palazzo, Piazza

Some architectural elements combine multiple functions and deserve specific terminology.

**Cortile** is an internal courtyard surrounded by arcades, characteristic of Italian Renaissance palaces. The Palazzo Medici-Riccardi and Palazzo Strozzi in Florence (both 15th-century) exemplify the cortile: a central open court surrounded by covered walkways on all sides, providing light, air, and semi-private circulation for residents. The cortile serves simultaneously as shelter (covered walks), circulation (protected movement around the courtyard), environmental control (passive ventilation and light), and social space (gathering place).

**Palazzo** is an Italian palace or substantial mansion, typically built in ashlar (squared stone) with symmetrical facades and monumental scale. Examples include the Palazzo Farnese and Palazzo Strozzi. A palazzo demonstrates wealth and power through material durability (stone), precise proportions, and abundant fenestration (glass was expensive, signaling affluence).

**Piazza** is a public square, typically surrounded by important buildings and serving as civic gathering space. The Piazza San Marco in Venice and Piazza Navona in Rome are world-famous examples. Piazzas serve the entire city—not shelter or individual circulation, but social, ceremonial, and commercial gathering.

### Ornament and Finishing: Style and Expression

Architectural terminology also encompasses stylistic periods and ornamental vocabularies:

**Rococo** (mid-18th-century French style) emphasizes ornament, asymmetry, gilding, pastel colors, sculpted molding, and trompe-l'œil frescoes (painted illusions) to create drama and movement. Rococo is a refinement and exuberance within aristocratic interiors; it contrasts sharply with the minimalism of contemporary modernism. The **Palazzo Pianetti** in Italy (begun 1748) exemplifies Rococo with elaborate carved and gilded interiors.

**Pulpit** is a raised platform or lectern in a church from which the clergy delivers sermons. The pulpit's height, position (typically facing the congregation), and sometimes elaborate decoration emphasize the importance of the word and the speaker's authority.

**Colossal order** (or **giant order**) refers to columns or pilasters extending through two or more stories, creating visual continuity and monumentality across a tall facade. Examples include the Palazzo Farnese and many Baroque palaces.

## Key Terms

**Balustrade** — A railing composed of small vertical spindles (balusters) supporting a continuous handrail, used along stairs, balconies, and terraces to prevent falls while providing visual transparency and elegance.

**Cortile** — An internal courtyard surrounded by covered arcaded walkways on all sides, characteristic of Italian Renaissance palaces and providing light, air circulation, privacy, and sheltered movement.

**Colossal Order** — Architectural columns or pilasters extending through two or more stories of a facade, creating visual unity and monumental effect; also called giant order.

**Dome** — A curved, vault-like structure spanning a circular or polygonal opening, distributing loads outward and downward, commonly used for civic, religious, and monumental buildings.

**Fenestration** — The planned organization and design of windows in a building facade, determining daylighting, views, thermal gain/loss, ventilation, and security characteristics.

**Louver** — A series of horizontal or vertical slats or blades that control sunlight and air movement while blocking direct exposure or unwanted views; can be fixed or operable.

**Mansard Roof** — A roof with four sides, each sloped at two different angles, with the lower slope much steeper, creating additional usable space within the attic area.

**Palazzo** — An Italian palace or grand townhouse, typically built in ashlar (dressed stone) with monumental proportions, symmetrical facade, and abundant fenestration signaling wealth and status.

**Piazza** — A public civic square or plaza, typically surrounded by important buildings and serving as a gathering place for commerce, ceremony, and community life.

**Pulpit** — A raised platform or lectern in a church, typically elevated and ornately decorated, from which clergy deliver sermons; emphasizes the speaker's authority and the centrality of the word.

**Rococo** — An ornate 18th-century European style emphasizing asymmetry, gilding, pastel colors, sculpted molding, and elaborately painted surfaces to create drama and movement, primarily in interiors.

**Shell Structure** — A thin curved structural surface that gains strength through curvature rather than thickness, allowing efficient spanning of large distances with minimal material; used for domes and vaults.

**Vestibule** — A transitional entrance space buffering the exterior and interior environments, controlling temperature, protecting from weather, and marking the threshold of arrival into the building.
