### Egyptian Architecture

Egyptian architecture emerged from the Nile Valley's particular resources and values. The Nile provided **limestone** (from quarries at Tura and Aswan), **granite** (from Aswan), and **sandstone** (from Nubia)—all durable stone suited to monumental construction. Egypt's society centered on the pharaoh as divine intermediary between the gods and the people. This theology drove architecture: temples honored gods; tombs ensured the pharaoh's eternal existence.

Egyptian architectural evolution followed a clear progression:

**Mastaba (c. 3100–2686 BCE):** The earliest royal tomb, a low rectangular mound of mud brick or stone with interior burial chambers. Mastabas evolved from simple pits to elaborate structures with offering chapels and false doors for the deceased's spirit.

**Stepped Pyramid (c. 2686–2613 BCE):** The transition form. **Djoser's Stepped Pyramid** at **Saqqara** (c. 2670 BCE, architect **Imhotep**) consisted of six stacked mastabas, creating a stepped silhouette. It stood 205 feet high and was the tallest man-made structure on earth for over 1,000 years. Imhotep's innovation was structural confidence: moving from mud brick to stone, from low horizontal forms to vertical monumentality.

**True Pyramid (c. 2613–2181 BCE):** The **Great Pyramid of Khufu** at **Giza** (c. 2560 BCE) represents the apex of pyramid construction. Original height: 481 feet (146.6 m); current height: 454 feet (138.5 m) due to loss of casing stones. Base: 756 feet (230.3 m) on each side. Built from approximately 2.3 million limestone blocks, each weighing 2.5 tons on average, fitted with precision so tight that a knife blade cannot fit between them. No mortar was used; blocks were set by weight and gravity alone. The internal structure includes the King's Chamber (containing a granite sarcophagus), the Queen's Chamber, and elaborate passages designed to frustrate tomb robbery. The true pyramid required understanding geometry (the 51-degree angle was carefully calculated to distribute weight), quarrying, and organization of labor.

**Temples (New Kingdom, c. 1550–1070 BCE):** As funerary practice evolved, temple-building dominated later Egyptian architecture. **Karnak Temple Complex** (dedicated primarily to **Amun-Ra**, expanded over 1,500 years) covers 62 acres and features the **Great Hypostyle Hall**—one of the most important religious spaces in antiquity. Built under **Seti I** and **Ramesses II** (c. 1290–1224 BCE), the hall measured 338 × 170 feet and contained 134 columns arranged in 16 rows. The central columns were taller (66 feet), with capitals shaped like papyrus flowers; peripheral columns were shorter (49 feet), with bud capitals. Light filtered through clerestory windows cut high in the central row, creating a sacred atmosphere. The hall demonstrated the engineer's ability to create vast interior spaces using stone columns—a technical triumph and a religious statement.

**Luxor Temple** (c. 1390 BCE, **Amenhotep III**, renovated by **Ramesses II**) featured a linear plan: a pylon gateway, open courtyard, hypostyle hall, and inner sanctuary. This sequence—from public to private, from bright to shadowed—guided worshippers and priests through sacred space.

Egyptian temple design relied on the **post-and-lintel system** (vertical columns supporting horizontal stone lintels) and employed the **hypostyle hall** (many columns supporting a roof). Walls were massive (to hold the weight of stone), tapering slightly inward (a practical response to stone's weight distribution). Reliefs and hieroglyphic inscriptions covered nearly every wall surface, serving both decorative and religious functions.

### West Asiatic Architecture

West Asiatic cultures (Hittites, Canaanites, Levantine peoples) built temples and fortifications adapted to mountainous terrain and defensive needs. **Hittite temples** (c. 1650–1200 BCE) were often small, inward-facing structures with thick walls and limited windows—practical in a rocky landscape and protective against heat and attack. The **Temple of Yazılıkaya** near Boğazköy (c. 1250 BCE) carved into a natural rock outcrop, with sculpted reliefs of gods and the king. The integration of natural stone and carved decoration was characteristic of Hittite practice.

**Fortress architecture** was central to West Asiatic building. The **walls of Jericho** (continuously occupied from c. 9000 BCE) show evolution from simple barriers to sophisticated defensive systems with towers and gates. Walls were built of stone or mud brick, often with a sloped base (glacis) to prevent scaling and to shed water.

## Key Terms

**Mastaba:** The earliest form of royal tomb in Egypt, a low rectangular mound with interior burial chambers and offering chapels; evolved from mud brick to stone.

**Stepped Pyramid:** A transitional pyramid form consisting of stacked mastabas or tiers, creating a stepped silhouette; Djoser's at Saqqara is the best-preserved example.

**True Pyramid:** A pyramid with smooth, triangular sides meeting at an apex; represents the final stage of pyramid evolution; the Great Pyramid of Khufu is the canonical example.

**Hypostyle hall:** A roofed space supported by many columns in a grid arrangement; allows for vast interiors and controlled lighting through clerestory windows.

**Pylon:** A monumental gateway structure flanking the entrance to an Egyptian temple, typically trapezoidal in plan.

**Clerestory:** A row of windows placed high in a wall (above eye level when standing in a hall) to provide light without exposing the interior to direct view from outside.

**Post-and-lintel:** A structural system using vertical supports (posts or columns) carrying horizontal beams (lintels); the dominant system in Egyptian and classical architecture.

**Glacis:** A sloped, reinforced base of a fortification wall, designed to prevent scaling and to shed water.

**Cartouche:** An oval or rectangular frame enclosing a pharaoh's name, commonly carved in relief on temple walls and monuments.

**Clerestory window:** High window providing light to the interior of a hypostyle hall; used strategically to create sacred atmosphere while controlling visibility.
