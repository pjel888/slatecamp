## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal

Distinguish the three Greek orders (Doric, Ionic, Corinthian) by proportion, capital form, and entablature design; identify the Parthenon's optical refinements; and explain the post-and-lintel system as the structural basis of Greek temple architecture.
## Core Concept

Greek architecture (c. 900–146 BCE) abandoned the massive walls and hypostyle halls of earlier civilizations. Instead, Greek builders created structures of disciplined proportion, rational geometry, and visual refinement. The **temple** became the primary building type—a rectangular chamber (naos) housing a statue of the deity, elevated on a platform (stereobate), surrounded by a colonnade (peristasis), and roofed.

The revolutionary step was the **order**—a system combining the column, capital, and entablature (the structure above the columns) into a coherent, proportional module. Three orders emerged, each with mathematical relationships between parts:

**Doric Order:** The earliest and most austere. A Doric column is squat and sturdy, roughly 4–6 times its diameter in height. The capital is a simple, squared abacus above a tapered echinus. The entablature features a triglyph (three vertical grooves) above each column, with a metope (relief-carved square) between triglyphs. The Doric conveys strength and simplicity. The **Temple of Hephaestus** at Athens (c. 450 BCE) and the **Temple of Zeus** at Olympia (c. 470–456 BCE) are Doric, but the most famous Doric application is the **Parthenon** (see below).

**Ionic Order:** More slender and elegant than Doric. An Ionic column is 8–10 times its diameter in height, with a base (unlike Doric) and a capital featuring two spiraling scrolls (volutes). The entablature is simpler: a continuous frieze (often sculpted) runs above the columns. The Ionic appears earliest in Asia Minor (present-day Turkey) and was adopted for mainland Greek temples later. The **Temple of Athena Nike** at Athens (c. 427–424 BCE) is Ionic and delicate.

**Corinthian Order:** The most ornate, emerging in the 5th century BCE and becoming dominant in Hellenistic and Roman periods. The Corinthian capital features acanthus leaves and other scrollwork, making it visually rich. The column is about 10 times its diameter in height, the slenderest of the three. The Corinthian order is rare in Classical Greece but common in later periods. The **Temple of the Olympian Zeus** at Athens (begun 174 BCE, completed under Roman rule) is a famous Corinthian example, though it postdates Classical Greece.

**The Parthenon (Temple of Athena Parthenos):** Built on the Acropolis at Athens between 447–432 BCE under the direction of the general **Pericles**, designed by **Iktinos** and **Kallikrates**, with sculpture overseen by **Phidias**. The Parthenon measures 228 feet (69.5 m) long and 101 feet (30.9 m) wide. Its plan is a rectangular naos with an outer colonnade (peristasis) of 8 columns on the short ends and 17 columns on the long sides. This *8 × 17* arrangement is standard, but the Parthenon's refinement lies in **optical refinement**: the columns are not perfectly straight but curve slightly (entasis); the base and upper levels taper imperceptibly; corner columns are thicker. These modifications, invisible to the naked eye at distance, prevent the horizontal lines from appearing to sag. The Parthenon is a masterpiece of visual perception as much as geometry.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Note" status="preserved"]

The Parthenon housed a chryselephantine (gold and ivory) statue of Athena, 40+ feet tall, now lost. The sculptural program included a continuous frieze around the naos depicting the Panathenaic procession, and pediment sculptures (now in the British Museum) depicting scenes of Athena's birth and patronage. The Parthenon's interior was as important as its exterior; it was a treasury and a sanctuary, not merely a visual monument.

**Other Temple Types:** The **Treasury of Atreus** at Mycenae (c. 1350 BCE, pre-Classical but influential) featured a corbelled dome—a "tholos" or circular structure that predates Greek post-and-lintel temples. Later Greek architects revived the tholos form for certain temples and treasuries, such as the **Tholos of Polyclitus** at Epidaurus (c. 360 BCE). The tholos employed the post-and-lintel system with internal colonnades, though the corbelled vault disappeared in favor of post-and-lintel construction.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0004 component="Warn" status="preserved"]

**City Planning:** Greek architects, particularly in the 4th century BCE, developed rational city plans. **Hippodamus of Miletus** designed **Miletus** (after 479 BCE) on a grid system—straight streets intersecting at right angles, dividing the city into rational blocks. This orthogonal plan influenced later Hellenistic and Roman cities.

**Materials and Craftsmanship:** Greek temples were built from **marble** (particularly Parian marble for sculpture, Pentelic marble for the Parthenon) and **limestone**, fitted with precision. Unlike Egyptian stone construction (massive blocks set by weight), Greek builders cut stone to economical sizes and fitted them with dowels and clamps of bronze or lead. The columns were built in drums (cylindrical sections) stacked vertically. Entasis (the slight curve) was achieved by subtle reductions in diameter between drums.

## Key Terms

**Order:** A system combining column, capital, and entablature into a proportional, coherent module; the three Greek orders are Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian.

**Doric order:** The simplest order; squat column (4–6 diameters), simple capital, entablature with triglyph and metope.

**Ionic order:** Medium slender column (8–10 diameters), scrolled volutes on capital, continuous frieze entablature, base present.

**Corinthian order:** Slenderest order (10+ diameters), ornate acanthus-leaf capital, emergence in Hellenistic period.

**Peristasis (or peristyle):** A colonnade surrounding a temple on all sides; described numerically (e.g., *8 × 17*) indicating columns on short and long sides.

**Naos (or cella):** The inner sanctum of a Greek temple, typically a rectangular chamber housing the cult statue.

**Stereobate:** The platform (base) on which a Greek temple rests; typically approached by monumental steps (stylobate is the top surface).

**Entablature:** The entire structure above the columns, including the frieze, architrave, and cornice.

**Entasis:** The subtle inward curve of a column shaft, creating the illusion of straightness and counteracting optical distortion.

**Chryselephantine:** A sculptural technique combining gold and ivory; famous examples include Phidias' Athena and Zeus.

**Caryatid (or Caryatids):** A sculpted female figure serving as a column or support; seen in the Porch of the Maidens at the Erechtheion.
