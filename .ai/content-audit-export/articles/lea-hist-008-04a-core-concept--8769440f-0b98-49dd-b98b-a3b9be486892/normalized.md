## Core Concept
The **Bauhaus** (1919–1933) was the most influential design school of the 20th century. Located in Germany, it unified art, craft, and industrial technology through radical pedagogy, attracting the era's greatest modernist thinkers and artists. Though it lasted only 14 years, it transformed design education globally and established principles that guide design thinking today.

**Founding and Philosophy (1919)**
**Walter Gropius** founded the Bauhaus in Weimar, Germany, in 1919, in response to post-World War I cultural crisis. Germany's defeat and economic collapse created an opportunity to rebuild from first principles. Gropius believed that integrating art and industry would restore cultural meaning to mass production. The Bauhaus combined a medieval craft guild model (master-apprentice) with modern industrial production. Students learned in workshops—typography, metalwork, weaving, furniture design, theater—under artist-teachers and craft masters. The curriculum had no predetermined style; instead, it taught principles of form, color, material, and human behavior applicable to any design problem.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Note" status="preserved"]

The school's name—"Bauhaus" (literally "building house")—expressed this totality. All design disciplines would feed into architecture, the highest synthesis of all arts. This was Gropius's grand vision: not a style school, but a philosophy school that would reshape German (and world) culture.

**The Weimar Bauhaus (1919–1925)**
In Weimar, the Bauhaus was experimental and exploratory. Gropius hired legendary artists: **Paul Klee** taught form and color; **Wassily Kandinsky** taught abstraction; **Oskar Schlemmer** directed the theater workshop. These were not professional designers—they were fine artists who brought conceptual rigor to craft. The school rejected the hierarchy that had separated fine art from craft; instead, all making was valued equally.

Key innovations emerged in Weimar: **László Moholy-Nagy** explored photography, film, and light; **Herbert Bayer** pioneered graphic design and typography; the **weaving workshop** (led by **Anni Albers**) produced textiles of extraordinary innovation. The Bauhaus Weimar was chaotic, idealistic, sometimes impractical. But it established that design could be intellectually rigorous and spiritually meaningful.

**Relocation to Dessau (1925–1932)**
When Weimar's conservative government withdrew funding, Gropius relocated the Bauhaus to Dessau, an industrial city more sympathetic to modernism. The move coincided with Germany's economic stabilization (the "Golden Twenties"), and the Dessau Bauhaus flourished commercially and artistically.

The **Bauhaus Building in Dessau** (1925–1926, designed by Gropius) became the school's symbol. The building is a manifesto in glass and steel: it demonstrates every principle taught inside. Classrooms, studios, workshops, and student housing are unified in a rationalized, efficient plan. The structure is honestly expressed. The building itself teaches modernism to everyone who enters.

In Dessau, the Bauhaus also became more pragmatic. Rather than pure experimentation, the school began collaborating with industry. **Marcel Breuer** designed the **Wassily Chair** (1925–1926, originally Model B3)—a tubular steel furniture that could be mass-produced, comfortable, and beautiful. Inspired by the tubular steel frame of a bicycle, Breuer created the first furniture in bent tubing, pioneering a new industrial method. The school developed lighting systems, textiles, typography, and ceramics intended for factory production. The Bauhaus was no longer just an educational institution; it was a design laboratory for modern industry.

**The Meyer and Mies Directorates (1928–1933)**
When Gropius stepped down in 1928, **Hannes Meyer**, a politically radical architect and educator, became director. Meyer emphasized functionalism and social responsibility. He introduced "scientific" design methods and prioritized serving working-class needs. The Bauhaus under Meyer was less artistic, more socially engaged. His tenure was brief (1928–1930) and controversial—he was forced out by conservative backlash against his political views.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0004 component="Warn" status="preserved"]

**Mies van der Rohe** took the helm in 1930. Under Mies, the Bauhaus became more austere, geometric, and formal. Mies had little interest in social ideology; he pursued architectural essence and spatial perfection. He reorganized the curriculum around fundamental design principles—form, color, material, space—rather than craft mediums. Mies's Bauhaus was more rigorous and less idealistic than Gropius's, more abstract than Meyer's.

**Nazi Closure (1933)**
The Nazi Party viewed the Bauhaus as a threat: its modernism contradicted Nazi aesthetics, its internationalism contradicted nationalist ideology, and many of its Jewish teachers were enemies of the regime. The Bauhaus was forced to close in 1933. Most faculty emigrated, spreading Bauhaus principles globally. **Gropius** went to America and influenced design education. **Mies** went to America and became the dominant figure in corporate modernism. **Moholy-Nagy** founded the Institute of Design in Chicago. **Breuer** became a major architect. The dispersion of the Bauhaus was actually its greatest success—it ensured the philosophy would influence design globally.

**Legacy and Principles**
The Bauhaus left several enduring principles:

1. **Total Design**: All design disciplines are unified by shared principles; no hierarchy between fine art, craft, graphic design, or architecture.

2. **Design Pedagogy**: Teaching principles and thinking rather than styles; students learn by doing, experimenting, and discovering rather than copying.

3. **Craft-Industry Integration**: Industrial mass production need not be soulless; by applying artistic principles to manufacturing, designers can create beautiful objects accessible to everyone.

4. **Form and Function**: Good design unifies aesthetic and functional concerns; form and function are inseparable.

5. **Human-Centered Design**: Design serves human needs, comfort, and behavior—it is not purely geometric or abstract.

These principles shaped post-war design globally. The International Style in architecture, the modernist design movement, and contemporary design education all bear the Bauhaus imprint.

## Key Terms

**Bauhaus**: A design school (1919–1933) in Germany founded by Walter Gropius, integrating art, craft, and industrial technology through radical pedagogy; one of the 20th century's most influential institutions.

**Total Design**: Philosophy that all design disciplines—architecture, furniture, graphics, textiles, products—are unified by shared principles; no hierarchy between fine art and craft.

**Master-Apprentice System**: Pedagogical model from medieval craft guilds; Bauhaus combined this with modern industrial production, pairing artist-teachers with craft masters.

**Functionalism**: Design principle that form should directly serve function; rejects applied ornament; form and function are inseparable.

**Workshop System**: Bauhaus teaching method where students learned by working in actual production studios (metalwork, textiles, typography, etc.) under teachers and masters.

**Geometric Abstraction**: Design approach reducing forms to essential geometric shapes; emphasis in Mies's Bauhaus directorate.

**Craft-Industry Integration**: Bauhaus principle that fine craftsmanship and industrial mass production are not opposites but can be unified; industry can produce beautiful, well-designed objects for everyone.

**Modernist Pedagogy**: Teaching method emphasizing principles, thinking, and experimentation over style copying; students learn how to design, not what to design.

**Design Principles**: Foundational concepts (form, color, material, proportion, scale, balance) taught at Bauhaus; applicable to any design problem across all disciplines.

**International Style**: Modernist architectural movement influenced by Bauhaus; characterized by geometric forms, open plans, honest structure, and universal applicability across climates and cultures.
