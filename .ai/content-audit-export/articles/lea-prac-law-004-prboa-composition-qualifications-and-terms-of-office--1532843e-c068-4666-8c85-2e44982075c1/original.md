## PRBoA Composition, Qualifications, and Terms of Office

## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- Describe the PRBoA composition: 1 Chairman and 2 Members appointed by the President.
- List the six (6) qualifications for PRBoA membership under RA 9266 §4.
- State the term of office (3 years, staggered, max 2 terms / 6 years).
- Identify the IAPOA (UAP) as the nominating body.

### Structural Framework: The Three-Person Collegial Board
Section 4 of RA 9266 establishes the Professional Regulatory Board of Architecture as a collegial body composed of a Chairman and two (2) Members—three (3) persons total. This structure was chosen deliberately as a balance: sufficient size to ensure diverse perspective without bureaucratic unwieldiness, and odd number to prevent deadlocked 2-2 votes.

The Board operates under the administrative supervision and control of the Professional Regulation Commission. This means the Commission provides budgetary, human resources, and facility support; establishes administrative procedures; and oversees the Board's operations. However, the Board retains decision-making autonomy over professional and technical matters. The PRBoA is not merely a commission staff committee; it is a collegial body with independent judgment on matters within its jurisdiction.

### Appointment Authority and the Nomination Process

The President of the Philippines appoints the Board members. However, the appointment is not presidential fiat; Section 4 constrains the President's choice through a nominating process. The Commission (on behalf of the President) receives recommendations from the Integrated and Accredited Professional Organization of Architects (IAPOA, now the United Architects of the Philippines, Inc.).

The process works as follows: for each vacant position, IAPOA submits a list of five (5) nominees. The Commission screens these and submits to the President a list of three (3) recommendations chosen from the five (5) nominees. The President then appoints from these three (3). This system prevents the President from entirely disregarding professional input while reserving final appointment authority to the executive branch.

The structural design reflects a nuanced approach to professional self-regulation: the President ensures democratic accountability (the architect cannot make themselves governors), while the IAPOA ensures that regulators remain rooted in professional knowledge and practice realities. Direct appointment by the President would make regulators political operatives; direct election by the profession would create regulatory capture. The three-stage process—nomination by profession, screening by Commission, appointment by President—balances these concerns.

### Mandatory Qualifications: Six Conditions for Board Service

Section 5 establishes six mandatory qualifications that each Board member must possess at the time of appointment. These are not suggestions or preferences; they are non-negotiable prerequisites for taking office. Failure to meet any single qualification makes an appointment invalid.

First, the member must be a citizen and resident of the Philippines. This ensures that Board members have a stake in the nation's built environment and are subject to Philippine law. Foreign nationals, even if registered and licensed in the Philippines, cannot serve on the Board. This reflects a policy that fundamental regulatory authority should rest with persons bound to Philippine sovereignty.

Second, the member must hold a Bachelor of Science degree in Architecture from a school, college, or university in the Philippines or abroad that is recognized or accredited by the Commission on Higher Education (CHED). This ensures minimum academic preparation in architectural theory, design, technical systems, history, and professional practice. The CHED accreditation requirement prevents reliance on unaccredited institutions.

Third, the member must hold a valid Certificate of Registration and Professional Identification Card. This proves that the person is currently registered and licensed to practice architecture in the Philippines. A person whose license is expired or suspended is ineligible. The requirement ensures that Board members understand contemporary registration requirements and maintain their own compliance.

Fourth, the member must be an active practitioner of architecture for at least ten (10) years on the appointment date. This requirement distinguishes the Board from purely academic regulation; members must have hands-on field experience managing projects, working with clients and contractors, navigating building codes, and dealing with the practical realities of design and construction. A purely academic architect or an architect who left practice decades ago may lack current understanding of practice challenges.

The ten-year minimum ensures depth; it excludes recent licensees who lack sufficient experience to judge complex technical and ethical questions. It also ensures that members understand the profession's evolution over the preceding decade.

Fifth, the member must not be a faculty member at any school, college, university, or review institution where architecture is taught or offered as a regular or review course. Additionally, the member cannot have pecuniary interest in such institutions. No faculty member can become a Board member unless they have officially resigned and completely stopped teaching, advising, or reviewing activities at least five (5) years before nomination.

This requirement prevents conflicts of interest. If a Board member is simultaneously teaching architecture, potential conflicts arise: the Board sets licensure exam standards and registration requirements that affect the educational market; faculty members have incentives to influence these standards to favor their curricula or to create demand for their programs. The five-year cooling-off period (from resignation until nomination) ensures that the appointee has genuinely disengaged from academic practice.

Sixth, the member must have never been convicted of any crime involving moral turpitude. Moral turpitude offenses include crimes of dishonesty (fraud, forgery, bribery, extortion), crimes of violence (assault, murder), and crimes violating trust (breach of fiduciary duty, theft). This requirement reflects the principle that persons entrusted with regulating the profession must themselves be of unblemished character.

Additionally, the member cannot be an elected officer of the IAPOA (now UAP) or other professional organizations of architects. This prevents organizational politicking from capturing the Board. If the IAPOA president, vice president, or treasurer served simultaneously on the Board, the Board is perceived as subject to organizational pressure. Independence from organizational office is essential to regulatory impartiality.

### Terms of Office: The Staggered System

Section 6 prescribes a staggered term system designed to ensure both continuity and regular renewal. Members hold office for three (3) years from appointment "or until their successors shall have been appointed and duly qualified," whichever is later. This language addresses the reality that successor appointments may be delayed by political processes, budget cycles, or administrative inefficiency; the incumbent's tenure extends until the successor is ready to assume office.

The statute specifies that of the three members first appointed under RA 9266, one shall serve as Chairman for three (3) years; one Member shall serve for two (2) years; and one Member shall serve for one (1) year. After the initial staggered terms expire, subsequent appointments follow the same pattern: the newly appointed Chairman serves three years; one new Member serves two years; one new Member serves one year.

This structure ensures that in any given year, at most one new appointment occurs (unless an unexpected vacancy requires mid-term filling). It prevents the President from replacing the entire Board in a single action. It guarantees that at least two continuing members provide institutional memory and policy continuity.

### Reappointment and Tenure Limits

Section 6 further states that "Each member of the Board may be reappointed for one full additional term only." However, the IRR clarifies that the reappointment term is a uniform three (3) years for all members, regardless of their initial term length. This language permits reappointment but limits it to a single additional term. Thus, the maximum tenure is:

- Chairman: 3 years (initial) + 3 years (one additional term) = 6 years maximum
- Member (2-year term): 2 years (initial) + 3 years (one additional term) = 5 years maximum
- Member (1-year term): 1 year (initial) + 3 years (one additional term) = 4 years maximum

This design prevents indefinite tenure while allowing sufficient time for a member to develop expertise and implement policy initiatives. A two-year Member with only two years initial tenure is just beginning to understand complex issues when term expiration arrives. By allowing one additional term, that Member could serve five years total, gaining reasonable experience.

The "one full additional term only" language is significant: partial additional terms are not permitted. If a two-year Member's initial appointment is delayed six months, that Member does not receive a "bonus" sixth month to the reappointment. The one reappointment is fixed at three (3) years regardless of the initial term length, as specified in the IRR.

### The Oath of Office Requirement

Section 6 mandates that "Each member of the Board shall qualify by taking the proper oath prior to the performance of their duties." This oath requirement, typical of public officials, creates a legal and moral commitment. The oath likely obligates the member to faithfully discharge duties, uphold the Constitution and laws, and avoid conflicts of interest.

Taking the oath is a prerequisite to assuming office; an appointed member who fails to take the oath cannot exercise Board powers. The oath creates a legal nexus between the Board member and the state, underscoring that the Board is a public body exercising sovereign regulatory authority.

### Transitional Provisions for the First Board

The statute includes special transitional language for the first Board appointed under RA 9266. The "incumbent members of the Board...shall continue to serve for the remainder of their term as members of the herein created Professional Regulatory Board of Architecture until a new Board shall have been properly organized."

This provided continuity; members of the prior architecture regulatory body (under RA 545) could continue serving pending appointment of the new PRBoA under RA 9266. Additionally, "the incumbent members of the Board may be appointed as members of the First Board," allowing prior members to be considered for reappointment without starting from scratch with a nominee list.

This pragmatic approach recognized that architectural expertise in government is scarce; losing all experienced regulators during the transition would have been inefficient and potentially destabilizing. It also provided immediate continuity pending the IAPOA nomination and presidential appointment process.

### Administrative Supervision and Support

Section 8 addresses the Board's relationship to the Commission. The Board operates "under the administrative supervision of the Commission." This means the Commission provides:

- Budgetary support and appropriations
- Human resources, including staff and secretarial support
- Facilities (office space, meeting rooms, equipment)
- Administrative procedures and protocols
- Record-keeping and document management

The Commission designates the Secretary of the Board and provides secretarial services. This relieves the Board of administrative burdens, allowing members to focus on professional and technical judgments. All Board records, including examination applications, questions, answer sheets, and investigation files, are "under the custody of the Commission."

This custody provision protects examination security and prevents Board members from accessing confidential records outside the formal proceeding context.

### Compensation and Allowances

Section 10 addresses compensation. The Chairman and Members "shall receive compensation and allowances comparable to that being received by the chairman and members of existing regulatory boards under the Commission as provided for in the General Appropriations Act."

This language benchmarks PRBoA compensation to other boards (Engineering, Medicine, Nursing, etc.), ensuring that architecture is neither privileged nor marginalized within the regulatory commission's hierarchy. Compensation comes from the General Appropriations Act, meaning the legislature must appropriate funds; the Board cannot unilaterally create compensation.

The compensation likely consists of per diem for meeting attendance, honorarium for case decisions, and reimbursement for allowable expenses. The precise amounts vary by year based on appropriations. This creates an incentive structure that encourages serious work (meetings are compensated, not performed gratis) while preventing compensation so generous that Board service becomes a financial sinecure.

### Requirements for Board Organization

Section 4 mandates that "The Board shall be organized not later than six (6) months from the effectivity of the Architecture Act of 2004." Since RA 9266 was signed March 17, 2004 and took effect immediately, and the IRR became effective December 1, 2004, the organizational deadline was June 1, 2005.

This deadline ensured that regulatory mechanisms would be in place within a reasonable timeframe. A longer delay would have left architecture practice unregulated during the transition; a shorter deadline might have been impractical given the need to nominate, screen, appoint, and qualify members.
