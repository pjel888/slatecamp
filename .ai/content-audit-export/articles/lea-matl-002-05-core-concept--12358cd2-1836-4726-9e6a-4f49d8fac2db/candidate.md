## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal

Identify cement types (Types I–V per ASTM C150 / PNS 43) by their chemical composition, strength gain rate, and appropriate applications, and explain how sulfate resistance, heat of hydration, and strength class determine Type selection.
## Core Concept

Hydration is the chemical reaction between portland cement and water that produces the glue holding concrete together. Hydration requires water (moisture), suitable temperature (minimum 10°C or 50°F), and time (minimum 7 days for ordinary portland cement at normal temperatures). Without proper curing, concrete remains weak, porous, and permeable, leading to premature failure, rebar corrosion, and reduced service life.

The LEA Board recommendation and ACI 308R guidance: _Maintain concrete above 10°C (50°F) and keep it moist for at least 7 days for ordinary portland cement (OPC). High-early-strength (Type III) concrete requires only 3 days above 10°C._

## Curing Methods: Moist Curing (Moisture Retention)

Moist curing keeps the concrete surface wet and prevents evaporation of internal moisture. This is the most effective curing method and is preferred by ACI 308R.

### Ponding (Submersion Curing)

The concrete surface is covered with water maintained at 1—2 inches depth. Water is continuously retained on the surface, providing ideal moisture and thermal stability.

**Application:** Flat slabs, floors, pavements, and pool decks where water impoundment is practical.

**Advantages:** Uniform temperature control, maximum moisture retention, simple maintenance.

**Disadvantages:** Only suitable for horizontal surfaces; water must be contained and managed; requires labor and material (plastic dam or forms).

### Continuous Sprinkling or Fog Spray

Water is applied via sprinklers or fog nozzles to keep the surface continuously wet without ponding. This is practical for vertical surfaces and slopes.

**Application:** Walls, columns, sloped surfaces, cast-in-place concrete structures.

**Advantages:** Works on all orientations; effective moisture retention; can be automated.

**Disadvantages:** Requires water supply and delivery system; if interrupted (power failure, valve closure), concrete dries rapidly; in arid climates, fog spray may not prevent evaporation if humidity is very low.

### Wet Coverings (Burlap, Straw, Cloth)

Concrete is covered with wet burlap, straw, or cloth that is kept continuously moist by sprinkling or submersion of the covering.

**Application:** Columns, walls, and exposed cast-in-place concrete where ponding is impractical.

**Advantages:** Simple, low-cost, effective for 7-day curing period.

**Disadvantages:** Requires daily maintenance to keep covering wet; if covering dries, moisture loss accelerates; labor-intensive; must ensure covering does not cause staining or damage.

## Membrane Curing (Chemical Curing Compounds)

Curing compounds (ASTM C309) are liquid or wax-based materials sprayed onto the concrete surface to form a waterproof seal, trapping internal moisture and preventing evaporation.

### Types of Curing Compounds (ASTM C309)

**Type 1: Clear (solvent-based):** Forms a transparent film; allows inspection of concrete; used when appearance is important.

**Type 1A: Clear/Translucent (water-based):** Environmentally preferable to Type 1; performance equivalent; gaining market acceptance.

**Type 2: White-pigmented:** Forms a white film that reflects solar radiation, reducing concrete temperature rise during hot-weather curing. Preferred for tropical climates to minimize temperature-induced cracking.

### Application and Coverage

- Application rate: 150—200 square feet per gallon (15—20 m²/L), depending on product and surface porosity
- Applied immediately after finishing (typically within 30 minutes) or after concrete is no longer tacky
- Must form a continuous film; thin or incomplete coverage allows evaporation and defeats the purpose
- Reapplication may be needed if the film is damaged (foot traffic, wind damage, cracks in film)

**Limitations of membrane curing:**
- Less effective than water curing for early strength development (approximately 70—80% as effective as continuous water curing)
- Sensitive to application defects (skipped areas, thin coverage)
- Not suitable for surfaces that will receive additional concrete placement (adhesion problems)
- May affect finishes applied later (paint, tile); surface must be cleaned

## Temperature Effects on Curing Rate

Curing rate increases dramatically with temperature. The rule of thumb is that concrete strength development approximately doubles for every 20°C (36°F) rise in temperature (within practical limits).

## Tropical Philippines Context: Hot-Weather Curing

The Philippines' tropical climate presents specific curing challenges:

- **High ambient temperature:** 30—35°C (86—95°F) year-round accelerates hydration but also increases evaporation rate
- **High humidity:** Typically 70—90% reduces evaporation compared to arid climates but does not eliminate it
- **Solar radiation:** Intense sun heating exposed concrete surfaces to 50—60°C (122—140°F), causing rapid surface drying and thermal stress
- **Rainfall:** Frequent tropical rains may interrupt curing if concrete is exposed; if curing compound is applied, rain does not improve curing (the seal prevents water ingress)

**Best practice for tropical Philippines:**

1. Use white-pigmented curing compound (Type 2, ASTM C309) to reflect solar heat and reduce surface temperature
2. Apply curing compound within 30 minutes of finishing; ensure complete coverage
3. If using moist curing, apply continuous fog spray or cover with wet burlap; maintain moisture for full 7 days
4. Schedule concrete placement to avoid peak sun hours (midday); pour in late afternoon or early morning
5. For large exposed surfaces (slabs, walls), use shade cloth or temporary structures to reduce solar heating
6. In wet areas (coastal regions), prioritize white-pigmented curing compound over water curing to prevent salt accumulation and efflorescence
7. Monitor concrete temperature; if surface exceeds 50°C during curing, risk of thermal cracking increases

## ACI 308 and ACI 305.1 (Hot-Weather Concreting)

**ACI 308R-16 (Guide to External Curing of Concrete)** specifies:
- Minimum 7-day moist curing at temperature >10°C for ordinary portland cement
- Minimum 3-day curing for high-early-strength (Type III) cement
- Curing compound application (ASTM C309) as alternative to water curing; effectiveness typically 70—80% of moist curing for strength development

**ACI 305.1-06 (Specification for Hot Weather Concreting)** addresses tropical and arid climates:
- Reduce water-cement ratio to minimize bleed water and drying shrinkage
- Minimize evaporation rate during curing (maintain &lt;1.0 kg/m²/hr if possible; &lt;0.5 kg/m²/hr preferred)
- Use evaporation-rate nomographs to determine if additional curing measures are required based on temperature, wind velocity, and relative humidity
- Use Type II or Type III cement to accelerate early strength gain, reducing form removal time
- Consider supplementary cementitious materials (fly ash, slag) to reduce peak temperature during hydration, mitigating thermal cracking

## Consequences of Inadequate Curing

Concrete that is inadequately cured (insufficient moisture, duration, or temperature) exhibits:

- **Low early strength:** Reduced capacity to carry load; extended form removal time; increased risk of handling damage
- **High permeability:** Porous, permeable concrete allows water infiltration, causing corrosion of embedded steel (rebar), spalling, and pop-outs
- **Reduced durability:** Insufficient hydration leaves unreacted cement and weak hydration products; service life shortened from 50+ years to 20—30 years
- **Efflorescence:** Incomplete hydration leaves weak zones; water migration through these zones deposits salts on the surface
- **Differential cracking:** Uneven curing creates local strength variations; stress concentrates in weaker regions, leading to random cracking

**ACI 308R data:** Concrete cured for only 1 day (instead of 7) at 20°C develops approximately 50—60% of the intended 28-day strength. Inadequate curing is a false economy; proper curing prevents future repair costs.

## Transition from Curing to Finishing

After the curing period (7 days minimum), the concrete continues to gain strength (albeit at a slower rate) for months or even years. At 28 days, ordinary portland cement concrete typically reaches 90—100% of design strength; supplementary cementitious materials (fly ash, slag) continue gaining strength beyond 28 days.

Finishing, painting, and sealant application should generally be deferred until after 28 days to allow complete surface hydration. Membrane selears applied before the surface has fully hydrated may trap internal moisture and cause efflorescence or delayed deterioration.

## How It's Tested

The LEA expects you to:

1. Identify appropriate curing methods for specific applications (ponding for slabs, spray for walls, compound for exposed structures)
2. Apply ACI 308 minimum curing duration (7 days at >10°C for OPC, 3 days for Type III)
3. Determine curing requirements based on tropical Philippine temperature and humidity conditions
4. Understand why inadequate curing leads to permeability, durability loss, and field failures
5. Apply ASTM C309 curing compound specifications (Type 2 preferred for tropical climate)
6. Recognize evaporation-rate nomographs and when additional measures are required
7. Distinguish between effective and ineffective curing practices

Typical questions present a concrete placement scenario in the Philippines and ask what curing method is appropriate, or ask about consequences of skipping or shortening the curing period.

<Tip>In the tropical Philippines, white-pigmented curing compound (Type 2, ASTM C309) is often the most practical choice. It prevents evaporation, reflects solar heat, requires minimal daily maintenance, and provides adequate strength development when properly applied. Always verify complete coverage; thin or patchy application is ineffective.</Tip>
