# SPP Document 201: Standards of Professional Practice on Pre-Design Services

## Overview

**SPP Document 201** governs the **Pre-Design Services** of Registered and Licensed Architects (RLAs) in the Philippines. It is part of the 2010 Standards of Professional Practice (SPP), approved on 27 July 2010 as the Supplemental Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRR) of **Republic Act No. 9266**, known as "The Architecture Act of 2004." It replaces the earlier **1979 UAP Doc. 201**, which referred to these non-creative early-stage services under the mnemonic "EPASSSSP" (Economic Feasibility Studies, Project Financing, Architectural Programming, Site Selection & Analysis, Site Utilization & Land Use Studies, Space/Management Studies, and Promotional Services).

## Learning Goal

After reading this article, you should be able to:

Identify the scope, deliverables, and compensation method for SPP Doc 201 Pre-Design Services: the six pre-design phases (project definition, feasibility studies, site analysis, space programming, design guidelines, and consultant selection); the per-diem or fixed-fee compensation basis; and distinguish pre-design services from regular design services under SPP 202.
---
Pre-Design Services are fundamentally different from design and construction services. They involve **no design or construction work**; instead, they provide the analytical and informational foundation upon which design decisions will later be made. For this reason, they are frequently described as **non-creative work**, and their compensation follows cost-based methods (such as MDPE) rather than percentage-of-construction-cost methods used in SPP Document 202.

SPP Document 201 is promulgated as part of the IRR of R.A. No. 9266, making its observance **mandatory for all Registered and Licensed Architects (RLAs)** in the Philippines. Only an RLA — a natural person with a valid PRC Certificate of Registration and a renewable 3-year Professional Identification Card — may legally render pre-design services.

An **Architectural Firm (AF)** may also render these services, provided it is registered with the DTI (as a sole proprietorship) or with the SEC and PRC (as a professional partnership or architectural corporation), in full compliance with Sec. 37 of R.A. No. 9266. Foreign architects wishing to provide pre-design services in the Philippines for projects on Philippine soil must secure a **Temporary/Special Permit (TSP)** from the PRC and a work permit from the **Department of Labor and Employment (DoLE)**, and must work in collaboration with a local RLA.

## Why Engage an Architect Early?

The SPP explicitly states that it is **most advantageous to the Client to involve the Architect in the earliest stages of the project**. An experienced architect can provide the client with:

- **Objective project analysis** — independent of any contractor, supplier, or financier
- **Optimization of building needs** against available resources and constraints
- **Early identification of risks**, feasibility gaps, or regulatory hurdles
- **Informed decision-making** before large capital expenditures are committed to design or construction

Because pre-design services determine whether a project is viable at all, engaging an architect at this stage often prevents costly mistakes in later phases.

***

## Scope of Pre-Design Services

The Pre-Design Services cover **a broad line of architectural services ranging from initial problem identification** to activities that allow the Architect to initially conceptualize an array of architectural and allied solutions. The complete list of services nominally included under SPP Document 201 is as follows:

### 2.1 Consultation

When a Client calls upon the Architect to give **oral or written advice and direction**, to attend conferences, or to make evaluations and appraisals regarding a contemplated project and similar activities, the Architect renders valuable inputs **whether or not the Client ultimately pursues the project**. This service covers the broadest and most preliminary stage of client-architect engagement.

### 2.2 Pre-Feasibility Studies

These preliminary studies involve the **procurement, analysis, and use of secondary information** gathered for the project to aid the Client in early decision-making. They represent the Architect's initial assessment of a project's soundness, allowing the Client to promptly explore available or readily identifiable directions and options. **Researched, processed, and validated secondary data** (e.g., electronic sources, print references, statistics) are generally used for such studies — no primary data gathering is required at this stage.

### 2.3 Feasibility Studies

Detailed analysis of the project based on pre-feasibility studies to **determine the viability of a proposed development**. The studies set the project against present and future trends to forecast how it will perform over time. This service requires **primary data gathering and analysis** — including market research, surveys, interviews, cost projections, and financial modeling — making it a more comprehensive and resource-intensive undertaking than a pre-feasibility study.

### 2.4 Site Selection and Analysis

This service entails the **formulation of site criteria**, assistance to the Client in site evaluation, and analysis to **determine the most appropriate site/s** for a proposed project or building program. The architect evaluates multiple candidate locations based on criteria such as accessibility, zoning, utilities, topography, and development potential, then recommends the most suitable option.

### 2.5 Site Utilization and Land-Use Studies

The detailed analysis of the site involves the **identification of a site's development potentials** through the proper utilization of land. The analysis covers:

- The context of the site and its surrounding environment
- **Development controls** that apply to the site and its environs (e.g., zoning ordinances, floor-area ratios, setback requirements, easements)

This service determines **how much of the site can be developed**, in what configuration, and subject to what regulatory constraints — information essential for programming, massing, and project costing.

### 2.6 Architectural Research

Architectural research entails the **conduct of primary and secondary researches** to assemble facts used as a basis for conclusions. This may include benchmarking comparable projects, reviewing regulatory requirements, investigating building typologies, or analyzing construction industry trends as they pertain to the project at hand.

### 2.7 Architectural Programming

This is an **analytical problem-seeking process** that leads to the statement and identification of both horizontal and vertical requirements in offering a solution. It incorporates a **space program** with characterizations of the envisioned spaces — such as ambiance, cost range, functional relationships, and performance requirements.

Architectural programming is the bridge between the client's organizational needs and the physical building. Its output (the Program of Requirements or POR) becomes the primary reference document for the architect in SPP 202 Regular Design Services.

### 2.8 Space Planning

The Architect determines the **adequate size and appropriate configuration and assemblage** for a proposed project, considering the use, allocation, and interface of spaces for given activities. Space planning is done mainly through **primary data gathering**, such as:

- Interviews
- Consultations
- Interfaces and focus group discussions (FGDs)
- Space planning surveys
- Space audits

Subsequent analyses include **spatial layouts with stacking concepts**, particularly for multi-storey structures.

### 2.9 Space Management Studies

An analysis of the **space requirements of the project based on organizational structure and functional set-up**, pinpointing linkages and interaction of spaces. The formulation of the space program resulting from this study serves as the basis for the development of the architectural plan/design. While similar to space planning, space management studies focus more on **organizational flow** and departmental relationships, while space planning focuses on the physical dimensions and configurations.

### 2.10 Value Management

This technique is applied in the **cost management process to minimize the negative effect of simplified operations** associated with many cost-reduction programs. The goal of value management is to achieve an **unimpaired program at minimum cost** — meaning that a plan, design, or system that has been successfully value-managed will still satisfy the same performance criteria as costlier alternatives. This is distinct from mere cost-cutting, which may compromise function or quality.

### 2.11 Design Brief Preparation

Under design brief preparation, the Architect states the **project terms of reference (ToR)**, including the concept, objectives, and other necessary requirements to **bid out architectural services** — whether for public or private sector projects. This document, once complete, becomes the basis for inviting and evaluating other architectural firms for comparative selection, as described in SPP Annex "A."

### 2.12 Promotional Services

Projects may require promotional activities in order to **develop and generate financial support and acceptance** from governing agencies or from the general public. In such cases, the Architect acts as the **agent of the Owner**, producing and coordinating the additional activities necessary to complete the services. Throughout such activities, the Architect must maintain professional status as the representative of the Owner.

## Manner of Providing Services

### Proposal Submission

After the initial meeting, conversation, or correspondence with the Client, **the Architect must submit a written proposal for pre-design services** stating the following:

1. **Scope of Work** — clearly identifying which pre-design services will be rendered
2. **Manner of Payment** — specifying the compensation method and schedule
3. **Owner's Responsibilities** — what the client must provide (data, access, information, approvals)
4. **Other Conditions of Services** — timelines, confidentiality, use of outputs, etc.

### How Services Are Delivered

The architect may render pre-design services in any of the following three ways:

**3.2.1 As an Individual Architect**
The architect must have special training and be knowledgeable in different fields to supplement his or her skills. This is appropriate for smaller, less complex pre-design engagements.

**3.2.2 Architect's Own Staff**
Architects working within a single firm may specialize in different aspects of pre-design — research, programming, spatial analysis — without losing the generalist orientation of the firm. This is suitable for medium to large-scale pre-design engagements.

**3.2.3 By Association, Consultation, or Networking**
Consultation between the Architect and firms of other disciplines (e.g., economists, environmental planners, market researchers) under the **extended terms of the Owner-Architect Agreement** is a common practice for complex, multi-sector pre-design work. The architect remains the lead professional and coordinator of all inputs.

## Method of Compensation

The Architect's compensation for Pre-Design Services is based on the architect's or firm's **tleants, skill, experience, imagination, and the type and level of professional services provided**. Unlike SPP 202 Regular Design Services, **Pre-Design Services do not use a percentage of Project Construction Cost (PCC)** as a compensation basis, because there is no project construction cost established at this stage.

Compensation for Pre-Design Services may be based on one or more of the following five methods:

***

### Method 1: Multiple of Direct Personnel Expenses (MDPE)

The **most commonly used method for pre-design services**. It is a cost-based method applicable to non-creative work such as accounting, secretarial services, research, data gathering, and preparation of reports. This method does not account for creative work, since the value of creative design cannot be measured by time spent.

**Formula:**

Let:

- \(A\) = Architect's rate per hour
- \(C\) = Consultant's rate per hour
- \(T\) = Rate per hour of Technical Staff, Researchers, and others
- \(AN\), \(CN\), \(TN\) = Number of hours spent by Architect, Consultants, and Technical Staff respectively
- \(M\) = Multiplier to account for overhead and reasonable profit (ranges from **1.5 to 2.5**)
- \(R\) = Reimbursable expenses

\[
\text\{Direct Cost\} = (A \times AN) + (C \times CN) + (T \times TN)
\]

\[
\text\{Fee\} = \text\{Direct Cost\} \times M
\]

\[
\text\{Total Cost to Client\} = \text\{Fee\} + R
\]

The multiplier M (ranging from 1.5 to 2.5) depends on the **office set-up, overhead, and experience of the Architect**, and the **complexity of the project**.

**Reimbursable Expenses (R) include:**
- Transportation, living, and housing allowances for foreign consultants
- Out-of-town living and housing allowances for local consultants and technical staff **assigned to places over 100 km** from the architect's area of operation
- Cost of printing extra sets of drawings, reports, maps, and contract documents over the five copies submitted to the Client
- Overseas and long-distance calls
- Technical and laboratory tests, licenses, fees, and taxes

### Method 2: Professional Fee Plus Expenses

This method is frequently used **where there is a continuing relationship** involving a series of projects. It establishes a **fixed sum over and above the reimbursement** for the Architect's technical time and overhead. An agreement on the general scope of work is necessary to set an equitable fee.

***

### Method 3: Lump Sum or Fixed Fee

This method may be applied to **government projects**, since they entail more paperwork and time-consuming processes. The fee is agreed upon upfront as a single fixed amount for the defined scope of work. This method provides certainty for both parties but requires that the scope be clearly defined at the outset.

***

### Method 4: Per Diem, Honorarium Plus Reimbursable Expenses

In cases where a Client requests an architect to perform work requiring personal time — such as:

- Attending project-related meetings, conferences, or trips
- Conducting ocular inspection of possible project sites
- Conferring with others regarding prospective investments or ventures

For these activities, the architect (acting as agent of the Owner) may be paid on a **per diem and honorarium basis**, plus **out-of-pocket expenses** such as travel, accommodations, and subsistence.

***

### Method 5: Mixed Methods of Compensation

The SPP provides for **more than one method of compensation** on a project. Each project should be examined to determine the most appropriate and equitable method, and it is common for a complex pre-design engagement to combine, for example, MDPE for research phases and a lump sum for the feasibility report.

The outputs of SPP 201 — particularly the feasibility studies, site analysis, and architectural program — form the **primary inputs to the Project Definition Phase of SPP 202**. Without sound pre-design work, the architect entering the design phase lacks the data needed to produce a viable, cost-appropriate, and regulatory-compliant building design.

When a project requires Comprehensive Architectural Services under **SPP Doc. 206**, Pre-Design Services are already included as the first component of that full-service package.

## Practical Notes for Architects and Clients

**For Architects:**
- Always formalize pre-design engagements with a written proposal even for seemingly minor consultations, as the Architect's time and expertise have professional and commercial value regardless of whether the client proceeds.
- Clearly distinguish **pre-feasibility** (secondary data, lower cost, faster) from **feasibility** (primary data, higher cost, comprehensive) in proposals, since clients often conflate the two.
- The MDPE multiplier (1.5–2.5) must be disclosed upfront; never apply a multiplier without the client's prior agreement.
- Pre-design services are **not covered by the building permit** process; they are entirely contractual between the architect and the client.

**For Clients / Project Proponents:**
- Engaging an RLA for pre-design services — especially feasibility studies and site analysis — before committing to land purchase or project financing is best practice and can prevent costly errors.
- Owners must understand that they are responsible for providing the architect with access to data, documents, and the project site as part of their obligations under the Owner-Architect Agreement.
- Outputs (reports, studies, design briefs) produced under SPP 201 are the **intellectual property of the Architect** under Sec. 33 of R.A. No. 9266 and must not be reproduced or used without authorization.
