## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal

Identify the ondol (underfloor heating) system of Korean architecture and explain how it shaped interior planning, social use of space, and the elevated floor culture, and recognize key Korean architectural types (hanok, temple, palace).
## Core Concept

Korean architecture synthesizes Chinese and Japanese traditions while responding to continental climate extremes and particular resource conditions. The signature achievement is the **ondol** underfloor heating system: hot smoke from a kitchen fire travels through stone or clay channels beneath the floor before exiting through an external chimney, warming interior rooms and enabling the use of combustible flooring (straw mats, wooden planks) in winter. This technical innovation restructures domestic space: the ondol floor rises slightly above ground level, requiring step-down entrances and reconfigured furniture. Inhabitants sit, sleep, and work on the warm floor rather than standing; furniture minimizes; spatial flexibility increases. Ondol heating allowed Korea to maintain timber-frame, light-partition architecture in a climate far colder than China or Japan, fundamentally shaping residential typology.

<Tip>
**Ondol = underfloor heating using smoke channels = sitting culture.** When the heating system defines interior use, people work and sleep on warm floors rather than standing or using furniture. This one innovation shapes every aspect of interior organization.
</Tip>

The **hanok** (traditional house) combines ondol with wood-frame structural logic adapted from Chinese dougong-like bracketing and Japanese tatami modularity, but calibrated to Korean proportions and landscape integration. A hanok sits on a raised stone or rammed-earth foundation, supporting wooden posts that interlock without nails. The main living space features an ondol-heated floor; the kitchen (with its heating hearth) sits adjacent, sometimes lower and unheated. Walls are plaster over timber and lattice. Roofs curve upward at eaves (like Chinese and Japanese models) but with gentler pitch suited to snow load and monsoon moisture. The house orients south-facing for solar gain in winter, hugging hillside slopes for wind protection. Interior room sizes follow proportional modules—though not strictly tatami-based like Japan—with sliding doors and wooden lattice screens allowing flexible subdivision. The layout privileges the master's room (often positioned to receive maximum ondol heat), with family quarters and servant spaces hierarchically arranged.

<Note>
Hanok's proportional modules differ from Japanese tatami. While both cultures use modular systems, Korean proportions reflect different climate needs (deeper overhangs for monsoon) and resource conditions (different timber availability). They are not interchangeable systems.
</Note>

**Gyeongbokgung Palace** (14th century, Seoul), the first major palace of the Joseon Dynasty, demonstrates state-scale application of Korean principles: a sprawling complex organized along a north-south axis (borrowed from Chinese imperial tradition) but modified for Korean topography and climate. The main throne hall (Geunjeongjeon) features ondol-heated chambers beneath ceremonial floors. Residential quarters (Gangnyeongjeon) integrate ondol for imperial comfort. The palace combines Chinese axial planning with Korean underfloor heating, Japanese-like timber refinement, and Korean site integration (built into natural terrain rather than imposing rigid geometry).

The aesthetic principle guiding Korean architecture is restraint and site responsiveness: buildings nestle into landscape; decorative elements minimize compared to China; spatial proportions emphasize usable comfort over cosmological symbolism. Timber quality, joinery precision, and site integration define status, not glazed tile or gilt.

<Warn>
Korean restraint is not poverty or simplicity—it is *sophisticated craft*. Do not mistake the absence of ornament for lack of status. Fine timber, precise joinery, and landscape integration are the visible marks of wealth and power in Korean architecture, where Chinese or Japanese traditions display gilding and glazed tile.
</Warn>

## Key Terms

- **Ondol (underfloor heating)**: Korean heating system where hot smoke from a kitchen hearth travels through channels beneath the floor before exiting through an external chimney, warming interior spaces and enabling comfortable winter living.
- **Hanok**: Traditional Korean timber-frame house, typically featuring ondol-heated floors, plaster walls over timber lattice, south-facing orientation, raised stone foundation, flexible interior partitions, and site-specific landscape integration.
- **Geunjeongjeon**: Main throne hall of Gyeongbokgung Palace, the ceremonial center where the monarch conducts state affairs and receives officials.
- **Gangnyeongjeon**: Royal residence quarters within Joseon palaces, featuring ondol-heated private chambers for the royal family, distinct from public ceremonial halls.
- **Stone foundation (platform)**: Raised base of stone or rammed earth supporting wooden posts in Korean and other East Asian architecture, elevating structures for drainage, protection from moisture, and visual prominence.
- **Timber-frame structure (mok-gou): Wooden post-and-beam construction without central nails, using interlocking joinery and load-bearing brackets, adapted from Chinese dougong principles but refined for Korean proportions and climate.
- **Plaster wall (todae)**: Exterior and interior walls constructed of timber lattice (wooden grid) covered with mud or clay plaster, providing insulation, fire resistance, and weather protection in Korean residential architecture.
- **South-facing orientation**: Deliberately siting buildings to face south, maximizing winter solar gain for ondol-heated interiors while maintaining wind protection from north and maintaining landscape views.
- **Flexible partitions (ajilsan)**: Interior sliding screens and removable wooden lattice dividers allowing quick reconfiguration of room sizes and functions within hanok houses.
- **Joseon Dynasty**: Korean royal house ruling 1392–1910, responsible for establishing state architecture (palaces, temples) and codifying hanok residential traditions; period of high cultural and architectural achievement.
