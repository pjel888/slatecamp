# Site grading

## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal
Read contour lines, calculate slope, classify grading difficulty from checked source ranges, and plan surface drainage while minimizing disturbance to existing landforms and site features.

Grading reshapes the site so buildings, access, drainage, and outdoor spaces can work together. The checked construction source defines topography as the configuration of surface features that influences where and how to build. Designers study topography through site sections or contour lines.

Contour lines connect points of equal elevation. Widely spaced contours indicate a relatively flat or gently sloping surface. Equally spaced contours indicate a constant slope. Closely spaced contours indicate a steep rise. Contours pointing toward lower elevations represent ridges; contours pointing toward higher elevations represent valleys.

The source gives usable grading guidance: slopes up to 5 percent are usable for most outdoor activities and are relatively easy to build on; 5 percent to 10 percent slopes are suitable for informal outdoor activities and can be built on without too much difficulty; slopes over 10 percent are challenging and more expensive; slopes over 25 percent are subject to erosion and difficult to build on.

Slope percentage equals elevation gain divided by horizontal distance, multiplied by 100. For example, a 4 meter elevation change over 40 meters of horizontal distance is 10 percent.

Surface drainage is the grading and surfacing of a site to divert rain and surface water into natural drainage patterns or a municipal storm sewer system. The general site-development intent is to minimize disturbance of existing landforms and features while taking advantage of natural slopes and microclimate.

## Key Takeaways

Use contour spacing and slope formula before choosing cut, fill, or drainage moves.

Do not import accessibility ramp or parking gradient values unless the question supplies them or the checked source packet supports them.

Good grading protects drainage, buildability, access, and existing site features; it is not simply flattening the whole site.
