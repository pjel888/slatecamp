# Modern Filipino Identity

## Layered Identity

Modern Filipino architectural identity is best read as a layered result of climate, materials, colonial exchange, professional institutions, and twentieth-century modern practice. The checked Filipino Architecture source describes Philippine character through tropical architecture, openness, transparency, lightness, schools, and professional organizations. It also records the pre-Spanish bahay kubo as a wood, bamboo, and palm-leaf dwelling adapted to the tropical climate.

## From Vernacular To Hybrid Forms

The same source connects Bahay na Bato to the Bahay Kubo through tropical-house traits: steep hip roof, post-and-lintel construction, light and airy structure, elevated living quarters, economy of materials, and flowing spaces. HOA Module 4 P1 describes Bahay na Bato as a hybrid of indigenous and Hispanic traditions, built partly of wood and partly of stone.

## Modern And Professional Anchors

For the American and Commonwealth periods, the local sources list Daniel Burnham for Manila and Baguio city plans, William Parsons, Juan Arellano, Tomas Mapua, Juan Nakpil, Pablo Antonio, and Leandro Locsin. The checked sources identify Juan Nakpil as first National Artist for Architecture, Pablo Antonio as second, and Leandro Locsin as third. They also identify works such as the Philippine Stock Exchange and the Cultural Center of the Philippines with Locsin.

## LEA Takeaway

Treat modern Filipino identity as continuity plus adaptation: climate-responsive dwelling types, Spanish-era hybrid construction, American-period professionalization, and modern Filipino architects. Avoid reducing it to a single style label.
