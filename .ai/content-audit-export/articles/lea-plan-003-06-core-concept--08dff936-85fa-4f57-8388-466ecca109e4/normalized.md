## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal
Distinguish in-situ upgrading from relocation under RA 7279, describe the SHFC Community Mortgage Program (CMP) as the practical upgrading model, apply eviction protections, and quantify the scale of Philippine informal settlements.

Informal settlements in the Philippines—commonly called squatter communities, slums, or "informal colonies"—house an estimated 2–3 million people in Metro Manila alone. **RA 7279** establishes the legal framework for addressing informal settlements, while **SHFC's Community Mortgage Program (CMP)** represents the practical model for in-situ upgrading. Understanding the distinction between in-situ upgrading and relocation, the scale of informal settlements, and the legal protections and constraints is critical.

## Core Concept

An informal settlement is an unplanned community occupying land without the occupants' legal ownership or formal tenancy rights. These settlements typically develop where formal housing is unaffordable or unavailable—urban fringe areas, public lands, private marginal lands, and especially riverbanks and drainage easements. The Philippines' rapid urbanization (cities growing 3–4% annually) combined with a housing shortage has driven explosive informal settlement expansion.

**Scale and Characteristics:** Metro Manila has roughly 2–3 million informal dwellers (20–30% of the metro population). Settlements range from spontaneous unplanned neighborhoods (many with internal community organization) to dense informal colonies with thousands of units. Typologically, informal settlements exist on esteros (drainage canals), under bridges, on riverbanks, on steep hillsides, and on private/public marginal lands.

**Danger Areas and Eviction Risk:** **RA 7279 Sec 28** defines danger areas where occupants face elevated eviction risk: esteros and drainage canals; riverbanks; railroad tracks; under bridges; power line easements; fault zones; and other areas declared dangerous by the HLURB (now DHSUD) or local government. The specific distances, however, come from other laws:

- **Within 3 meters** of drainage canals/esteros with constructed banks (**PD 1067 Water Code** easement)
- **20 meters from the edge of the bank** of natural waterways in urban areas; **40 meters** in agricultural/rural areas (**PD 1067 Sec 59**)
- **Within 50 meters** of railroad tracks (danger from trains)
- Under bridges
- Within power line easements
- Within active fault zones (PHIVOLCS mapping)

Importantly, occupants in danger areas can still be evicted but (unlike non-danger-area evictions) are not entitled to relocation by the government. This distinction is critical: all evictions under RA 7279 require a **30-day notice**; danger areas differ only in removing the relocation obligation (not the notice requirement).

**In-Situ Upgrading vs. Relocation:** RA 7279 and later policy experience favor in-situ upgrading (improving settlements where they stand) over relocation (moving communities to new sites). In-situ preserves livelihood networks, community institutions, and social capital. **SHFC's Community Mortgage Program (CMP)** operationalizes this: communities collectively mortgage land to formalize tenure and then upgrade incrementally. Relocation is reserved for cases where danger is imminent and no in-situ alternative exists.

**Slum Characteristics Beyond Housing:** While informal settlements are defined by tenure insecurity, the term "slum" often connotes additional deprivations: inadequate water/sanitation, overcrowding, poor infrastructure, and lack of basic services. Slum upgrading addresses these multi-dimensional deprivations, not just housing formalization.

## Philippine Context

The Philippines' informal settlement crisis emerged from a combination of factors: rapid migration to cities (urbanization rate ~3.5% annually), formal housing unaffordability (median house prices exceed 6–8 years of median household income), and limited government production capacity. By 2000s, informal settlements had become the de facto housing solution for the urban poor, with an estimated 70% of urban poor living informally.

Policy shifted dramatically post-1992 with **RA 7279 (UDHA)**. Instead of mass demolition-relocation (the pre-1990s approach), RA 7279 emphasized community rights, livelihood preservation, and in-situ alternatives. **SHFC's Community Mortgage Program**, launched in 1989 and expanding post-1992, became the flagship in-situ upgrading tool. CMP has formalized tenure for ~500,000+ families as of 2020s, demonstrating viability of the in-situ approach.

However, implementation remains inconsistent. Many local government units (LGUs) still resort to relocation without adequate planning or livelihood assessment. Post-disaster relocation (especially post-Yolanda 2013, with its 40m coastal no-build zone) has created new informal settlements when relocated residents lack livelihood access.

## How It's Tested

**Pattern 1 — Danger Area Eviction:** "Occupants in a settlement within 5 meters of an estero can be evicted. Do they have a right to government relocation?" → NO (danger areas exempt from relocation obligation). But "What notice is required?" → 30 days (all evictions require notice).

**Pattern 2 — Upgrading vs. Relocation:** "Why is in-situ upgrading preferred to relocation?" → Preserves livelihood, community, social capital. Relocation causes economic disruption.

**Pattern 3 — RA 7279 Framework:** "What law establishes the eviction framework and relocation rights?" → RA 7279. "What is a danger area?" → The specific danger area categories listed in RA 7279 Sec 28, with distances sourced from PD 1067 and other laws.
