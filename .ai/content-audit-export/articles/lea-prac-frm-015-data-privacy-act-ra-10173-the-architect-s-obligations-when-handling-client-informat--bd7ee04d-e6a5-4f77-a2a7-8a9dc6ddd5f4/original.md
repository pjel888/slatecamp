## Data Privacy Act (RA 10173)—The Architect's Obligations When Handling Client Information

The Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173) protects personal information in the hands of both public and private sector organizations. When architects design buildings, they collect sensitive information about client operations, financial capacity, occupant numbers, security arrangements, and facility use patterns. Architects must treat this information as personal data and comply with RA 10173 obligations to protect it.

### Scope of RA 10173: Personal Information Controllers and Processors

RA 10173 defines a _personal information controller_ (PIC) as an entity that decides the purpose and manner of processing personal data. A _personal information processor_ (PIP) is an entity that processes data on behalf of a PIC. In typical architectural practice, the client (owner or facility manager) is the PIC, and the architect may be a PIP if processing data at the client's direction.

However, architects sometimes act as PICs—for example, when maintaining a database of building occupants for emergency egress planning, or collecting tenant information for a mixed-use development. In both roles, architects have legal obligations to protect data and implement reasonable security measures.

### Key Obligations Under RA 10173

The law imposes several core obligations on PICs and PIPs. First, _transparency_: the data subject (occupant or tenant) must be informed what personal information is collected, why, and how it will be used. Second, _purpose limitation_: data collected for one purpose cannot be used for another without consent. Third, _data security_: the organization must implement reasonable measures to prevent unauthorized access, disclosure, alteration, or destruction.

Fourth, _data retention_: personal information must not be kept longer than necessary for the stated purpose. Building emergency plans may require occupant lists; once the building is demolished or the tenant leaves, this data should be deleted. Fifth, _breach notification_: if personal data is compromised, the PIC must notify the National Privacy Commission and affected individuals without undue delay, as per NPC guidelines.

### Reasonable Security Measures Architects Must Implement

"Reasonable security" is not absolutely defined but requires measures proportionate to the sensitivity of the data and the risk of unauthorized access. For architects, reasonable measures include: (1) limiting access to sensitive project information to authorized personnel only; (2) encrypting digital files containing personal or confidential client information; (3) securing physical files in locked cabinets with restricted key access; (4) requiring confidentiality agreements from staff; (5) implementing access logs to track who accessed sensitive files; and (6) securely disposing of information when no longer needed (shredding paper, securely deleting digital files).

For BIM models containing detailed site and occupancy information, architects should restrict access to the project team, require login credentials, and avoid storing sensitive data on public cloud services without encryption. Client facility plans should not be posted on promotional websites without permission.

### Privacy Considerations in Project Delivery

When architects conduct site investigations, they may photograph occupants, measure occupied spaces, or document existing conditions that reveal operational information. Before collecting such data, architects should inform the client and document the client's consent. Design charrettes and programming sessions may elicit sensitive information about client operations, financial constraints, or strategic plans. This information is confidential and must not be disclosed to third parties or competitors.

During construction administration, architects may observe security weaknesses, occupancy patterns, or operational issues. While architects have a duty to report code violations or safety hazards, they should not disclose operational information that goes beyond what is necessary to address the specific issue. For example, identifying that a security camera placement is ineffective is relevant; disclosing the entire security system layout to unrelated parties is not.

### Duty to Implement Data Protection Officer Requirements

RA 10173 requires designation of an individual(s) accountable for compliance; large architectural firms processing significant personal data should assess if a Data Protection Officer (DPO) is required per NPC guidelines. The DPO oversees data protection compliance, fields privacy complaints, and ensures the organization meets legal obligations. Firms should assess whether they process large-scale data or sensitive information of occupants, tenants, employees, or building users. If so, appointing a DPO (internal or external) demonstrates legal compliance and professional responsibility.

### Client Information and Intellectual Property Rights

Architects must distinguish between intellectual property in the design (which the architect typically retains copyright to) and the client's confidential business information. The client's operational data, financial information, and facility requirements are the client's property. The architect's copyright extends to the design expression (plans, drawings, specifications) but not to the underlying information the client provided.

Service agreements should clarify: (1) what project information is confidential; (2) what use the architect may make of project information for professional development, case studies, or awards submissions; (3) whether the architect may photograph or publicize the finished building; and (4) under what circumstances the architect may share project information with consultants, contractors, or third parties.

### Enforcement and Penalties

The National Privacy Commission (NPC) enforces RA 10173. Violations can result in administrative fines, orders to cease processing, and directives to delete unlawfully held data. In extreme cases, criminal penalties apply for willful and malicious processing. A data breach that the architect fails to report can expose the architect and the firm to liability both to the NPC and to affected individuals.

Professional disciplinary bodies (Board of Architecture) may also view serious privacy violations as grounds for disciplinary action under the Code of Ethical Conduct. An architect who negligently disclosed a client's confidential information or failed to protect data adequately could face complaints alleging breach of professional ethics.

### Practical Compliance Checklist for Architects

Architects should implement the following: (1) maintain a data inventory documenting what personal information is collected, why, and how long it is retained; (2) draft privacy notices to inform data subjects; (3) obtain written consent before collecting sensitive data; (4) implement confidentiality agreements with staff; (5) encrypt sensitive files; (6) restrict access to authorized personnel only; (7) maintain audit trails; (8) secure disposal of information when no longer needed; (9) have a breach response plan; and (10) provide staff training on data protection obligations.
