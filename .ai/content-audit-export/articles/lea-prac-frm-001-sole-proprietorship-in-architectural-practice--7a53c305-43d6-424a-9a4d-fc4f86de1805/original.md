## Sole Proprietorship in Architectural Practice

A sole proprietorship is an individual architect practicing and delivering architectural services, duly registered with the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI), Board of Architecture (BOA), and Professional Regulation Commission (PRC). This structure is the simplest form of architectural practice under RA 9266. The architect maintains complete ownership and control of the practice.

The sole proprietor must hold a valid Certificate of Registration and Professional Identification Card. The architect is personally responsible for all debts, liabilities, and obligations of the practice. This unlimited personal liability is a defining characteristic that distinguishes sole proprietorships from corporate structures. Annual registration and renewal are required with all regulatory bodies.

### Ownership and Control Structure

In a sole proprietorship, the individual architect retains 100 percent ownership of the practice. All professional decisions, client relationships, and project management fall under the architect's direct authority. The architect may employ other architects, engineers, or administrative staff, but remains the principal responsible party. Profit and loss flow directly to the owner.

The architect maintains exclusive rights to the architectural documents, plans, and specifications produced. These materials constitute intellectual property owned solely by the proprietor unless otherwise stipulated in writing. The architect may assign work to employees, but the architect-of-record remains fully responsible for all architectural documents issued under the architect's seal.

### Liability and Continuity Implications

Personal liability in a sole proprietorship extends to all professional negligence, breach of contract, and civil claims arising from architectural services. The architect's personal assets are exposed to judgment claims without corporate protection. Insurance becomes critical—professional liability coverage protects both the architect and clients. Registration of the sole proprietorship name with the DTI provides basic legal recognition but does not shield personal liability.

Continuity of the practice depends entirely on the architect's ability and willingness to continue. Death, incapacity, or retirement of the sole proprietor terminates the practice unless ownership is transferred. There is no automatic succession mechanism, making succession planning essential. Clients must be notified of any change in the architect-of-record, and new registration may be required for ongoing projects.

### Advantages and Disadvantages

Advantages include minimal startup costs, complete autonomy in business decisions, simpler registration procedures, and direct receipt of all profits. The architect maintains full control over project selection, fee structures, and client relationships. No corporate formalities or board meetings are required. Administrative burden is lighter than partnership or corporate structures.

Disadvantages include unlimited personal liability, limited capacity to expand operations, difficulty attracting investment capital, and vulnerability to personal creditors. A single architect may lack specialized expertise across all architectural disciplines. The practice cannot continue beyond the architect's involvement, creating succession challenges. Health issues or personal circumstances can jeopardize client relationships and ongoing projects.
