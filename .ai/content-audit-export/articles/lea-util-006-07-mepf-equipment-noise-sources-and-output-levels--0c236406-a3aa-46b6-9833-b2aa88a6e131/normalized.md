## What You Need to Know for the LEA

Noise control in MEPF work starts with basic architectural acoustics: sound is a pressure wave, the decibel scale is logarithmic, room finishes affect reflection and absorption, and vibration can transmit through structure, pipes, ducts, walls, floors, and ceilings.

## Sound and Decibels

The checked lighting and acoustics handout describes sound as a longitudinal pressure wave in air or another elastic medium. It places the audible sound range from about 0 dB to 140 dB and the frequency range from about 15 Hz to 20,000 Hz.

Salvan's acoustics text explains why decibels are used: the ear responds logarithmically, not arithmetically, to sound pressure and intensity. The text gives the useful rule that doubling sound intensity raises the level by about 3 dB, while doubling distance from a source reduces intensity level by about 6 dB in the free field. For exam problems, never add decibel values by ordinary arithmetic unless the item tells you to use a simplified comparison.

## Noise Sources in MEPF Design

MEPF equipment becomes an architectural issue because motors, fans, pumps, compressors, generators, air movement, and duct or pipe vibration can transmit sound into occupied spaces. The source-safe exam task is to identify the path:

- Airborne sound leaves the equipment and travels through air.
- Structure-borne vibration travels through concrete, steel, wood, glass, supports, pipes, or ducts.
- Flanking paths bypass the obvious wall or partition.
- Reverberation increases persistence of sound inside the room.

The article's practical focus is therefore not a memorized product table. It is matching a control method to the transmission path.

## Absorption, Blocking, and Damping

The checked handout states that building materials affect how sound is reflected, absorbed, or transmitted. Absorptive materials reduce reflected sound and reverberation inside a room. High-density vinyl barrier material is described as blocking sound transmission through walls, floors, and ceilings. The same material may be used as pipe and duct wrap to damp vibration and reduce noise.

Choose the treatment by problem type:

- Excess reflected noise in a room: add absorptive finishes or ceiling treatment.
- Transmission through partitions: improve the barrier path through mass, sealing, or sound-blocking material.
- Pipe or duct vibration: wrap, isolate, or damp the vibrating element.
- Equipment vibration: separate the equipment from the structure with appropriate isolation and flexible connections.

## Reverberation

Reverberation is the prolongation of sound after the source stops. The checked handout states that reverberation time depends on room volume and the rate at which sound energy is absorbed by wall surfaces and objects in the room. It also identifies RT60 as the time for the sound level to drop 60 dB.

The handout gives the Sabine relationship:

`RT60 = 0.049 V / a`

where `V` is room volume in cubic feet and `a` is total room absorption in sabins. Total absorption depends on surface area and the absorption coefficient or NRC of materials.

## LEA Focus

For board-style questions, read the noise item in this order:

1. Identify whether the problem is airborne sound, structure-borne vibration, reverberation, or flanking transmission.
2. Identify the receiver: office, classroom, corridor, residence, auditorium, or equipment room.
3. Choose a treatment that acts on the correct path.
4. Use decibel logic carefully: 3 dB relates to doubled intensity, and 6 dB relates to doubled distance in the free field.

The best answer is the one that connects source, path, receiver, and treatment without inventing equipment noise values that are not in the checked source family.
