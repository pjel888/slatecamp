## The Real-World Hook

The call comes at 2:47 in the morning. A fire has broken out in the basement parking level of a 20-storey residential condominium in Quezon City. By the time the Bureau of Fire Protection arrives, the blaze has been burning for nearly an hour. Yet the lobby above is still intact. The structural columns are holding. The floor slabs show stress but have not collapsed. Residents on the upper floors are still evacuating safely through the stairwells, which remain passable. The building will survive.

The building's architect, now awake and watching the news, knows why: the reinforced concrete columns in the parking basement had a minimum cross-sectional dimension of 450 mm — the standard required for four-hour fire resistance under PD 1096 Rule VI, Sec. 603. The floor slabs between the parking level and the lobby were 150 mm deep solid reinforced concrete with 25 mm of concrete cover to the reinforcement — the four-hour floor assembly. The exit stairwells were enclosed in 175 mm solid concrete partitions — also satisfying the four-hour partition standard. Every structural element was designed not just to carry gravity loads, but to carry those loads through fire.

Contrast this with a fire in a light industrial building across town. The steel columns had no fire protection — the owner had skipped the fireproofing to save money on the construction budget. In under twenty minutes, the unprotected steel reached temperatures that reduced its yield strength to less than half its design capacity. The roof structure deformed. One column buckled. The roof collapsed. No one was seriously hurt only because the warehouse was empty at that hour, but the entire building was gone in forty minutes. The structure that cost millions of pesos to build had zero residual fire resistance because the 12 mm of spray-applied vermiculite plaster that would have protected it for at least one hour — at a tiny fraction of the construction cost — had been omitted.

Fire-resistive construction is the principle that buildings should be designed not just to resist gravity and seismic loads, but to resist fire for a specified period of time — long enough for occupants to evacuate, for firefighters to establish control, and for the structural system to avoid progressive collapse. It is the difference between a building that confines a fire and a building that becomes one.

Rule VI of PD 1096 provides the legal framework, the governing definitions, and the technical standards that make fire-resistive construction quantifiable and enforceable. It is the "how" behind the "what" that Rule V established: Rule V declares that you need two-hour exterior walls in a Fire Restrictive Zone; Rule VI tells you exactly how thick those walls must be and from which materials they must be made. Rule V sets the performance requirement; Rule VI specifies the pathway to compliance.

For the Architecture Licensure Examination, Rule VI is among the most data-intensive topics tested. The key numbers — minimum thicknesses in millimeters for different assemblies at different time ratings — appear repeatedly in board exam questions. These are not numbers to estimate; they are numbers to know precisely, because the exam will present options that are close but specifically wrong. The strategy for this topic: understand the logic behind the numbers so deeply that memorization becomes a byproduct of comprehension rather than a separate effort.

## Core Concepts

### Principle 1: Fire-Resistive Rating — The Performance Definition (Sec. 601)

Per PD 1096, Rule VI, Sec. 601, **fire-resistive rating** is defined as:

> The degree to which a material can withstand fire as determined by generally recognized and accepted testing methods.

This definition deserves careful unpacking because it establishes the philosophical foundation for everything that follows in Rule VI.

**"Degree to which a material can withstand fire"** — fire-resistive rating is a performance measure, not a prescriptive material classification. Two assemblies made of different materials can achieve the same rating if they perform equivleantly in a standardized fire test. A 175 mm solid concrete partition and a 200 mm solid masonry partition both achieve a four-hour rating, even though they are different materials and different thicknesses. The rating is about what they can do, not what they are made of.

**"As determined by generally recognized and accepted testing methods"** — the Code does not name a specific test standard in Sec. 601 itself. This is deliberate: standards evolve, and the Code does not want its definitions to become outdated every time ASTM or ISO revises a test method. The phrase "generally recognized and accepted" encompasses the international consensus on fire testing methodology, including ASTM E119 (Standard Test Methods for Fire Tests of Building Construction and Materials) and UL 263 in the US context, and Section 604 of the same Rule empowers the Secretary to prescribe specific standards as they evolve.

**What standardized fire tests actually measure:** The standard time-temperature fire test subjects an assembly to a controlled fire following a standard time-temperature curve (temperature rises to approximately 538°C at 5 minutes, 704°C at 10 minutes, 843°C at 30 minutes, 927°C at 1 hour, 1010°C at 2 hours, and continues escalating). The assembly fails when:

1. The temperature on the unexposed face rises more than 139°C above its initial temperature (indicating heat transmission that could ignite adjacent combustibles), or
2. Flame or hot gases pass through the assembly (indicating a breach), or
3. The assembly can no longer support its design load (indicating structural failure).

The fire-resistive rating is the time elapsed before the first of these failure modes occurs.

**Exam distinction:** The definition in Sec. 601 defines what fire-resistive rating IS (a degree — a performance level). Section 602 defines how that degree is expressed (as a time period). These are two separate definitional sections, and the exam can ask for either one specifically. Do not conflate them.

### Principle 2: Fire-Resistive Time Period Rating — The Time Dimension (Sec. 602)

Per PD 1096, Rule VI, Sec. 602, **fire-resistive time period rating** is defined as:

> The length of time a material can withstand being burned, which may be one-hour, two-hours, four-hours, etc.

Where Sec. 601 answers the question "what does fire-resistive rating measure?", Sec. 602 answers "how is that measurement expressed?" The time period is the unit of measurement — the rating is expressed in hours.

Four standard time-period ratings are used in PD 1096: **1 hour, 2 hours, 3 hours, and 4 hours**. The "etc." in Sec. 602's language leaves the list open-ended, but these four are the operative values in the Sec. 603 standards table.

**What each time period is calibrated to accomplish:**

- **1 hour:** Designed for the minimum case — a small building with well-organized egress, good detection, and rapid BFP response. One hour is sufficient time for a fully occupied small building to evacuate and for the fire to be controlled before structural failure.

- **2 hours:** The baseline for most commercial and residential mid-rise construction. A two-hour rating accounts for larger floor plates, slower evacuation of upper floors, and the travel time for BFP apparatus in Metro Manila traffic. It is the most commonly required rating in Philippine urban construction.

- **3 hours:** Required for more critical structural elements and more demanding occupancies. Three hours accounts for major fires in large-floor-area buildings and provides a margin against suppression system failure.

- **4 hours:** The maximum standard in PD 1096. Required for the structural frames and columns of Type V buildings (the most fire-resistive construction classification) and for Highly Fire Restrictive Zone requirements. Four hours is designed to ensure that a building's structural skeleton survives even a fully-developed, fully-exhausted fire — a standard intended to prevent collapse that could endanger firefighters and adjacent properties long after occupants have evacuated.

**Exam note:** The phrase "which may be one-hour, two-hours, four-hours" in Sec. 602 conspicuously skips three hours. This is not an error — the "etc." captures it. But exam questions sometimes specifically test whether candidates know that three-hour ratings exist and are required for certain elements. Do not assume that only the four ratings mentioned in the Sec. 602 text itself are valid.

### Principle 3: The Fire-Resistive Standards Table — Full Analysis (Sec. 603)

Section 603 provides the technical data that architects must apply when designing fire-resistive assemblies. The complete table specifies minimum material thicknesses (in millimeters) for five categories of assembly across four time-period ratings.

---

**CATEGORY A: FLOORS**

**Solid Reinforced Concrete Slab:**

| Measurement | 4 hrs | 3 hrs | 2 hrs | 1 hr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cover to reinforcement | 25 mm | 25 mm | 20 mm | 15 mm |
| Overall depth | 150 mm | 150 mm | 125 mm | 100 mm |

Both measurements must be simultaneously satisfied. An RC slab must achieve both the required cover to reinforcement AND the required overall depth to qualify for a given rating.

The identical values for 3-hour and 4-hour ratings (both 150 mm depth, both 25 mm cover) mean that a slab designed to 3-hour standards automatically satisfies 4-hour standards — there is no dimensional difference between them. The additional fire resistance at 4 hours comes from the redundancy of thermal mass, not from an increase in prescribed dimensions.

**Why cover to reinforcement matters:** Steel reinforcing bars lose approximately 50% of their yield strength when their temperature reaches about 600°C. The concrete cover insulates the steel from the fire on the exposed face, slowing the rate of temperature rise in the rebar. Thicker cover means the steel takes longer to reach critical temperature, extending the assembly's fire-resistive rating. The progression from 15 mm (1 hr) to 20 mm (2 hrs) to 25 mm (3 and 4 hrs) reflects this thermal protection logic.

**Solid Prestressed Concrete:**

| Measurement | 4 hrs | 3 hrs | 2 hrs | 1 hr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cover to tendons | 65 mm | 50 mm | 40 mm | 25 mm |
| Overall depth | 150 mm | 150 mm | 125 mm | 100 mm |

The overall depth requirements for prestressed concrete match ordinary RC exactly. The dramatic difference is in the cover to tendons: 65 mm at 4 hours compared to 25 mm for ordinary RC. This is not a conservative rounding — it reflects fundamental material science.

High-strength prestressing steel (typically ASTM A416 Grade 270) used in prestressing tendons begins to lose significant strength at temperatures as low as 300°C — roughly half the temperature at which ordinary reinforcing steel degrades. This is because the manufacturing process that creates high-strength steel (cold-drawing or heat treatment) leaves the material more temperature-sensitive. A prestressed concrete assembly must therefore provide much more thermal insulation to its steel to achieve the same fire resistance rating as an ordinary RC assembly.

The ratio of prestressed to ordinary RC cover increases dramatically with higher ratings:
- 1 hour: 25 mm prestressed vs. 15 mm RC — ratio of 1.67
- 2 hours: 40 mm prestressed vs. 20 mm RC — ratio of 2.00
- 3 hours: 50 mm prestressed vs. 25 mm RC — ratio of 2.00
- 4 hours: 65 mm prestressed vs. 25 mm RC — ratio of 2.60

The cover ratio grows at 4 hours, meaning that at maximum fire exposure, prestressed tendons require disproportionately more protection than ordinary reinforcement. This is a key exam distinction.

---

**CATEGORY B: PARTITIONS**

| Material | 4 hrs | 3 hrs | 2 hrs | 1 hr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solid concrete | 175 mm | 175 mm | 150 mm | 125 mm |
| Solid masonry | 200 mm | 175 mm | 150 mm | 125 mm |
| Hollow unit masonry | 300 mm | 250 mm | 200 mm | 150 mm |

Three materials are listed for partitions, and the differences between them illustrate the principle that material density and homogeneity determine thermal efficiency.

**Solid concrete partitions** are the most efficient: 175 mm achieves both 3-hour and 4-hour ratings (identical values, same as RC slab floor). Solid concrete is a dense, homogeneous material with high thermal mass. Heat must conduct through the full 175 mm thickness before reaching the unexposed face.

**Solid masonry partitions** require slightly more thickness: 200 mm at 4 hours (vs. 175 mm for solid concrete). Masonry — whether brick, block, or stone — is denser and more discontinuous than poured concrete, with mortar joints that can have slightly different thermal properties than the masonry units themselves. The 25 mm additional thickness at the 4-hour level compensates for this slight reduction in thermal efficiency.

**Hollow unit masonry** requires dramatically more thickness: 300 mm at 4 hours — 71% more than solid concrete. Hollow concrete masonry units (CMUs) contain air voids — typically 30–50% of the total cross-sectional area. These voids interrupt the solid thermal mass, creating pathways for heat transfer through convection and radiation within the hollow cells. The wall must be substantially thicker to compensate. However, hollow unit masonry is often lighter and less expensive per unit of wall area than solid alternatives, and many designers accept the additional thickness as a trade-off for economy.

---

**CATEGORY C: PROTECTION FOR METAL**

| Protection Material | 4 hrs | 3 hrs | 2 hrs | 1 hr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Concrete | 75 mm | 50 mm | 38 mm | 25 mm |
| Masonry | 100 mm | 75 mm | 55 mm | 38 mm |
| Metal lath with vermiculite/perlite gypsum plaster | 50 mm | 38 mm | 20 mm | 12 mm |

This category — protection for metal structural members (beams, columns, girders) — is arguably the most practically critical section of the Sec. 603 table. Unprotected steel is the most common cause of catastrophic structural fire failure. Steel begins to lose structural capacity at approximately 300°C and reaches critical failure at approximately 550°C, both of which can occur within 10–15 minutes of a fully developed structural fire. No protection means no structural fire resistance.

Three protection systems are available:

**Concrete encasement:** An effective and durable system. 25 mm of concrete at 1 hour; 75 mm at 4 hours. The concrete is cast around the steel member, providing both thermal mass and physical protection. Disadvantages: adds significant weight to the structural member and requires formwork and casting around complex structural shapes. Used primarily for columns in high-exposure conditions.

**Masonry encasement:** The heaviest and thickest option — 38 mm at 1 hour, 100 mm at 4 hours. Masonry is robust and permanent but the least thermally efficient of the three options. The additional thickness (compared to concrete) reflects the hollow joints and varying thermal conductivity of masonry units and mortar. Masonry encasement is rarely used for beams in modern construction; it is most practical for ground-level columns where the additional mass is structurally manageable.

**Metal lath with vermiculite/perlite gypsum plaster:** The most thermally efficient and lightest option — 12 mm at 1 hour, 50 mm at 4 hours. This system uses a metal lath (a thin wire mesh framework) attached to the structural member, over which a gypsum plaster mixed with vermiculite or perlite is applied in layers. Vermiculite and perlite are naturally occurring siliceous minerals that have been expanded by heating — they have microscopic void structures that make them excellent thermal insulators. The result is a lightweight, conformable system that can be applied to complex structural shapes and achieves high fire ratings at minimal thickness.

The efficiency ratio at 4 hours:
- Metal lath + plaster: 50 mm (baseline)
- Concrete: 75 mm (50% thicker than plaster)
- Masonry: 100 mm (100% thicker than plaster = double)

Modern spray-applied fire-resistive materials (SFRM), also called "spray fireproofing" or "intumescent coatings," are the contemporary successors to traditional vermiculite plaster. While SFRM systems are not explicitly named in Sec. 603, they fall under the general principle of Sec. 601 (materials tested per accepted methods) and Sec. 604 (Secretary prescribes testing standards).

---

**CATEGORY D: EXTERIOR WALLS**

| Material | 4 hrs | 3 hrs | 2 hrs | 1 hr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solid concrete | 180 mm | 150 mm | 125 mm | 75 mm |
| Solid masonry | 200 mm | 175 mm | 150 mm | 100 mm |
| Hollow masonry | 300 mm | 250 mm | 200 mm | 150 mm |

Exterior walls must handle a more demanding exposure scenario than interior partitions. They face fire from one side (the interior) while simultaneously being exposed to weather, thermal cycling, and potentially heat from an adjacent burning building on the exterior side. This dual exposure explains why exterior wall thicknesses are generally greater than partition thicknesses at equivleant ratings.

Compare solid concrete partitions (175 mm at 4 hrs) vs. solid concrete exterior walls (180 mm at 4 hrs) — only 5 mm difference. The exterior exposure adds relatively little at the 4-hour level because at that extreme duration, the thermal mass of 150+ mm of concrete is already so substantial that the additional exposure does not dramatically change the required thickness.

At the 1-hour level, the difference is more pronounced: solid concrete partition = 125 mm, solid concrete exterior wall = 75 mm. Wait — the exterior wall is actually *thinner* at 1 hour? This appears counterintuitive, but it reflects the fact that at 1 hour, what matters most is the thermal barrier performance at relatively lower temperatures, and the exterior wall specification may also account for typical wall thickness conventions in practice. The 1-hour exterior wall specification at 75 mm for solid concrete aligns with the fact that many Philippine exterior walls in low-rise construction are poured concrete at 75 mm with rendering on both sides, which has historically been tested to 1-hour performance.

The same solid vs. hollow masonry hierarchy applies as for partitions: hollow masonry requires 300 mm at 4 hours — the same requirement for both exterior walls and partitions at this level.

---

**CATEGORY E: COLUMNS (ALL FACES EXPOSED)**

| Material | 4 hrs | 3 hrs | 2 hrs | 1 hr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reinforced concrete | 450 mm | 400 mm | 300 mm | 200 mm |

This is the most architecturally significant row in the entire Sec. 603 table. Unlike floors, partitions, and walls — where the table specifies cover or thickness — for columns, the table specifies the **minimum overall dimension of the reinforced concrete column** assuming all four faces are simultaneously exposed to fire.

The progression: 200 mm (1 hr) → 300 mm (2 hrs) → 400 mm (3 hrs) → 450 mm (4 hrs).

**Why this sequence has a near-arithmetic pattern:**

Fire attacks a column from all four faces simultaneously. The temperature front advances inward from each face, and the column fails when the temperature at the centroid (or at the reinforcement location) reaches critical steel temperature. For a square column, the critical thermal path is from face to core. Increasing the column dimension increases the thermal path length, extending the time to critical temperature.

From 200 to 300 to 400 mm, the increments are exactly 100 mm — a clean arithmetic progression. The step from 400 to 450 mm (+50 mm, not +100 mm) at the 4-hour level reflects diminishing returns: at very large section sizes, the additional concrete provides less incremental protection because the thermal mass at 400 mm is already substantial, and the remaining thermal vulnerability is at the column core rather than at the faces.

**Design implications:** A Type V building (4-hour structural frame) cannot use slender columns. The 450 mm minimum dimension forces a grid of substantial concrete piers. In a 10-metre structural bay, a 450 mm column occupies 4.5% of the span — not trivial in plan. Structural engineers must account for this when establishing column sizes in Type V buildings. Architects must coordinate their space planning with the structural grid to ensure that the required column dimensions do not create unacceptable constraints on floor plan flexibility.

This is where architectural design and fire-resistive construction standards intersect most tangibly. The 450 mm column is not just a code compliance issue — it is a planning driver.

### Principle 4: Fire-Resistive Regulations — The Secretary's Authority (Sec. 604)

Section 604 is the enabling provision that allows the fire-resistive standards of Rule VI to remain current without requiring legislative amendment to PD 1096 each time technology or testing methods evolve.

> "The Secretary shall prescribe standards on testing of construction materials for flame-spread characteristics, fire tests, door assemblies, tinclad fire doors, window assemblies, fire doors/windows installation, smoke/fire detectors, controlled interior finish, fire-resistive protection for structural members, walls, partitions, floor/roof ceiling assemblies, assemblies for protection of openings, fire-retardant roof coverings."

The Secretary's authority under Sec. 604 covers fourteen categories of fire-resistive performance standards:

**1. Flame-spread characteristics:** How quickly fire spreads across the surface of an interior finish material. Flame-spread index (FSI) is measured by the tunnel test (ASTM E84). Materials are classified Class A (FSI 0–25), Class B (26–75), or Class C (76–200). Highly occupied spaces like corridors, stairwells, and assembly rooms require the lowest flame-spread materials.

**2. Fire tests:** The specific testing protocols for rating assemblies. The Secretary can adopt or reference ASTM E119, ISO 834, or other internationally recognized standards.

**3. Door assemblies:** Fire-rated door frames, door leaves, and hardware assemblies. A fire-rated wall is only as fire-resistant as its weakest penetration — doors must be tested as complete assemblies (frame + door + hardware + closer) to achieve a rating.

**4. Tinclad fire doors:** A specific historical fire door type using tin-clad wood construction. Still referenced in the Code for legacy installations.

**5. Window assemblies:** Fire-rated glazing and frames. Modern wired glass, ceramic glass, and specialized intumescent glazing systems enable rated window openings in rated walls.

**6. Fire doors and windows installation:** Proper installation is critical. A rated door installed without a proper frame, threshold, and seals will not perform to its rated capacity. The Secretary's standards govern installation details.

**7. Smoke and fire detectors:** Detection system requirements for various occupancy types. Early detection enables earlier evacuation and suppression response, which in turn reduces the structural fire exposure duration.

**8. Controlled interior finish:** Flame-spread rating requirements for wall and ceiling finishes throughout the building. Even a fully fire-resistive structural frame can create a corridor of death if the interior finish materials have high flame-spread characteristics.

**9. Fire-resistive protection for structural members:** Detailed standards for column, beam, and girder protection systems — the Category C and E elements in the Sec. 603 table. The Secretary's standards can expand on the prescriptive table with tested assembly options.

**10–11. Walls, partitions, floor and roof ceiling assemblies:** Detailed specifications for rated assemblies beyond the prescriptive minimums in Sec. 603.

**12. Protection of openings:** How openings in rated assemblies must be protected. Openings are fire's preferred pathway — every penetration for pipes, ducts, conduits, and cables must be fire-stopped to maintain the assembly's rating.

**13. Fire-retardant roof coverings:** Roof covering classification systems analogous to Class A/B/C in US standards.

**14. (Implied):** As technology evolves — intumescent coatings, aerogel insulation blankets, engineered mass timber fire protection — the Secretary's authority allows these to be incorporated into the regulatory framework without amending PD 1096 itself.

### Principle 5: The Material Efficiency Hierarchy — A Unifying Framework

Synthesizing the Sec. 603 table reveals a consistent hierarchy of thermal efficiency across all categories:

**For protecting steel (Category C):**
1. Metal lath + vermiculite/perlite gypsum plaster — thinnest, lightest (12 mm at 1 hr; 50 mm at 4 hrs)
2. Concrete encasement — intermediate (25 mm at 1 hr; 75 mm at 4 hrs)
3. Masonry encasement — thickest, heaviest (38 mm at 1 hr; 100 mm at 4 hrs)

**For walls, partitions, and columns (Categories B, D, E):**
1. Solid concrete — thinnest for equivleant rating
2. Solid masonry — slightly thicker
3. Hollow unit masonry — significantly thicker (approximately 50–71% more than solid concrete)

The principle driving this hierarchy: **dense, homogeneous thermal mass is the most efficient fire barrier**. Materials with internal air voids (hollow masonry) or joints of different thermal properties (masonry mortar) require greater overall thickness to achieve the same thermal barrier performance.

**Why this matters for design decisions:** When an architect has freedom to choose between wall materials, understanding this hierarchy allows rational selection based on project constraints:
- Space-constrained project in a Highly Fire Restrictive Zone → choose solid concrete (thinnest option)
- Budget-constrained project where material cost matters more than thickness → hollow masonry may be acceptable if additional thickness can be accommodated in the structural grid
- High-rise steel frame → choose metal lath + vermiculite plaster for column and beam protection (thinnest, lightest, does not add to dead load significantly)

| **Overall thickness tendency** | PH prescriptive thicknesses generally more conservative (thicker) than IBC prescriptive equivleants, especially for RC columns | IBC provides more detailed gradations by concrete mix, aggregate type, and other variables |

**Key insight for the board exam:** Philippine RC column minimums (200–450 mm) are notably larger than typical IBC prescriptive column sizes (203–305 mm for normal-weight concrete). This reflects the Philippine Code's conservatism, which accounts for the combined effects of fire and seismic loading — columns must be robust enough to maintain structural integrity under both threat types. The IBC, while highly detailed, specifies somewhat smaller column sizes because it separates structural (seismic) design from fire-resistance design more cleanly at the code level.

## Cross-Referencing: How Rule VI Connects to Other Rules

### Rule V — Fire Zones (Sec. 501–507): The Required Rating is Set Here

Rule VI cannot be applied without Rule V, and vice versa. Rule V defines which fire zone a building is in and what fire resistance rating is required. Rule VI provides the material thickness data needed to achieve that rating.

The operational cross-reference map:

| Rule V Requirement | Rule VI Implementation |
|---|---|
| Fire Restrictive Zone: exterior walls — 2 hr | Solid concrete ext. wall ≥ 125 mm; solid masonry ≥ 150 mm; hollow masonry ≥ 200 mm |
| Fire Restrictive Zone: interiors — 1 hr | Solid concrete partition ≥ 125 mm; solid masonry ≥ 125 mm; hollow masonry ≥ 150 mm |
| Highly Fire Restrictive Zone: throughout — 3–4 hr | RC columns ≥ 400–450 mm; solid concrete partitions ≥ 175 mm; hollow masonry ext. walls ≥ 250–300 mm |
| Type V Construction (Sec. 401): structural frame — 4 hr | RC columns ≥ 450 mm; metal protection (plaster) ≥ 50 mm |

### Rule IV — Types of Construction (Sec. 401): The Framework for Construction Requirements

Rule IV classifies buildings into Types I through V based on the fire resistance of their structural elements. Rule VI provides the technical content of each classification — it is the standard to which Rule IV's definitions refer.

- Type V (fire-resistive): 4-hour structural frame → RC column ≥ 450 mm (Rule VI)
- Type IV (two-hour fire-resistive): 2-hour exterior walls → solid concrete ≥ 125 mm (Rule VI)
- Type III (one-hour fire-resistive): 1-hour walls and partitions → solid concrete ≥ 125 mm, hollow masonry ≥ 150 mm (Rule VI)

### Rule VII — Location on Property (Sec. 704): Where Distance Determines Required Rating

Rule VII, Sec. 704(2), requires exterior walls to have fire resistance based on proximity to property lines. The required rating for a given distance is implemented through the Sec. 603 exterior wall thickness table. An architect evaluating whether a proposed exterior wall at a specific distance from the property line satisfies Rule VII must use the Rule VI table to confirm the wall assembly achieves the required rating.

Sec. 704(2) also limits openings in rated exterior walls to not more than 50% of total wall area per storey. Opening protectives (fire-rated doors and windows) governed by Sec. 604 of Rule VI are the means by which those openings can exist at all in a rated assembly.

### Rule III — Building Permits and Certificate of Occupancy (Sec. 308–309)

The BFP inspection required before a Certificate of Occupancy can be issued (per Sec. 309) specifically examines fire-resistive construction compliance. Inspectors verify that installed column dimensions, wall thicknesses, floor slab depths, and metal protection systems match both the approved plans and the Sec. 603 requirements. Deficiencies discovered at this stage — a column at 380 mm where 400 mm was required for a 3-hour rating, for example — can prevent issuance of the Certificate of Occupancy and require remediation.

### National Structural Code of the Philippines (NSCP): Structural Sizing Must Satisfy Both Codes

The fire resistance requirements of Rule VI must be coordinated with the structural design requirements of the NSCP. An RC column designed to NSCP for gravity and seismic loads might be structurally adequate at 250 mm in section, but Rule VI may require 300 mm (2-hour) or larger for the fire zone in which the building sits. The critical governing size is always the larger of: (a) the structurally required size from the NSCP, and (b) the fire-resistance required size from Rule VI, Sec. 603. Architects must explicitly coordinate this with the project's structural engineer of record.

### Republic Act 9514 — Fire Code of the Philippines: Active vs. Passive Fire Protection

Rule VI governs **passive fire protection** — the inherent fire resistance of the building's materials and assemblies, present whether or not a detection or suppression system activates. RA 9514 governs **active fire protection** — sprinkler systems, fire alarms, standpipes, extinguishers. The two approaches are complementary:

- Passive protection (Rule VI) limits the growth and spread of fire and maintains structural integrity during fire exposure.
- Active protection (RA 9514) detects fire early and suppresses it before it reaches the structural fire resistance limit.

In some countries, the active protection system can substitute for (or partially reduce) passive protection requirements — a building with a full NFPA 13 sprinkler system may be permitted to use lighter-rated assemblies. PD 1096 Rule VI does not explicitly provide this trade-off within the passive protection tables, though the interaction between the two statutes is governed by BFP regulations under RA 9514.

## Synthesis: Applying the Sec. 603 Table — A Step-by-Step Decision Framework

When a board exam question involves fire-resistive construction standards, the following approach prevents errors:

**Step 1: Identify the assembly category.**
Determine which of the five Sec. 603 categories applies: Floor, Partition, Metal Protection, Exterior Wall, or Column. The category determines which rows of the table are relevant.

**Step 2: Identify the required fire resistance time period.**
This comes from Rule V (fire zone) or Rule IV (construction type), not from Rule VI itself. Rule VI provides the *how*; the required rating is set by Rule V or Rule IV. Typical required ratings:
- Non-Fire Restricted Zone: not specified (Types I–II)
- Fire Restrictive Zone: 1 hr interior, 2 hr exterior
- Highly Fire Restrictive Zone: 3–4 hr throughout

**Step 3: Identify the material being used.**
For the category identified in Step 1, locate the correct row: solid concrete, solid masonry, hollow masonry, RC slab, prestressed concrete, concrete encasement, masonry encasement, or plaster system.

**Step 4: Read the minimum thickness from the table.**
Use the column for the required time period. For floor slabs (both RC and prestressed), read BOTH sub-rows: cover to reinforcement/tendons AND overall depth. Both must be met simultaneously.

**Step 5: Check for the "more limiting criterion" trap.**
For assemblies with two sub-requirements (cover + depth for slabs), the achievable rating is the lower of the two criteria. If depth qualifies for 2 hours but cover only qualifies for 1 hour, the assembly achieves only 1 hour.

**Step 6: For metal protection, select the right system.**
If the question gives a specific protection material (concrete, masonry, or plaster) and a specific thickness, read the table to determine what rating that combination achieves. If the question asks which system achieves a given rating at minimum thickness, the answer is always metal lath with vermiculite/perlite gypsum plaster.

**Step 7: For RC columns, use the minimum dimension rule.**
The Sec. 603 column size (200/300/400/450 mm) is the **minimum overall cross-sectional dimension** of the RC column with all faces exposed. It is not a cover or clearance dimension. The structural engineer must ensure the column size satisfies both the structural demand (NSCP) and the fire resistance requirement (Rule VI).

## Philippine Context: Why These Specific Thicknesses Exist

The Sec. 603 table did not emerge from arbitrary selection. Understanding the reasoning behind the numbers reinforces retention and enables judgment when edge cases arise:

**Why RC columns need to be so large (200–450 mm):**

Philippine Type V construction is designed for dense urban environments where buildings are large, occupant loads are high, and firefighting conditions are challenging. Manila's traffic, narrow streets, and high-density blocks mean that BFP apparatus may take 20–40 minutes to deploy in a serious structural fire. During that time, the structural frame must maintain integrity without suppression assistance. A 450 mm RC column provides enough thermal mass that the steel reinforcement at the core does not reach critical temperature for approximately four hours, even in the face of a fully developed fire on all four column faces simultaneously.

**Why prestressed concrete needs so much more cover than ordinary RC:**

Prestressing tendons are manufactured to very high tensile strength (typically 1,860 MPa vs. 415 MPa for ordinary rebar). This strength comes from the molecular alignment created during the cold-drawing manufacturing process, which also makes the material more temperature-sensitive. At 300–350°C, prestressing tendons lose significant strength and begin to creep. Ordinary reinforcing bars remain relatively stable up to about 550–600°C. The Code's requirement for 65 mm cover at 4 hours for prestressed concrete (vs. 25 mm for ordinary RC) directly reflects this temperature-sensitivity difference. In the Philippine context, where prestressed concrete hollow-core planks and beam-and-slab systems are common in mid-rise residential and commercial construction, this cover requirement has significant structural design implications.

**Why hollow masonry needs so much more thickness:**

Hollow concrete masonry units (CMU), known in the Philippines as "hollow blocks" (HB), are the most ubiquitous construction material in the country. Understanding why they require 300 mm for 4-hour fire resistance (vs. 175 mm for solid concrete) is practically important for local architects. The hollow cells in standard Philippine hollow blocks occupy approximately 40–50% of the cross-sectional area. Fire entering these hollow cells can travel upward by convection, and radiation within the cavity accelerates heat transfer to the inner face. The net effect is that the fire "sees" far less solid material than the nominal wall thickness suggests. The 300 mm requirement forces enough solid material in the path of the fire that adequate thermal mass is available despite the hollow geometry.

**Why the plaster system is so efficient:**

Vermiculite and perlite are natural siliceous minerals that are processed by rapid high-temperature expansion ("popping") to create lightweight particles with microscopic void structures. When mixed into gypsum plaster, they create a composite material that has:
- Very low thermal conductivity (high insulation)
- Low density (minimal structural load addition)
- Ability to conform to complex structural shapes
- Resistance to chipping and abrasion once set

In Philippine high-rise construction, where steel structural frames are increasingly used in mixed-use and commercial towers, spray-applied vermiculite or modern SFRM coatings are the dominant fireproofing method. The Sec. 603 table's recognition of the plaster system as the thinnest-code-compliant option has driven its adoption in practice.

**Why exterior walls have different requirements from partitions:**

Exterior walls in the Philippines face a dual fire threat: fire from within the building AND potentially fire from an adjacent burning structure. Dense urban blocks in Tondo, Sampaloc, and Pasay see buildings separated by as little as 0.50–1.00 metre from their neighbours. In a serious fire, radiation from an adjacent burning building can raise the exterior wall surface temperature significantly even before the interior fire reaches that wall. The slightly larger exterior wall thicknesses (especially at 1-hour: 75 mm vs. 125 mm for partitions for solid concrete) reflect this dual exposure, though the counterintuitive 1-hour exterior wall being thinner than the 1-hour partition for solid concrete reflects practice-driven calibration for typical exterior concrete walls with render.

## Exam Trap Learts

**TRAP 1: "A 125 mm overall depth RC slab with 25 mm cover satisfies the 2-hour fire resistance rating for floors."**

TRUTH: A 125 mm overall depth satisfies the 2-hour depth requirement (125 mm at 2 hrs, per Sec. 603). However, 25 mm cover satisfies the **3-hour and 4-hour** cover requirement (25 mm), not the 2-hour requirement (20 mm). Having cover in excess of the minimum is not a problem — it actually exceeds the 2-hour standard and satisfies the higher 3 and 4-hour cover requirement. So the overall assembly (125 mm depth + 25 mm cover) meets the 2-hour depth standard but exceeds the 2-hour cover standard. The maximum rating the assembly can achieve is governed by the **more limiting criterion**: the depth (125 mm) limits it to **2 hours** even though the cover (25 mm) would qualify it for 3 or 4 hours. The exam will test whether you know that both criteria must be satisfied simultaneously.

---

**TRAP 2: "Metal encasement in masonry provides better fire protection than metal lath with perlite gypsum plaster because the masonry material is denser and more robust."**

TRUTH: Per PD 1096, Rule VI, Sec. 603, for equivleant thickness, metal lath with vermiculite/perlite gypsum plaster always achieves a **higher fire resistance rating** than masonry encasement. At 50 mm, plaster = 4-hour protection, masonry = between 1 and 2 hours (55 mm is needed for 2 hours). At 38 mm, plaster = 3 hours, masonry = 1 hour. The robustness or durability of masonry has no bearing on fire-resistive time period rating, which is determined solely by thermal performance in a standardized test. Density alone does not predict thermal insulation efficiency — it is the microstructural void geometry (microscopic pores in perlite/vermiculite) that makes the plaster system thermally superior despite being less dense in bulk form.

---

**TRAP 3: "Fire-resistive rating and fire-resistive time period rating are the same concept stated in different words."**

TRUTH: PD 1096 Rule VI defines them in separate sections — Sec. 601 and Sec. 602 — because they describe different aspects of fire resistance:

- **Fire-resistive rating (Sec. 601):** The performance measure — the *degree* to which a material or assembly can withstand fire, as determined by testing. This is the concept.
- **Fire-resistive time period rating (Sec. 602):** The unit of expression — the *length of time* in hours that quantifies how that degree of resistance is measured. This is the quantification.

The exam can ask specifically which section defines which concept. Sec. 601 = the "what it is" (degree/performance). Sec. 602 = the "how it's measured" (time period in hours). Conflating these two definitions is an easy mistake that is easy to avoid once you recognize that the Code authors intentionally separated them into two sections.

An architect must select a fire protection system for exposed structural steel beams in a 4-hour fire-resistive assembly (Type V construction). - C) Metal lath with vermiculite/perlite gypsum plaster at 50 mm — the most thermally efficient system achieves 4-hour rating at least thickness.

> **ANSWER: C**

The key principle is that thermal efficiency is determined by material properties, not by bulk density or perceived robustness. Vermiculite and perlite's microscopic void structure creates excellent thermal insulation at minimal thickness. Option D is wrong because all three materials are tested by the same methodology but achieve different ratings at the same thickness precisely because of their different thermal properties.

**Question 3**

A solid prestressed concrete floor slab has 40 mm of cover to the prestressing tendons and an overall depth of 125 mm. What is the maximum fire-resistance time period rating this assembly can achieve under PD 1096 Rule VI, Sec. 603?

- A) 4 hours — 40 mm cover significantly exceeds the 1-hour minimum of 25 mm and demonstrates substantial fire resistance.
- B) 3 hours — 40 mm cover to tendons meets the 3-hour cover requirement for prestressed concrete.
- C) 2 hours — 40 mm cover satisfies the 2-hour cover requirement; the 125 mm overall depth satisfies the 2-hour depth requirement; both criteria are met.
- D) 1 hour — prestressed concrete always requires significantly greater protection than ordinary RC, making 40 mm cover marginal.

> **ANSWER: C**
>
> For a solid prestressed concrete floor slab, per Sec. 603, BOTH the cover to tendons AND the overall depth must simultaneously meet the rating criteria.
>
> At 2 hours: cover required = 40 mm (this assembly has exactly 40 mm ✓); overall depth required = 125 mm (this assembly has exactly 125 mm ✓). Both criteria are precisely satisfied. Rating = 2 hours.
>
> At 3 hours: cover required = 50 mm (this assembly has only 40 mm ✗); overall depth required = 150 mm (this assembly has only 125 mm ✗). Both criteria fail for 3 hours.
>
> The maximum achievable rating is **2 hours**. Option A is wrong because 40 mm is exactly the 2-hour standard for prestressed (not "significantly exceeds"), and the overall depth fails the 3-hour and 4-hour requirements. Option B is wrong because 3-hour prestressed cover requires 50 mm (not 40 mm) and the depth is insufficient. Option D mischaracterizes 40 mm as "marginal" — it is precisely the 2-hour standard.
