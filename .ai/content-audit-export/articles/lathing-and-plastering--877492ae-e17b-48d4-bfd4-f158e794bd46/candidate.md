# Lathing and Plastering
## Learning Goal

After reading this article, you should be able to:

Identify the materials, thicknesses, and application methods for lath and plaster construction under PD 1096 Rule XVI; distinguish metal lath, gypsum lath, and wire lath by fire-resistance rating; apply the minimum plaster thickness for fire-rated assemblies; and determine when lath-and-plaster is an acceptable fire-resistive covering.

You are reviewing construction documents for a five-storey Type IV (steel frame) commercial building in Quezon City. The plans specify 9.5 mm standard gypsum board on corridor walls — the walls separating tenant spaces from the means of egress. You know the corridor must be fire-rated. You reach for your red pen. ## 2. Core Concepts with Mnemonic

### The Governing Provision

**PD 1096, Section 1215 (Basic Law, 1977):**
> "The installation of lath, plaster and gypsum wall board shall conform to the fire-resistive requirements as set forth by the Secretary."

**2004 Revised IRR, Rule XII, Section 1215:**
> "The installation of lath, plaster and gypsum wall board shall conform to the fire-resistive rating requirements and the type of construction of building."

**What changed:** The IRR is more specific — it ties installation requirements to both (a) the fire-resistive rating requirements under Rule VI and (b) the type of construction under Rule IV. The basic law delegated to the Secretary of Public Works; the IRR operationalizes that delegation by pointing to the existing rules framework.

---

### The Five Key Principles of Section 1215

**Principle 1 — Type of Construction Governs Material Selection**
The type of construction (Types I–V, per PD 1096, Rule IV) determines whether lath, plaster, and wall board must be combustible, limited-combustible, or incombustible. A Type V (fire-resistive) building cannot use wood lath. A Type I (wood frame) building has no fire-resistive requirements for interior partitions but is still subject to fire zone restrictions.

**Principle 2 — Fire-Resistive Ratings Are Non-Negotiable at Key Locations**
Per the 2004 Revised IRR, Rule VI (Fire-Resistive Standards), certain building elements must achieve tested fire-resistance ratings regardless of the occupancy group or construction type when code mandates them (e.g., corridor walls, shaft walls, stairwell enclosures). The wall board or plaster assembly must be part of a rated assembly — not just a single product specification.

**Principle 3 — It Is an Assembly, Not a Single Product**
No single product achieves a fire-resistance rating in isolation. A 15.9 mm (5/8") Type X gypsum board achieves a 1-hour rating only when installed as part of a complete tested assembly: the correct stud size and spacing, fastener pattern, joint treatment, and any required insulation. Per ASTM C840 and UL-listed assemblies, deviation from the assembly specification voids the rating.

**Principle 4 — Plaster Thickness and Application Method Are Prescribed**
Plaster thickness requirements are prescribed by material type and substrate. The three-coat system (scratch coat → brown coat → finish coat) is required over metal lath. The two-coat system is permitted over gypsum lath. Exterior cement plaster is not interchangeable with interior gypsum plaster; using the wrong plaster type in the wrong environment causes premature failure.

**Principle 5 — Philippine Practice Anchors on CHB + Plaster, but Drywall Is Growing**
The dominant Philippine wall system — concrete hollow block (CHB) with cement plaster finish — inherently satisfies fire-resistive requirements for most types of construction due to the incombustibility of masonry. Gypsum board (drywall) partitions are common in commercial fit-outs; these require careful specification of the board type, thickness, and assembly to meet fire-rating requirements.

## 4. In-Depth Technical Analysis

### 4.1 The Legislative Structure Behind Section 1215

Section 1215 is one of the shortest provisions in PD 1096, occupying a single sentence in the basic law. Its brevity is intentional: the original law delegates technical content to standards set by the Secretary of the DPWH. The 2004 Revised IRR fills this delegation by directing practitioners to Rules IV and VI — creating a cross-referencing framework rather than restating technical standards within Section 1215 itself.

This legislative structure has a critical implication for the LEA examination: **Section 1215 is not a standalone provision.** It is the connector between the fire-resistive framework (Rule VI), the construction type framework (Rule IV), and the actual installation of wall finishes. Exam questions on this section will frequently test your ability to trace that connection.

### 4.2 Types of Lath in Detail

**Metal Lath** is the most important lath type for fire-rated construction in the Philippines. Expanded metal lath (diamond mesh) is the most common form, produced by slitting and expanding a metal sheet. It provides mechanical keying for plaster adhesion and forms part of fire-rated plaster assemblies.

- **Weight classification:** Standard weight for wall applications is approximately 2.4 kg/m²; heavier lath is used for ceiling and soffit work where the plaster must bond overhead.
- **Corrosion protection:** Galvanized or painted; critical in the Philippine coastal and humid environment.
- **Fire performance:** Metal lath with three-coat Portland cement plaster over steel studs can achieve a 1-hour fire-resistance rating when the assembly conforms to an ASTM or UL-tested design.

**Gypsum Lath (Rocklath)** consists of a core of gypsum plaster with a paper facing that provides mechanical bond for finish plaster coats. At 9.5–12.7 mm thickness, it is lighter than solid gypsum wall board and is used primarily as a plaster base for two-coat plaster systems. It is suitable for Types I through V construction when used as part of a tested, rated assembly.

**Wood Lath** is historical. In the Philippine context, wood lath is classified as combustible and is incompatible with fire-resistive requirements. It may appear in pre-code residential construction but should not be specified for any new work where fire-resistive construction is required. Per PD 1096, Rule IV, it is appropriate only in Type I (combustible) construction and is not permitted where fire-resistive ratings must be achieved.

### 4.3 Plaster Systems in Detail

**Three-Coat System (Over Metal Lath)**

This is the standard exterior plaster application and the required system for metal lath substrates:

1. **Scratch coat** — Applied immediately after lath installation. The wet plaster is forced through the mesh openings to create a mechanical key. While still wet, it is scratched (scored) to provide a rough surface for the brown coat. Typical thickness: 9–10 mm.

2. **Brown coat** — Applied after the scratch coat has cured (minimum 48 hours for cement plaster). This is the leveling coat; it brings the surface to a true, flat plane. Thickness: 10–12 mm. The brown coat must be damp-cured for Portland cement plasters to prevent premature drying and cracking.

3. **Finish coat** — The final surface coat, applied thin (3–5 mm) over a dampened brown coat. For exterior: sand finish or textured. For interior: smooth finish (gypsum putty or lime-based) or texture.

**Two-Coat System (Over Gypsum Lath or Masonry)**

On gypsum lath or smooth masonry, a scratch coat is not required because mechanical keying is provided by the substrate surface or paper facing:
1. **Base coat** — Single leveling coat, 6–10 mm depending on substrate.
2. **Finish coat** — Same as three-coat system, 3–5 mm.

**Material Compatibility Rules:**

| Rule | Rationale |
|------|-----------|
| Gypsum plaster = interior only | Gypsum is water-soluble; exterior exposure causes dissolution and failure |
| Portland cement plaster = exterior and wet areas | Cement cures by hydration; moisture-resistant after full cure |
| No calcium sulfate in interior cement plaster | Calcium sulfate interferes with cement hydration (per IBC 2511.3, adopted as referral standard) |
| Lime plaster over masonry | Lime is compatible with masonry chemistry (both alkaline); adhesion is excellent |

### 4.4 Gypsum Wall Board Systems in Detail

**Standard (Type R) Gypsum Board**

Regular gypsum board consists of a gypsum plaster core encased in paper facings. The gypsum core is non-combustible and slows heat transmission, but standard board without additives does not achieve tested fire-resistance ratings on its own. It is appropriate for non-rated interior partitions in Types I through V construction.

**Type X Gypsum Board (Fire-Rated)**

Type X board contains glass fibers and other additives distributed throughout the gypsum core that reinforce the board during fire exposure and inhibit shrinkage. At 15.9 mm thickness, a tested assembly using Type X board on steel studs typically achieves 1-hour fire-resistance rating per ASTM E119 or the equivalent UL assembly.

Critical characteristics:
- Must be labeled "Type X" or "Fire-Rated" by the manufacturer
- The rating belongs to the assembly, not the board alone
- Double-layer applications (two layers of 15.9 mm Type X) can achieve 2-hour ratings in tested assemblies
- Per the 2004 Revised IRR cross-reference to Rule VI and referral codes, any claim of fire resistance must be backed by a tested assembly

**Type C Gypsum Board**

Type C board is an enhanced formulation beyond Type X. It typically contains more glass fibers and special additives that allow the board to maintain its integrity longer under fire exposure. While not explicitly defined in PD 1096, Type C boards are specified in fire-rated assemblies under the referral standards framework. They are particularly useful in lightweight steel-frame construction.

**Moisture-Resistant Board (Green Board)**

MR board has a water-resistant paper facing and moisture-resistant additives in the core. It is appropriate for areas with elevated humidity (e.g., behind kitchen cabinetry, in laundry rooms) but is explicitly NOT appropriate for direct water contact or as a direct tile substrate in shower enclosures. In the Philippine context, a common error is specifying green board in toilet and bath tile areas — the correct substrate is cement board.

**Cement Board**

Cement board (e.g., HardieBacker, Durock, and locally available equivalents) is a cementitious panel with fiberglass reinforcement. It is the correct substrate for tile in wet areas (bathrooms, shower enclosures, kitchen splash areas) because it is dimensionally stable when wet and does not support mold growth. It is not a fire-rated product per se, but it is incombustible.

### 4.5 Installation Requirements for Gypsum Board

The following installation principles apply in Philippine practice under the referral standards framework:

**Framing and Fastening:**
- Steel studs at 400 mm or 600 mm on center (standard spacing for rated assemblies — deviation voids the rating)
- Screws at 200–300 mm on center at field; 150–200 mm at edges for rated assemblies
- Board joints must be staggered between layers in multi-layer assemblies
- Gypsum board must not touch the floor slab — maintain a 10–12 mm gap to prevent moisture wicking

**Joint Treatment:**
- All joints, fasteners, and corners must be taped and compounded to complete the rated assembly
- Untreated joints represent gaps in the fire barrier and void the tested assembly

**Penetrations:**
- All penetrations through rated assemblies (pipes, conduits, cables) must be protected with UL-listed firestopping systems per Rule VI and RA 9514

**Grab Bar Blocking (BP 344):**
- Where grab bars are required per BP 344 (Accessibility Law), solid wood or steel blocking must be installed within the wall cavity at the correct height to provide a substrate for fastening — gypsum board alone will not support the required loads

### 4.6 Fire-Resistive Construction and the Role of Wall Finishes

The fire-resistive rating of a wall assembly is achieved through the combined thermal resistance of all components: the framing, insulation (if any), the board layers, and the joint treatment. Section 1215 is best understood as the code's recognition that the interior finish layer is not cosmetic — it is a structural component of the fire barrier.

**At corridor walls** (Rule XII, Section 1207): A 1-hour fire-resistive rating is required for corridor walls in most occupancy groups. This means the gypsum board type, thickness, fastener pattern, stud spacing, and joint treatment must all conform to a tested 1-hour assembly. Specifying 12.7 mm (1/2") standard board on a corridor wall fails this requirement.

**At shaft walls** (Rule XII, Section 1204): Elevator, mechanical, and plumbing shafts must be enclosed in rated construction. Shaft wall assemblies use special gypsum liner panels (often 25 mm / 1" thick) designed for one-sided installation into the shaft without interior access.

**At exit stairways:** Stair enclosure walls require fire-resistive construction per Rule XII and Rule VI. Metal lath and Portland cement plaster or tested gypsum board assemblies are used.

## 5. PH vs. USA Comparative Analysis

### 5.1 Structure of the Code

The fundamental difference is one of **specificity vs. framework**:

- **PD 1096 / IRR (PH):** Section 1215 is a one-sentence provision that delegates all technical content to (a) the type of construction rules, (b) the fire-resistive standards, and (c) referral codes adopted by the DPWH Secretary. The IRR does not reproduce ASTM standards or UL assembly numbers. Practitioners must cross-reference to the referral code library.

- **IBC (USA):** Chapter 25 is a detailed standalone chapter with specific provisions on gypsum board thickness (Section 2506), lath weight (Section 2510), plaster coat thickness (Section 2508), specific prohibitions (Section 2511.3 — no calcium sulfate in interior cement plaster), and explicit cross-references to ASTM standards for each material.

**Implication for LEA Candidates:** Philippine code on this topic is a framework, not a recipe book. On the exam, you will be tested on the framework (which rule governs? what type of construction? what fire-resistive rating is required?) rather than memorized ASTM tables.

### 5.2 Material Specifications

| Specification | PH Standard | USA Standard (IBC) | More Restrictive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exterior plaster total thickness | 15–20 mm (practice) | 22 mm minimum (IBC 2508.1) | USA |
| Metal lath — walls | Per referral standard | Min. 1.36 kg/m² (IBC 2510) | USA (explicit) |
| Metal lath — ceilings | Per referral standard | Min. 1.85 kg/m² (IBC 2510) | USA (explicit) |
| 1-hour corridor wall assembly | 15.9 mm Type X in rated assembly | 15.9 mm Type X in tested assembly | equivalent |
| Green board in wet areas | Not explicitly prohibited in code text; standard practice prohibits | Not permitted as direct tile substrate | equivalent (practice) |
| Fire-rating verification | Assembly conformance (referral codes) | UL assembly listing required | USA (more prescriptive) |
| Wood lath in Types II–V | Prohibited (combustible) | Prohibited by fire-resistive requirements | equivalent |

### 5.3 Philippine Construction Context

The Philippine building industry differs from the US in several ways that affect how Section 1215 is applied in practice:

1. **CHB dominance:** In the Philippines, masonry walls (CHB + cement plaster) are the default. This system is inherently incombustible and meets fire-resistive requirements without reference to tested drywall assemblies. The Section 1215 framework matters most for the growing commercial drywall market.

2. **Tropical climate:** High humidity and the presence of coastal salt air accelerate corrosion of steel lath and studs. Galvanized or stainless-steel framing is essential, particularly near the coast. Gypsum board in high-humidity environments without proper air conditioning requires moisture-resistant board as minimum.

3. **Seismic conditions:** The Philippines is in a high-seismic zone. Gypsum board partitions must accommodate seismic drift — steel stud systems must be detailed with sufficient clearance at the top track (typically 25–38 mm) so that the partition does not carry vertical load during seismic movement. This is a structural detail, not a Section 1215 issue per se, but it affects wall board installation.

4. **Fire zone requirements (Rule V):** Buildings in Fire Zone 1 face additional restrictions. In Zone 1, exterior wall finishes must be non-combustible, which further restricts the use of certain lath and plaster systems.

## 6. Exam Traps

### Trap 1 — "Any Gypsum Board Satisfies a 1-Hour Rating"

**Wrong answer:** A 12.7 mm (1/2") standard gypsum board on steel studs provides a 1-hour fire-resistance rating.

**Why it's wrong:** Fire-resistance ratings belong to assemblies, not individual products. Standard (Type R) gypsum board does not achieve a 1-hour rating regardless of thickness. A 1-hour rated assembly requires, at minimum, 15.9 mm (5/8") **Type X** board with the correct stud spacing, fastener pattern, and joint treatment — all as part of a tested assembly per ASTM E119 or an equivalent protocol.

**Correct answer:** The 1-hour rated assembly must conform to a tested design using Type X gypsum board (15.9 mm) installed per the assembly specification. Per the 2004 Revised IRR, Rule XII, Section 1215, the installation must conform to fire-resistive rating requirements — meaning the entire assembly must be rated, not just one component.

---

### Trap 2 — "Gypsum Plaster Can Be Used Anywhere Plaster Is Required"

**Wrong answer:** Specifying gypsum plaster for the exterior facade of a building satisfies the plaster requirement of Section 1215.

**Why it's wrong:** Gypsum plaster is **interior-only**. Gypsum is water-soluble and will dissolve, crack, and fail when exposed to weather. Philippine tropical conditions — high humidity, heavy rainfall, and direct sun — make exterior gypsum plaster wholly inappropriate. The correct exterior plaster is Portland cement plaster (stucco), which cures by hydration and becomes moisture-resistant.

**Correct answer:** Portland cement plaster, mixed at 1:3 to 1:4 (cement:sand), applied in a three-coat system over metal lath, is the correct specification for exterior plaster. Gypsum plaster is limited to interior, dry areas only.

---

### Trap 3 — "Type of Construction Doesn't Affect Wall Board Thickness"

**Wrong answer:** Because Section 1215 is a brief provision, wall board thickness is a matter of architect's choice and is not affected by the type of construction.

**Why it's wrong:** The 2004 Revised IRR explicitly ties Section 1215 to the fire-resistive rating requirements *and* the type of construction. In a Type IV or Type V building, partitions that require fire-resistive ratings must use tested assemblies with Type X (or better) gypsum board. The type of construction determines whether fire-resistive ratings are required for a given building element; once a rating is required, the assembly (including board thickness and type) is fixed by the tested design.

**Correct answer:** Per the 2004 Revised IRR, Rule XII, Section 1215, installation must conform to both the fire-resistive rating requirements (Rule VI) and the type of construction (Rule IV). The type of construction directly determines whether a fire-rated assembly is required, which in turn determines the board type and thickness.

## 7. Board-Prep Mnemonics

### Mnemonic 1: **"SMOKE"** — Five Things That Determine Your Wall Board Spec

> **S** — **Site location** (exterior or interior; wet or dry)
> **M** — **Material type permitted** by type of construction (combustible or incombustible)
> **O** — **Opening/Assembly** — is it a rated assembly or a non-rated partition?
> **K** — **Key code requirements** — Rule IV, Rule VI, RA 9514 flame spread, BP 344 blocking
> **E** — **Environment** — tropical humidity, seismic drift gap, corrosion of framing

Every gypsum board spec decision runs through SMOKE. Get one wrong and the wall fails — on site or on the exam.

---

### Mnemonic 2: **"SBF"** — The Three-Coat Plaster Sequence

> **S** — **Scratch coat** (mechanical key; scratched while wet)
> **B** — **Brown coat** (bulk; leveling coat; bulk of thickness)
> **F** — **Finish coat** (final surface; thin 3–5 mm)

**Memory hook:** "Scratch the mud, Brown it flat, Finish it smooth." The order never changes — applying a finish coat before the brown coat has cured produces a delaminating, cracked surface. Remember also: brown coat must be **damp-cured** for Portland cement plaster.

## 8. Active Recall Questions

**Question 1**
*(LEA Area 2 — Materials & Methods | Subtopic: Gypsum Wall Board)*

A building's corridor wall separating a Type IV office building's egress corridor from adjacent tenant spaces must be fire-rated per the 2004 Revised IRR. Which gypsum board specification correctly begins to satisfy this requirement?

- A. 9.5 mm (3/8") standard gypsum board on both sides of steel studs at 600 mm o.c.
- B. 12.7 mm (1/2") standard gypsum board on both sides of steel studs at 400 mm o.c.
- C. 15.9 mm (5/8") Type X gypsum board on both sides of steel studs at 400 mm o.c. in a tested 1-hour assembly
- D. 15.9 mm (5/8") moisture-resistant gypsum board on both sides of steel studs at 600 mm o.c.

> **Correct Answer: C**
> A 1-hour fire-resistive corridor wall requires a tested assembly using Type X gypsum board (15.9 mm / 5/8") at the stud spacing and fastener pattern prescribed by the assembly. Standard board (Options A and B) and moisture-resistant board (Option D) do not contribute to tested fire-resistance ratings. Per the 2004 Revised IRR, Rule XII, Section 1215, the installation must conform to fire-resistive rating requirements — which means a complete, tested assembly.

**Question 2**
*(LEA Area 2 — Materials & Methods | Subtopic: Plaster Types — PH vs. USA Standard)*

A building in Manila requires an exterior plaster finish on concrete hollow block walls. Which specification is correct, and which standard (Philippine or US) is more restrictive regarding minimum total plaster thickness?

- A. Gypsum plaster, 12 mm; PH and USA are equivalent
- B. Portland cement plaster, 15 mm; Philippine practice (15–20 mm) is less thick than the IBC minimum of 22 mm for 3-coat metal lath systems
- C. Lime plaster, 20 mm; USA is more restrictive
- D. Portland cement plaster, 12 mm; Philippine practice exceeds the IBC minimum

> **Correct Answer: B**
> Exterior plaster must be Portland cement plaster (gypsum plaster is interior-only). Philippine practice specifies 15–20 mm for exterior walls, which satisfies PD 1096 requirements. The IBC (Section 2508.1) requires a minimum 22 mm (7/8") for 3-coat exterior Portland cement plaster over metal lath — making the USA standard more prescriptively restrictive for that specific substrate. Option D is incorrect because PH practice (15–20 mm) is less than 22 mm, not exceeding it.

**Question 3**
*(LEA Area 2 — Materials & Methods | Subtopic: Section 1215 Framework)*

Under PD 1096 and the 2004 Revised IRR, which two primary rules work together to determine the required lath, plaster, and wall board specifications for a given building element?

- A. Rule III (Permits and Inspections) and Rule XII (General Building Requirements)
- B. Rule IV (Types of Construction) and Rule VI (Fire-Resistive Standards)
- C. Rule V (Fire Zones) and Rule VIII (Light and Ventilation)
- D. Rule X (Sanitation) and Rule XI (Electrical Systems)

> **Correct Answer: B**
> The 2004 Revised IRR, Rule XII, Section 1215 explicitly ties lath, plaster, and wall board installation to (1) fire-resistive rating requirements (Rule VI) and (2) the type of construction (Rule IV). Rule IV determines what types of materials are permitted and whether fire-resistant construction is required; Rule VI specifies the ratings that must be achieved. This two-rule framework is the core of Section 1215.

## 9. Cross-Referencing Section

Section 1215 does not exist in isolation. Understanding it requires integrating several layers of the Philippine building code framework and allied legislation.

### Rule IV — Types of Construction (PD 1096)
The most direct cross-reference. Rule IV classifies buildings into Types I through V based on the combustibility and fire resistance of their structural elements. Each type has corresponding requirements for walls, partitions, floors, and roof structures. Section 1215 draws its technical requirements directly from this classification: a Type I building has no fire-resistive requirement for interior partitions; a Type V building requires incombustible, fire-resistive construction throughout. This determines whether any fire-rated lath or wall board assembly is required at all.

### Rule V — Fire Zones (PD 1096)
Fire zones impose additional restrictions based on location within a municipality. In Fire Zone 1 (densely built, high-risk areas), exterior construction materials must be non-combustible. This affects the choice of lath and plaster for exterior walls, ensuring cement plaster over metal lath rather than any combustible lath system. Rule V requirements apply independently of and in addition to Rule IV type-of-construction requirements.

### Rule VI — Fire-Resistive Standards (PD 1096)
Rule VI is the technical backbone of Section 1215. It establishes the fire-resistance ratings required for building elements (walls, columns, beams, slabs) by type of construction and occupancy group. When a wall requires a 1-hour fire-resistance rating per Rule VI, Section 1215 requires that the lath, plaster, or wall board assembly achieve that rating. Rule VI also cross-references to ASTM test methods (E119) and UL assembly listings.

### Rule XII, Section 1204 — Vertical Openings (Shaft Walls)
Elevator, mechanical, and plumbing shafts must be enclosed in fire-rated construction. Shaft walls are a specialized application of Section 1215 requirements: the wall board (typically gypsum liner panels) and lath must be part of a rated shaft wall assembly. These assemblies are tested independently of standard partition assemblies.

### Rule XII, Section 1207 — Exits and Corridors
Corridor walls in most occupancies must achieve a 1-hour fire-resistive rating. This is one of the most frequently tested applications of Section 1215 in practice. The specification of 15.9 mm Type X gypsum board in a tested 1-hour assembly on corridor walls flows directly from the interaction of Section 1207 (requiring the rating) and Section 1215 (requiring the installation to conform to that rating).

### RA 9514 — Fire Code of the Philippines
The Fire Code governs interior finish materials independently of PD 1096. Section 1215 addresses fire-resistive construction (structural fire protection), while RA 9514 governs interior finish flame-spread ratings (surface burning characteristics). A wall board may achieve a 1-hour structural fire-resistance rating but still fail RA 9514 requirements if its exposed surface has a flame-spread index above the permitted limit for the occupancy and location. Both standards must be satisfied simultaneously.

### BP 344 — Accessibility Law
Barrier-free design requires grab bars at accessible toilet and bathing facilities. Grab bars require solid support — gypsum board alone cannot support the required loads (150–200 kg minimum pull-out load per fixture). BP 344 indirectly affects wall board installation by requiring solid blocking (wood or steel) within the wall cavity at prescribed heights. The architect or designer must coordinate blocking requirements during the design of the wall assembly, not as an afterthought during construction.

### NSCP — National Structural Code of the Philippines
While not a direct cross-reference of Section 1215, the NSCP governs the structural framing that supports lath and wall board. In seismic Zone 4 (Metro Manila) and Zone 5 (high-risk regions), steel stud partitions must be designed to accommodate seismic inter-story drift. A clearance of 25–38 mm at the top track is standard practice, preventing the partition from acting as an unintended structural element during seismic events. This gap must be properly firestopped where the partition is part of a rated assembly.

### ASTM and Referral Standards (as adopted under PD 1096)
The following ASTM standards are part of the referral code library applicable under PD 1096:
- **ASTM C36 / C1396:** Gypsum wallboard specifications
- **ASTM C840:** Installation of interior gypsum board
- **ASTM C926:** Application of Portland cement plaster
- **ASTM E119:** Standard fire-resistance test method (basis for fire-resistance ratings)
- **GA-216 (Gypsum Association):** Fire-Resistance Design Manual — the primary reference for tested gypsum board assemblies
