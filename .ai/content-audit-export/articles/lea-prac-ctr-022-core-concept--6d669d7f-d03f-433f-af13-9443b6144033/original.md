Liquidated damages are pre-agreed monetary sums payable by the contractor to the owner as compensation for contractor breach, typically delay in project completion. They are enforceable under Philippine law (Articles 1226-1230, Civil Code) provided they are: (1) reasonable estimates of probable harm, not penalties, (2) agreed upon by parties in the contract before breach occurs, and (3) not unconscionable or iniquitous. Liquidated damages differ from actual damages—they are a contractual formula that eliminates the owner's burden to prove actual harm. Computation is typically: (contract daily rate) × (number of delay days) = liquidated damages amount.

## Core Concept

Liquidated damages clauses establish the contractor's financial obligation for delay without requiring the owner to document or prove actual losses. Once the contractor fails to achieve Substantial Completion by the contract completion date, the owner is entitled to liquidated damages at the rate specified in the contract, accruing daily until Substantial Completion is certified. The contract typically specifies the daily rate (e.g., 0.1% of contract price per day of delay), and the contractor's liability accumulates until the project milestone is reached. Liquidated damages end at Substantial Completion, not Final Completion; after Substantial Completion, the contractor is no longer liable for delay damages.

Liquidated damages are distinct from penalties. A penalty is an excessive amount imposed to punish breach and is not enforceable under the Civil Code. A liquidated damages clause is enforceable if it represents a reasonable, pre-agreed estimate of the damage the owner would suffer from contractor delay. Courts examine whether the amount is proportionate to the probable harm. If the liquidated damages amount is manifestly disproportionate to the anticipated loss (e.g., 10% daily charge on a small project), courts may reduce the amount under Article 1229, Civil Code, which authorizes courts to equitably reduce iniquitous stipulations.

The contractor's payment obligation for liquidated damages is triggered automatically upon failure to achieve Substantial Completion by the contract completion date. The owner does not need to give notice or make a demand; liquidated damages accrue from the first day of delay. However, the contract or law may require formal notice to the contractor that delay is occurring. Once liquidated damages accrue, they are typically deducted from the contractor's final payment, or the owner may invoice the contractor separately. Disputes over liquidated damages calculation (correct number of delay days, correct daily rate) are common.

## Philippine Practice Context

Under Philippine law (Civil Code Article 2226 and construction practice), liquidated damages are enforceable provided they represent a reasonable, pre-agreed estimate of delay-related harm. The owner's advantage is that it does not need to prove actual damages from delay (lost rent, financing costs, loss of use, etc.); the liquidated damages amount stands as fixed compensation. This is particularly valuable in commercial construction where the owner would otherwise need to document exactly what it lost due to the contractor's delay.

Typical liquidated damages rates in Philippine construction range from 0.05% to 0.1% of the contract price per calendar day of delay. A project with a 100 million peso contract price and 0.1% daily rate incurs 100,000 pesos in liquidated damages per day of delay. The rate must be reasonable—if a contract specifies 1% per day (1 million pesos daily on the example), courts would likely reduce it as unconscionable. The contractor has the burden of proving the amount is iniquitous; the owner has the burden of proving the rate is reasonable if challenged.

Liquidated damages end at Substantial Completion. A critical distinction: if the project is delayed 30 days past the contract completion date but Substantial Completion is finally certified on day 30, liquidated damages accrue for 30 days, then stop. Final Completion (punch list work) is not subject to liquidated damages; the contractor's obligation to finish punch items is separate. The contractor who achieves Substantial Completion late but is prompt with Final Completion avoids additional delay damages.

Enforcement of liquidated damages is typically straightforward: the owner deducts the calculated amount from the contractor's final payment certificate, or invoices the contractor separately. If the contractor disputes the calculation (number of delay days or daily rate), the dispute is resolved through claim process, arbitration, or litigation. The contractor may argue that the delay was not its fault (owner-caused delay, differing site conditions) and seek relief from liquidated damages. The burden of proving contractor non-responsibility for delay rests on the contractor.

## How It's Tested

**Question Pattern 1:** Calculation—Given contract price, daily rate, and days of delay, compute liquidated damages owed. (Tests arithmetic and application of formula)

**Question Pattern 2:** Enforceability—Is a liquidated damages clause with a specified rate enforceable under Philippine law? (Tests reasonableness and non-unconscionability requirements)

**Question Pattern 3:** Trigger—When do liquidated damages begin accruing and when do they end? (Tests Substantial Completion as control point)

**Question Pattern 4:** Unconscionability—Can a court reduce liquidated damages if the amount is manifestly disproportionate? (Tests Article 2226 equitable reduction authority)

**Question Pattern 5:** Contractor responsibility—If the contractor claims delay was not its fault, can it avoid liquidated damages? (Tests contractor burden to prove non-responsibility)
