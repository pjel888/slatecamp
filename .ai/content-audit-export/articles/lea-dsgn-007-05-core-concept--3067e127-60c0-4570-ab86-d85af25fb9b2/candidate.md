## What You Need to Know for the LEA

Reading and analyzing a design brief is a critical skill for architects. The brief sets project parameters, client requirements, site constraints, and regulatory context. Comprehensive brief analysis informs all subsequent design decisions and prevents misalignment with client expectations.

## Learning Goal

Analyze a design brief to extract program elements, site data, regulatory context, and design intent. Calculate total GFA and occupant load from program requirements, identify ambiguities requiring client clarification, and verify occupancy group classification early in the design process.

## Core Concept

A design brief is a document prepared by the client or their representative (often in consultation with the architect during Pre-Design) that defines what the project must accomplish. The brief is not a design solution; it is a statement of requirements and constraints. Effective brief analysis requires careful reading, identification of explicit requirements, recognition of implied constraints, and clarification of ambiguities before design begins.

**Program Elements** define functional requirements. The brief specifies space types and quantities (e.g., 5 offices at 20 sqm each, 2 meeting rooms at 30 sqm, 1 reception at 40 sqm). These elements establish the project scope. The architect calculates total area requirements from the program and determines occupancy group based on intended use.

**Site Data** provides constraints and opportunities. The brief includes site location, dimensions, topography, existing utilities, environmental conditions (solar orientation, wind, drainage), and access points. Site data directly influences building placement, orientation, circulation planning, and utility integration. Environmental factors (tropical climate, flood risk, seismic zone) drive design decisions and code compliance requirements.

**Regulatory Context** establishes mandatory constraints. The brief identifies applicable building codes (NBCP, local ordinances), occupancy group classification (A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I, J), required certifications or permits, and accessibility standards. Misunderstanding regulatory requirements can invalidate a design during permit review.

**Design Intent and Constraints** articulate client goals and limitations. The brief may specify aesthetic preferences (modern, vernacular, sustainability emphasis), performance targets (energy efficiency, resilience), budget parameters, or schedule constraints. Identifying implicit constraints (e.g., "minimizing excavation" suggests challenging soil conditions) is as important as recognizing explicit requirements.

**Ambiguities and Clarifications** require early identification. Briefs may contain incomplete information, contradictory requirements, or vague statements. The architect's role includes identifying these gaps and obtaining clarification from the client before design begins. Assumptions made without verification create project risk.

## Key Provisions

| Brief Element | Information | Architect's Use |
|--------------|-------------|-----------------|
| Program | Space types, quantities, adjacencies | Establish project scope, calculate GFA |
| Site Data | Location, dimensions, orientation, utilities, environmental conditions | Inform building placement, circulation, utility planning |
| Regulatory Context | Building code, occupancy group, accessibility standards, permits required | Ensure design compliance, plan permit strategy |
| Design Intent | Client goals, aesthetic preferences, performance targets | Guide conceptual direction, set design parameters |
| Budget & Schedule | Project cost limits, timeline | Influence material selection, phasing strategy |
| Client Organization | Decision-makers, approval process, stakeholders | Plan communication strategy, manage expectations |

**Brief Analysis Checklist:**
- Identify all space types and areas in program
- Calculate total GFA and occupant load
- Verify occupancy group classification
- Identify site constraints (orientation, topography, utilities, access)
- List regulatory requirements (codes, permits, certifications)
- Clarify design intent and aesthetic preferences
- Identify ambiguities requiring client clarification
- Document all assumptions made in brief interpretation

## Philippine Practice Context

Design briefs in the Philippines follow the Unified Architects Practice (UAP) framework, which emphasizes comprehensive Pre-Design investigation. Philippine clients often prepare briefs that emphasize tropical climate considerations (monsoon resilience, solar heat management, natural ventilation). Regulatory context typically includes NBCP compliance, local zoning ordinances, Bureau of Fire Protection (BFP) requirements for fire-rated occupancies, and Department of Health standards for institutional/medical facilities. Briefs may include references to specific municipal building codes that exceed NBCP minimums. Client organizations in government and institutional sectors may have internal design standards (e.g., Department of Education standards for school buildings) that become brief constraints. Early clarification of these standards prevents design rejection at permit or construction phases.

## How It's Tested

**Pattern 1 — Program Calculation:** A brief specifies offices, conference rooms, reception, and support spaces. Calculate total GFA, occupant load, and identify the occupancy group. Tests application of occupancy classification and area calculations.

**Pattern 2 — Site Constraint Analysis:** A brief includes site data showing steep topography, flood-prone location, and limited access. Identify implications for building design (foundation strategy, elevation above flood level, site circulation planning). Tests reading comprehension and design application.

**Pattern 3 — Ambiguity Identification:** A brief describes a mixed-use facility with vague adjacency requirements and unclear scope. Identify missing information and formulate questions for client clarification. Tests critical thinking and risk recognition.

<Tip>Brief = requirements, not solutions. Read carefully: explicit requirements, implied constraints, ambiguities. Clarify before design. Program + Site + Code + Intent = Design parameters. Verify occupancy group early; it drives many other decisions.</Tip>

**Exam Tip — ERR-DSGN-002:** Design principles (proportion, scale, balance, rhythm, hierarchy) are tested through visual analysis. The LEA may present a building image and ask which principle is violated or best represented.
