# American Colonial Architecture

## Board Exam Quick Check

First identify the region and period. New England, Chesapeake, Spanish Florida, and Federal civic architecture do not use the same material logic or symbolic purpose.

## What You Need to Know for the LEA

American colonial architecture is not one style. It is a set of regional building responses shaped by climate, available materials, settlement patterns, labor systems, and political identity. The LEA often tests recognition through physical clues: central chimney and steep roof, brick plantation house and dependencies, coquina fortification, or refined Federal symmetry.

Historic New England supports the New England First Period pattern: timber framing, steeply pitched roofs, small windows, asymmetry, and massive central chimneys in cold climates. NPS Federal Style sources support the later Federal vocabulary of restrained ornament, symmetry, fanlights, and lighter proportions. NPS Castillo de San Marcos sources support Spanish Florida as a coquina masonry fortification tradition rather than an English timber or Georgian plantation type.

## Core Concept

New England houses often used timber, steep roofs, and central chimneys because cold winters and snow shaped domestic building form. Chesapeake and Virginia plantation houses often used brick, symmetrical facades, Palladian or Georgian ideals, and dependency buildings, but those forms must be read together with plantation labor and slavery. Mount Vernon records are useful because they show the mansion's Palladian composition and the quarters and work structures that made the estate function.

Federal architecture is a post-Revolutionary refinement of Georgian order. It uses symmetry, restrained ornament, fanlights, elliptical or octagonal rooms, and classical references to express the new republic. The Architect of the Capitol source map supports neoclassical civic buildings as an American symbolic language derived from Greek and Roman precedent.

## Real World Scenario

A preservation architect should not specify one colonial repair approach for all regions. A New England timber house raises roof, chimney, and clapboard questions. A Chesapeake plantation estate must address mansion, dependencies, and enslaved labor spaces. A Spanish Florida fortification requires coquina and defensive-wall knowledge. A Federal civic building requires symmetry, classical detail, and early-republic symbolism.

## How It Is Tested

Expect regional-recognition items: central chimney plus steep roof points to New England; brick symmetry plus dependencies points to Chesapeake/plantation contexts; coquina and bastions point to Spanish Florida.

Expect ideology questions: Federal and neoclassical buildings use classical restraint and temple imagery to express republican virtue, not aristocratic colonial hierarchy.

Expect ethics/context questions: plantation architecture cannot be analyzed only as style; its spatial order is tied to enslaved labor and estate operations.

## Common Mistakes

Do not call every colonial house Georgian. Many early New England houses are post-medieval timber buildings.

Do not ignore slavery when discussing plantation architecture. Dependencies, work yards, and quarters are part of the architectural system.

Do not treat Federal style as the same as colonial Georgian. Federal architecture is later, lighter, and tied to the early republic.

## Key Terms

**First Period / Post-Medieval English** - Early New England domestic tradition with timber frames, steep roofs, and central chimneys.

**Georgian** - Symmetrical eighteenth-century Anglo-American architectural vocabulary.

**Federal Style** - Early-republic style with symmetry, fanlights, delicate ornament, and classical restraint.

**Dependency** - Secondary building supporting estate operations; in plantation contexts it must be read with labor systems.

**Coquina** - Shell limestone used in Spanish Florida fortifications such as Castillo de San Marcos.
