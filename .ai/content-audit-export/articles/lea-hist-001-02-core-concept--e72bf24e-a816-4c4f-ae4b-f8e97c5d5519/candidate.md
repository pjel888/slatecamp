# Mesopotamian Architecture

## LEA Point
Mesopotamian architecture is a mud-brick urban tradition organized around temple platforms, palaces, gates, city walls, and relief programs. Its exam identity comes from material limits, river-plain geography, and the civic-religious role of cities such as Ur, Uruk, Eridu, Babylon, and Assyrian palace centers.

## Source Anchors
- UNESCO Ahwar anchors Uruk, Ur, and Eridu as southern Mesopotamian archaeological cities in a marsh/delta landscape.
- UNESCO Babylon anchors Babylon as a major Mesopotamian city with walls, gates, palaces, temples, and later imperial rebuilding.
- Banister Fletcher's local conversion anchors clay/brick material conditions, ziggurat temple-building, Assyrian palace platforms, guardian figures, relief programs, and arch/vault development.

## Core Pattern
Because the alluvial plain supplied clay more readily than building stone, Mesopotamian builders developed large-scale brick construction. Ziggurats used stepped massing to elevate a sacred focus above the city. Palaces and city gates used platforms, courts, decorated surfaces, and guardian figures to communicate royal authority. Assyrian reliefs should be read as narrative and political architecture, not merely decoration.

## Exam Guardrails
- Do not call a ziggurat an Egyptian tomb; it is a Mesopotamian temple-platform tradition.
- Do not use unsupported exact dimensions or palace layouts unless the local source file anchors them.
- Lamassu identify Assyrian/Mesopotamian guardian figures, not Greek caryatids or Egyptian sphinxes.
- Mud brick explains mass, wall thickness, revetment, and vulnerability; it is not evidence that builders lacked all technical skill.

## Real World Scenario
A board item says a city gate uses glazed brick and guardian figures while a nearby stepped mass supports a temple focus. Source-safe reasoning should classify the setting as Mesopotamian and separate the ziggurat, palace/gate, and relief programs instead of treating them as one building type.
