## Grounds for Suspension and Revocation of the Certificate of Registration

The Certificate of Registration grants architects the legal authority to practice, sign and seal documents, and represent themselves as licensed professionals. This certificate remains in force only while the architect maintains compliance with RA 9266, the Code of Ethical Conduct, and Standards of Professional Practice. The Board of Architecture may suspend or revoke registration upon discovery of grounds establishing violation of law, ethics violations, or professional incompetence.

### RA 9266 Section 23 Statutory Grounds

Section 23 of RA 9266 establishes the primary grounds for suspension or revocation:

**Unauthorized Signing and Sealing (UAP Code of Ethical Conduct, Canon II Sec 2)**
An architect shall not sign and seal plans or documents that the architect did not prepare or that were not prepared under direct supervision. This prohibition protects public safety by ensuring accountability for design content. An architect who affixes a seal to plans prepared by unqualified persons shares responsibility for any defects, code violations, or structural failures. The signature and seal represent the architect's professional judgment and competence regarding all selead documents.

"Direct supervision" requires the architect's personal involvement in design decisions, not merely administrative review of completed work. If the architect is absent during critical design phases, even with periodic check-ins afterward, direct supervision has not occurred. The architect cannot delegate fundamental design responsibility while maintaining supervisory accountability.

**Aiding Unauthorized Practice (UAP Code of Ethical Conduct, Canon II Sec 3)**
An architect shall not aid or abet unauthorized practice of architecture. This extends the responsibility beyond the architect's own conduct to include knowing participation in others' unlicensed practice. An architect who permits non-licensed staff to perform work only licensed architects may perform, without reporting or intervention, aids unauthorized practice.

Examples include allowing an unlicensed employee to negotiate with clients for architecture services, permitting a non-architect to seal documents under the architect's name, or failing to supervise until code violations appear. The architect's knowledge of the unauthorized practice creates liability regardless of benefit received.

**Improper Solicitation (UAP Code of Ethical Conduct, Canon IV)**
An architect shall not openly solicit projects without a valid service agreement or allow exploitation through undertaking services without compensation agreement. This addresses two distinct misconduct forms: (1) public solicitation not backed by legitimate service agreements, and (2) accepting architectural assignments and then claiming the client must pay without written agreement.

The phrase "openly solicited" suggests public or widely-known offers of services. An architect cannot advertise architectural expertise and then deny service obligations based on lack of written agreement. A legitimate service agreement must precede or immediately accompany the engagement offer.

"Actually allowed himself to be exploited" extends the prohibition to situations where the architect passively permits non-payment or accepts work knowing compensation will not follow. This includes donating services for commercial projects (distinguished from legitimate civic or charitable work) or accepting architectural work from clients with established payment refusal patterns.

**Code and Standards Violations (RA 9266 Sec 23)**
An architect shall not violate any provision of RA 9266, its implementing rules and regulations, the Code of Ethical Conduct, or Standards of Professional Practice. This catch-all provision encompasses all Code violations discussed in other articles: obligations to clients, contractors, colleagues, and the public.

### IRR Section 23 Procedural Provisions

The Implementing Rules and Regulations specify the procedures and evidentiary standards applicable to suspension or revocation proceedings.

**Investigation Authority**
The Board may motu proprio (on its own motion) investigate conduct constituting sufficient grounds for suspension or revocation. The Board need not await formal complaint. Discovery of published work showing unselead designs, notice of unsafe construction practices, or evidence of unlicensed persons performing architectural services all trigger Board investigation authority.

**Charges and Documentation**
Any person, firm, or association may prepare charges against a registrant. The charges must be sworn by the person making them and filed with the Secretary of the Board. This requirement creates documentary accountability for allegations while protecting against frivolous complaints requiring Board investigation. A sworn complaint carries legal consequences if allegations prove false or maliciously intended.

**Administrative Investigation Standard**
The Board conducts investigation under the rules on administrative investigation issued by the Commission. The decision becomes final after fifteen (15) days unless appelead to the Commission. The Commission's decision may be appelead to the Court of Appeals in accordance with Rules of Court.

### Suspension vs. Revocation Distinction

While both suspend and revoke registration, these remedies differ significantly:

**Suspension** temporarily removes the architect's legal authority to practice. The Certificate remains technically issued but inactive. After suspension expires or the architect demonstrates compliance restoration, practice authority may resume. Suspension typically applies to remedial violations: an architect who selead work without direct supervision might receive suspension with a requirement to complete ethics training before reinstatement.

**Revocation** permanently withdraws the Certificate. The architect loses all practice authority and must retake the licensure examination to reregister. Revocation applies to serious, non-remedial violations: an architect convicted of falsifying inspection reports or repeatedly sealing work not personally prepared would face revocation.

### The Two-Year Reinstatement Opportunity

RA 9266 Section 24 (as implemented by IRR) permits reinstatement after two years from the revocation date upon application and Board finding that reasons for revocation have been remedied. This provision recognizes that some architects may deserve second chances: an architect revoked for briefly permitting unlicensed practice, who subsequently completed enforcement training and implemented supervision systems, might reapply after two years.

However, the Board retains discretion to deny reinstatement if the architect has not genuinely remedied the conduct justifying revocation. A two-year waiting period alone does not guarantee reinstatement.

### Fraud and Deceit as Special Category

Beyond Section 23 grounds, architects perpetrating fraud in obtaining registration face additional sanction. Fraudulent application for examination, false evidence of experience, misrepresented educational credentials, or false statements in registration applications justify refusal to issue or revocation of registration even if subsequent conduct complies with standards.

### LEA Test Points

1. **Direct supervision scope** — An architect reviews final construction documents prepared by junior architects but does not participate in schematic design or preliminary design phases. This is review, not supervision; direct supervision requires involvement in all critical design stages.

2. **Aiding unauthorized practice identification** — An architect permits a non-licensed engineering technician to design structural systems independently, knowing the person lacks licensure. The architect reviews the work after completion. The architect aids unauthorized practice through permitting the independent design work.

3. **Service agreement necessity** — An architect discusses a building design project with a potential client, and the client requests the architect proceed on a handshake agreement promising to work out compensation details later. Accepting this engagement without written agreement creates vulnerability to exploitation violation.

4. **Catch-all provision application** — An architect violates Code Section 2-h by accepting money from a contractor in exchange for favorable contract administration. This Code violation also constitutes grounds for registration revocation under Section 23-d.

5. **Motu proprio investigation authority** — The Board discovers through publication or professional complaint that an architect has been sealing residential plans with structural systems designed by the architect's employee who lacks engineering licensure. The Board may investigate without awaiting formal written complaint.

6. **Sworn complaint requirement** — A competitor files an unsworn allegation that an architect is unlicensed. The Board cannot initiate investigation based on this unsworn allegation alone; the competitor must file a sworn statement.

7. **Suspension reinstatement timing** — An architect receives a suspension for improper seal use. After completing the suspension term and ethics remediation, the architect may apply for reinstatement. Early reinstatement is at Board discretion if remediation is demonstrated. if remediation demonstrates compliance restoration likelihood.

8. **Revocation permanence** — An architect's registration is revoked for repeatedly sealing work not personally prepared. The architect must retake and pass the licensure examination to reregister, demonstrating renewed competence.

### Misconceptions

Many architects assume suspension and revocation are interchangeable terms. In reality, suspension is temporary and conditional; revocation is permanent unless reinstatement is granted after two years. The distinction affects the architect's career trajectory fundamentally.

Second, some architects believe investigation requires formal written complaint. The Board's motu proprio authority means investigation may begin from any reliable source: published evidence of misconduct, public complaints, professional standards violations discovered through ordinary regulatory oversight.

Third, architects sometimes assume that a one-time violation, if disclosed and corrected, avoids sanction. The Code violations still warrant investigation and potential sanction even if the architect self-reported and implemented preventative measures. The Board's discretion permits considering remediation in deciding suspension vs. revocation severity, but violation alone justifies disciplinary action.

Fourth, some misunderstand the two-year reinstatement provision. Two years' waiting does not guarantee reinstatement; the architect must demonstrate that conditions justifying revocation have been remedied. An architect revoked for sealing unlicensed staff work must show implementation of direct supervision systems and actual compliance for two years before reinstatement becomes likely.

### Practical Application

Architects must ensure direct personal involvement in all design decisions affecting selead documents. Reviewing work after junior architects complete it is insufficient supervision. Weekly design meetings, participation in schematic phase decisions, and involvement in preliminary and final design phases constitute adequate direct supervision.

Before accepting any project engagement, architects should execute written service agreements specifying scope, compensation, and project timeline. Handshake agreements or verbal understandings create vulnerability to exploitation allegations and disputes about whether services were authorized.

Architects should conduct regular audits of all selead documents to ensure they prepared or directly supervised the work. If documents are discovered that do not meet this standard, the architect should notify the Board immediately and take corrective action.

Knowledge that unlicensed persons are performing architectural work creates obligation to report and prevent continuation. An architect who discovers a contractor's in-house "designer" (unlicensed) is performing design work should report this to the Board rather than acquiescing silently.

These requirements protect the public by ensuring that only competent, accountable professionals seal design documents. The threat of suspension or revocation incentivizes architects to maintain high standards of professional responsibility and legal compliance.
