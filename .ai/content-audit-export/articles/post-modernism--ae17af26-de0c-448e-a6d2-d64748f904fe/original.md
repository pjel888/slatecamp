# Post-Modernism

## The Real-World Scenario (The Hook)

You encounter an LEA question showing a photograph of a building with a comically oversized broken pediment, playful color bands, and what appears to be a television antenna sculpted as a decorative finial. The question asks: "Which architectural movement rejects Modernist purity in favor of historical allusion and irony?" You are looking at Post-Modernism — and you need to know its manifesto, its architects, and its key buildings cold.

## Core Concepts

- **Rejection of Modernist Orthodoxy:** Post-Modernism arose in the 1960s–70s as a direct rebellion against the perceived sterility, elitism, and monotony of Late Modernism and the International Style. Where Mies said "Less is More," Robert Venturi countered: **"Less is a bore."**

- **Complexity and Contradiction:** Venturi's seminal book Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture (1966) argued that architecture should embrace ambiguity, irony, historical layering, and the "messy vitality" of real life rather than Modernism's obsessive pursuit of purity and simplicity. He championed the "difficult whole" over the "easy unity."

- **The Decorated Shed vs. The Duck:** In Learning from Las Vegas (1972), Venturi and Denise Scott Brown analyzed the Las Vegas commercial strip and proposed two building types: the "Duck" (where the building's form itself is the symbol — e.g., a building shaped like a duck) and the "Decorated Shed" (a generic building box with applied signs and ornament). They argued most architecture should be the Decorated Shed — ordinary structure with communicative, symbolic surfaces.

- **The Return of Ornament:** Post-Modernism reversed Adolf Loos's Modernist dictum "Ornament and Crime" (1910). Post-Modern architects reintroduced columns, pediments, keystones, arches, and other Classical elements — but used them ironically, out of sclea, in unexpected materials, or in playful combinations to comment on rather than merely copy history.

## LEA Exam Traps and Common Mistakes

- **Trap 1: Confusing Post-Modernism with Neo-Classicism.** Both use Classical elements (columns, pediments), but Post-Modernism uses them ironically, out of sclea, in unexpected colors, or in jarring combinations. Neo-Classicism uses them sincerely and proportionally. The Piazza d'Italia has neon-lit stainless-steel Corinthian columns — that is irony, not reverence.

- **Trap 2: Misattributing "Complexity and Contradiction" to Charles Jencks.** Venturi wrote the manifesto (1966); Jencks named and popularized the movement in The Language of Post-Modern Architecture (1977). Both are essential figures, but their contributions are distinct. LEA distractors often swap them.

- **Trap 3: Confusing the "Duck" with the "Decorated Shed."** The Duck is a building whose entire form is a symbol (a hot-dog-shaped hot-dog stand). The Decorated Shed is a conventional box with signs and ornament applied to its surface. Venturi and Scott Brown preferred the Decorated Shed. LEA questions frequently test this distinction.

## Board-Prep Mnemonics

- **"Venturi's VLC — Vanna house, Less is a bore, Complexity and Contradiction"**: The three things you must associate with Venturi: his first built manifesto (Vanna Venturi House, 1964), his anti-Mies slogan, and his book title.

- **"DUCK = form IS symbol; SHED = form HAS symbol"**: The Duck's shape is the message; the Decorated Shed's sign is the message. "IS" vs. "HAS" — two words to lock the distinction.

## Active Recall Questions

**Question 1.** Robert Venturi's phrase "Less is a bore" was a direct response to which architect's philosophy?

A. Le Corbusier's "machine for living"
B. Frank Lloyd Wright's "organic architecture"
C. Mies van der Rohe's "Less is More"
D. Louis Sullivan's "Form follows function"

> **Correct Answer: C.** Venturi's "Less is a bore" was a direct, deliberate rejection of Mies van der Rohe's Modernist reductivism. It appeared in Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture (1966) and became the rallying cry of Post-Modernism.

**Question 2.** In Learning from Las Vegas (1972), Venturi and Scott Brown describe two types of buildings. Which statement correctly defines the "Decorated Shed"?

A. A building whose overall form acts as a three-dimensional symbol of its function
B. A conventional structural box with applied signs, ornament, and symbolic decoration on its surfaces
C. A building with no decoration of any kind, expressing only its structural system
D. A parametrically generated shell with integrated digital signage

> **Correct Answer: B.** The "Decorated Shed" is an ordinary, economical building form with communicative ornament and signage applied to its surfaces. The "Duck" (Option A) is the building whose form itself is the symbol. Venturi and Scott Brown argued that most architecture should be the Decorated Shed.

**Question 3.** Charles Jencks declared the symbolic "death of Modern Architecture" to have occurred on July 15, 1972. What event did he associate with this date?

A. The demolition of the Pruitt-Igoe housing complex in St. Louis
B. The completion of the Centre Pompidou in Paris
C. The publication of Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture
D. The opening of Philip Johnson's AT&T Building

> **Correct Answer: A.** Jencks famously pinpointed the demolition of the Pruitt-Igoe public housing project (designed by Minoru Yamasaki, completed 1956, demolished 1972) as the symbolic death of Modernism — a failed social housing megaproject that proved Modernist idealism could not solve urban problems. He published this claim in The Language of Post-Modern Architecture (1977).
