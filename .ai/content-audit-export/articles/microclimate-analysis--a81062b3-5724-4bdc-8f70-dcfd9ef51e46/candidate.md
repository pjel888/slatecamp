# Microclimate Analysis

## Board Exam Quick Check

A microclimate question is asking how the site modifies heat, wind, shade, and moisture at human scale. The checked climate-resilience source repeatedly links tree cover, sun path, prevailing winds, open spaces, high-reflectance materials, green roofs, green walls, and shade to urban heat and comfort.

## Source-Grounded Core

The climate-resilience reference tool identifies urban heat as a planning and design issue. It discusses strategies to cool central business districts and growth centers by increasing tree cover, locating green spaces strategically, incorporating sun-path and prevailing-wind considerations in site and district design, and promoting resource-efficient buildings.

The same source describes high urban heat stress in highly concrete areas with limited tree cover and open space. It cites vertical gardens, tree-shading requirements for outdoor parking spaces, solar reflectance standards for roofs and pavements, tree landscaping for open areas, pedestrian shading, setbacks for non-motorized mobility, parks and open-space zones as breezeways and air paths, and density or height incentives for climate-adaptation technologies.

## Microclimate Checklist

For LEA-level site analysis, check:

- Solar exposure and shade at outdoor routes, waiting areas, parking, and open spaces.
- Prevailing wind paths and whether massing blocks or channels them.
- Paved and concrete surfaces that store heat.
- Tree cover, canopy continuity, green roofs, green walls, and landscaped open ground.
- Drainage and permeability because stormwater and heat-island strategies often overlap.
- Public-space comfort and access during extreme heat.

## Sitework Interface

The Siteworks sources support the ground-system side. Geotextiles and geosynthetics can support filtration, drainage, erosion control, and slope stabilization. These details matter because microclimate strategies such as trees, bioswales, rainbeds, and permeable areas must also work with soil, drainage, and erosion conditions.

## LEA Answer Pattern

For exam items, choose the answer that reads sun, wind, shade, vegetation, surface heat, and water together. The strongest design response usually combines planting, shaded movement, reflective or vegetated surfaces, breezeways, drainage, and open-space planning instead of treating temperature as an air-conditioning issue only.
