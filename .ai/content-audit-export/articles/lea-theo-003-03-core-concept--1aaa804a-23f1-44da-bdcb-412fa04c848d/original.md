## What You Need to Know for the LEA

<Callout>Kinesthetics is the study of the body in motion through space. The LEA tests your understanding of how architectural sequences—stairs, ramps, corridors, thresholds, transitions—shape the experience of moving through a building. You must know the Philippine legal standards for these elements: PD 1096 (National Building Code), RA 9514 (Fire Code), and BP 344 (Accessibility Law). These dimensions are heavily tested because they are non-negotiable safety and accessibility requirements; poor stair design causes falls; poor ramp design excludes wheelchair users.</Callout>

## Core Concept

Architecture is not experienced as a frozen image or a floor plan. It is lived through the body in motion. Walking up a stair, navigating a corridor, crossing a threshold, ascending a ramp—these actions are architectural events. Kinesthetics elevates motion from mere circulation to aesthetic and psychological experience. Le Corbusier called it the _promenade architecturlea_ (architectural promenade): the carefully choreographed sequence of spaces that unfolds as one moves through a building. Every corner turn, every step up or down, every change in ceiling height or light is intentional and creates meaning.

<Tip>Kinesthetics = **the body in motion through architecture**. It's not just circulation efficiency—it's choreography, rhythm, and meaning-making through movement sequences.</Tip>

**The Kinesthetic Dimensions:**

**Stairs** are the most tested kinesthetic element. Stair design is governed by the National Building Code (PD 1096) and the Fire Code (RA 9514). These codes set minimum safety requirements, but ergonomic comfort is also tested.

- **Riser (vertical height of each step):** PD 1096 Rule VIII Sec. 806(b) allows **maximum 200 mm for residential** and **maximum 190 mm for commercial**; the minimum is 100 mm. Comfortable riser heights typically fall between 150–180 mm, but the LEA tests the code limits. Too low (< 100 mm) is a trip hazard; too high (> 200 mm) is dangerous and non‑compliant.

- **Tread (horizontal depth of each step):** PD 1096 Rule VIII Sec. 806(b) requires **minimum 250 mm** for both residential and commercial. The Fire Code (RA 9514) also requires a minimum tread of 279 mm (11 inches) for exit stairs. A comfortable tread is between 250–300 mm. The proportional rule **2 × riser + tread = 600–630 mm** is an ergonomic guideline (not a code requirement) that ensures a comfortable stair. For example, a riser of 170 mm and tread of 280 mm gives 2×170 + 280 = 620 mm.

- **Consistency:** All risers and treads in a single stair run must be uniform. PD 1096 allows a maximum variation of **5 mm** in riser heights and tread depths within a flight. Even a small variation creates a trip hazard. The LEA tests: "Why must all riser heights in a stair flight be uniform?" Answer: Inconsistency disrupts the body's rhythm and creates falls; muscle memory expects equal steps.

<Note>The **2R+T = 600–630mm** rule (2 × riser + tread depth) is an ergonomic guideline ensuring stairs feel proportionally comfortable. It's not a code requirement, but it's the gold standard for step design.</Note>

- **Handrails:** PD 1096 Rule VIII Sec. 806 requires handrails on both sides for stairs wider than 1.10 m, and for any stair with more than 3 risers. The handrail height must be **800–900 mm** measured vertically from the tread nosing. BP 344 (Accessibility Law) specifies an additional handrail at 700 mm for children. Handrails must be graspable (30–50 mm diameter) and extend at least 300 mm beyond the top and bottom of the flight.

**Ramps** are the kinesthetic equivleant of stairs for wheelchair users and those with mobility aids. The accessibility slope is critical and governed by BP 344.

- **Maximum slope:** BP 344 2024 Revised IRR Section 5.2.2 requires **1:20 maximum** for accessible ramps in buildings. The 1:12 slope is only allowed for curb ramps or very short ramps (≤1.5 m vertical rise). A 1‑meter vertical rise therefore requires 20 meters of horizontal ramp length. The LEA tests: "An accessible ramp in a building must not exceed a slope of (a) 1:8 (b) 1:10 (c) 1:12 (d) 1:20." Answer: (d) 1:20. A variant: "To span a 1.5 m vertical height with a 1:20 ramp, the minimum horizontal run is (a) 15 m (b) 20 m (c) 30 m (d) 40 m." Answer: (c) 30 m (1.5 × 20 = 30 m).

- **Maximum rise per run:** BP 344 limits the rise per ramp run to **750 mm** before a landing is required. This prevents fatigue and allows rest. A long, uninterrupted ramp of 20+ meters is psychologically and physically exhausting.

- **Width:** Minimum clear width is **1.20 m** (BP 344). A ramp 0.80 m wide accommodates a single wheelchair but blocks two‑way movement; 1.20 m allows two‑way traffic or a wheelchair with a companion.

**Corridors and Passages** are kinesthetic channels. The body navigates corridors through proprioceptive sensing (feeling the boundary walls). Dimensions are governed by PD 1096 and RA 9514.

- **Single‑file passage:** PD 1096 Rule VIII Sec. 804 requires **minimum 0.90 m** clear width for residential corridors. At 0.90 m, a single person passes; two people must synchronize or one steps aside.

- **Two‑way traffic:** For corridors serving more than one dwelling unit or for communal areas, PD 1096 generally requires **1.20 m** minimum. At 1.20 m, two people can pass without deliberate accommodation; the corridor feels safe and welcoming. Fire exit corridors must also meet the minimum width requirements of RA 9514.

- **Perspective and compression:** A long, straight corridor at 1.20 m feels boring; a corridor with 30–50 mm height variation, intermediate walls, or alcoves feels layered. This is the **compression‑release** principle: a narrow, low space (compression) opens into a tall, wide space (release), creating psychological rhythm.

**Thresholds and Transitions** mark kinesthetic change.

- **Door thresholds:** BP 344 requires that floor surfaces at doorways be level (vertical change ≤ 6 mm) for accessibility. A slight step of 15–20 mm can signal a change in territory, but it must be beveled if between 6–13 mm, and any vertical change over 13 mm requires a ramp.

- **Level changes:** A step of 300–600 mm between spaces clearly separates territories; below 300 mm, the body may not consciously perceive it; above 600 mm without a ramp or stair, the body is forced to stop and acknowledge the boundary.

**Compression-Release (Promenade Architecturlea):**

Le Corbusier taught that architecture unfolds as a sequence. A visitor enters a low, narrow vestibule (compression), then steps into a soaring atrium (release). The contrast creates emotional impact. The sequence of compression and release, repeated through a building, creates rhythm and meaning. The LEA tests this as design critique: "How does the sequence of tall atrium → narrow corridor → intimate alcove → tall gallery exemplify promenade architecturlea?" Answer: The alternation of open (release) and confined (compression) spaces creates psychological rhythm and guides the user through meaning-making.

**Why kinesthetics matters for the LEA:**

Kinesthetics is tested because it is where perception (THEO-003) meets design responsibility. A student might design a beautiful floor plan but create an unusable stair or an inaccessible ramp. The LEA assumes that architects know the Philippine legal dimensions and understand their human and safety implications. Additionally, kinesthetics is where the body's agency and the architect's intention meet. A student who understands compression-release, threshold meaning, and stair rhythm creates buildings that feel intentional and welcoming rather than accidental and disorienting.

<Warn>The most common error: designing a beautiful plan but ignoring kinesthetic details (stair dimensions, ramp slopes, corridor widths, threshold heights). In the body's actual experience of moving through space, kinesthetic failures become immediately obvious. Dimensional accuracy is non-negotiable.</Warn>

## How It's Tested on the LEA


<ExamTip>1. **Direct Stair Dimension Recall (MCQ):** "According to PD 1096, the maximum riser height for residential stairs is (a) 150 mm (b) 180 mm (c) 200 mm (d) 250 mm." Answer: (c) 200 mm. Straightforward recall of code limits.</ExamTip>


<ExamTip>2. **Stair Design Calculation via 2R+T Rule:** "A stair has a 170 mm riser. Using the 2R+T = 600–630 mm ergonomic guideline, what is the ideal tread depth? (a) 230 mm (b) 260 mm (c) 290 mm (d) 320 mm." Answer: (c) 290 mm (2×170+290 = 630 mm). Tests understanding of proportional relationship, not just memorization.</ExamTip>


<ExamTip>3. **Accessibility Ramp Slope:** "An accessible ramp in a building must span a 1.5 m vertical height. What is the minimum horizontal length at the maximum allowable slope? (a) 15 m (b) 20 m (c) 30 m (d) 40 m." Answer: (c) 30 m (1.5 m × 20 = 30 m, using 1:20 slope per BP 344). Tests calculation and accessibility law knowledge.</ExamTip>


<ExamTip>4. **Corridor Psychology and Comfort:** "A residential circulation corridor should be at least (a) 800 mm (b) 900 mm (c) 1200 mm (d) 1500 mm to allow two people to pass without discomfort." Answer: (c) 1200 mm. Tests understanding that proxemic comfort requires adequate width.</ExamTip>


<ExamTip>5. **Compression-Release Sequence (Design Critique):** "In Le Corbusier's promenade architecturlea, the sequence low‑narrow entrance → tall central atrium → intimate room → broad gallery exemplifies (a) poor planning (b) compression‑release rhythm (c) accessibility failure (d) structural inefficiency." Answer: (b) compression‑release rhythm. Tests conceptual understanding of kinesthetic experience.</ExamTip>


<ExamTip>6. **Threshold Meaning:** "A 20 mm step at a building entrance signals (a) construction error (b) transition/boundary recognition (c) accessibility violation (d) no particular meaning." Answer: (b) transition/boundary recognition, but note that under BP 344 a 20 mm step is an accessibility violation unless it is beveled or a ramp is provided. The LEA may test both the semantic and the regulatory aspect.</ExamTip>

## Key Terms

**Kinesthetics** — The study of the body in motion through space, including the muscular, proprioceptive, and psychological experience of moving through architecture.

**Promenade architecturlea** — Le Corbusier's term for the carefully choreographed sequence of spaces and experiences as one moves through a building; a fundamental principle of kinesthetic design.

**Riser** — The vertical face of a stair step, regulated by PD 1096 (max 200 mm residential, 190 mm commercial; min 100 mm).

**Tread** — The horizontal surface of a stair step, regulated by PD 1096 (min 250 mm) and RA 9514 (min 279 mm for exit stairs).

**2R+T rule** — An ergonomic proportional formula (2 × riser height + tread depth = 600–630 mm) ensuring comfort; not a code requirement but widely used in design.

**Handrail** — A graspable rail (30–50 mm diameter) positioned at 800–900 mm (main) and 700 mm (child) per PD 1096 and BP 344; must extend 300 mm beyond the flight.

**Ramp slope** — The angle of incline, expressed as rise:run ratio. Under BP 344 2024 IRR, building ramps must be 1:20 maximum; curb ramps may be 1:12.

**Accessible design (kinesthetic context)** — Design that accommodates wheelchair users, elderly, and disabled persons, including ramp slopes (1:20 max), handrails, and level changes (≤6 mm vertical; 6–13 mm beveled; >13 mm requires ramp).

**Compression-release** — A rhythmic kinesthetic principle in which narrow, low spaces (compression) alternate with tall, wide spaces (release), creating psychological and emotional impact.

**Threshold** — A boundary between spaces, often marked by a slight change in floor level; regulated by accessibility laws to be ≤6 mm or beveled.

**Handrail extension** — The portion of a handrail that extends 300 mm beyond the top and bottom of a stair flight, ensuring users can grip while transitioning.

**Corridor psychology** — The spatial and sensory experience of moving through a corridor, influenced by width (900 mm single‑file, 1200 mm two‑way), height, light, and surface texture.
