### The Problem Seeking Methodology (Peña & Parshall)

William Peña's five-step framework, developed at Caudill Rowlett Scott (CRS) and published in *Problem Seeking: An Architectural Programming Primer* (1977, revised 2001), establishes the industry standard. The five steps are: (1) establish the client; (2) establish goals; (3) gather facts; (4) identify and unify the problem; and (5) test the problem.

**Step 1: Establish the Client** requires identifying not merely the paying party but all stakeholders—end users, operators, management, regulators, and community. A retail bank programming must account for the teller, the loan officer, the customer, the security officer, and compliance auditors. Misidentifying the client or ignoring a stakeholder class leads to incomplete programs and design failure.

**Step 2: Establish Goals** captures the overarching mission and measurable outcomes. Goals are ideological statements: a school library seeks to foster independent learning; a hospital seeks to provide compassionate care while optimizing clinical workflow. Goals inform every subsequent programming decision and constrain design solutions.

**Step 3: Gather Facts** is the systematic collection of data through multiple methods: interviews, surveys, observation, analysis of existing buildings, study of regulatory codes, benchmarking against comparable projects, and testing of technical systems. Peña identifies four categories of facts: human (user behavior, demographic data, psychological needs), activity (what tasks occur, frequency, duration, staffing), relationships (which functions must be adjacent, which must be separated), and physical (site characteristics, climatic conditions, infrastructure capacity).

**Step 4: Identify and Unify the Problem** synthesizes gathered facts into explicit problem statements. This step reveals contradictions, constraints, and requirements that the design must satisfy. The problem statement becomes the binding document for design; without it, designers operate in ambiguity.

**Step 5: Test the Problem** validates the program against real-world conditions before design begins. This may involve review by users, testing against historical performance data of similar buildings, or simulation of operational scenarios. Testing reveals oversights and confirms that the program is complete and accurate.

### Information Gathering Methods

Effective programming requires multiple information sources. **Client interviews** establish organizational structure, decision-making authority, budget, timeline, and long-term vision. **User interviews and surveys** gather behavioral data: how occupants currently work, what frustrates them, what facilitates productivity. **Observational studies** document actual use patterns—where people naturally congregate, where conflicts arise, how spaces are occupied or abandoned.

**Benchmarking studies** compare area allocations, staffing ratios, and space standards against peer institutions or precedent projects. A programmer designing a mid-size law firm office may study three comparable existing firms to establish baseline area per attorney (typically 200–250 square feet net), support staff ratios, conference room count, and library requirements. **Code analysis** confirms minimum and optimal room dimensions, circulation widths, egress requirements, and accessibility standards. **Site analysis** documents existing conditions, infrastructure, environmental factors, and development constraints.

### Programming vs. Design: The Critical Distinction

Programming produces the program document—a text specification stating what spaces are required, their areas, adjacencies, functional relationships, performance requirements, and constraints. A program might specify: "Reception area: 200–300 net SF, must be visible from entry, must accommodate 4 visitors waiting, acoustically separated from executive offices, single public restroom adjacent."

Design then interprets this prescription spatially: the designer decides the form, layout, materials, and aesthetic language. The program does not prescribe that reception is rectangular, round, or split-level; it prescribes only functional and area requirements. A common LEA candidate error is conflating program diagrams (bubble diagrams, adjacency matrices) with preliminary design—they are not designs, they are analytical documents that organize programmatic relationships before form-making begins.

### Program Components and Deliverables

A complete architectural program contains: (1) **project narrative** establishing mission and goals; (2) **stakeholder analysis** identifying all users and decision-makers; (3) **space program** listing all required spaces, their areas, adjacencies, and functional requirements, often presented as an **area summary table**; (4) **adjacency matrix or bubble diagram** showing required relationships between spaces; (5) **performance standards** specifying environmental conditions, acoustic targets, lighting requirements, and operational constraints; (6) **code compliance summary** documenting applicable building codes, zoning, and accessibility standards; and (7) **assumptions and constraints** clarifying what was assumed in the program and what external factors constrain the design solution.

### The Program Area Summary

The area summary table quantifies net and gross areas for each space, calculates total net area, applies department circulation multipliers (10–20% depending on building type), and projects total gross area. A typical healthcare program multiplies net clinical area by 1.35 to 1.50 to account for corridors, mechanical spaces, and structural walls—the gross multiplier—yielding the target building envelope. This is testable: if a hospital program specifies 50,000 net SF of clinical space and a 1.40 multiplier, the anticipated building is 70,000 gross SF.

### Information Analysis

Once gathered, information must be analyzed and synthesized. Peña's approach uses **graphic analysis**—diagramming relationships, flows, and conflicts. Which functions generate noise that cannot disturb others? Which must be proximate to reduce walking distance? Which benefit from daylighting, which require controlled darkness? Analysis transforms raw data into actionable conclusions that form the basis of the problem statement.

## Key Terms

**Architectural Programming** — Systematic investigation and analysis phase preceding design that documents space requirements, functional relationships, performance standards, and constraints.

**Problem Seeking** — William Peña's five-step methodology for architectural programming: establish client, establish goals, gather facts, identify problem, test problem.

**Net Assignable Area (NAA)** — Floor area assigned to functional program spaces (offices, classrooms, clinics), excluding circulation and infrastructure.

**Gross Building Area (GBA)** — Total enclosed floor area; calculated as NAA divided by efficiency factor, accounting for circulation, structure, and mechanical spaces.

**Adjacency Matrix** — Table documenting spatial relationships between functions: essential (proximity required), desirable (beneficial proximity), unimportant, or undesirable (separation required).

**Program Document** — The binding written specification of all required spaces, their areas, adjacencies, performance requirements, and operational constraints.

**Stakeholder** — Any individual, group, or entity affected by or responsible for the building's operation: end users, operators, owners, regulators, community.

**Performance Specifications** — Quantifiable and testable requirements for environmental conditions, acoustics, lighting, thermal performance, and operational standards.

**Efficiency Factor** — Ratio of net assignable area to gross building area; values typically range 0.60 to 0.85 depending on building type and complexity.

**Code Compliance** — Verification that the program satisfies applicable building codes, zoning ordinances, accessibility standards (ADA, DPWH), and life safety requirements.

**Area Summary Table** — Quantitative schedule listing all spaces, their quantities, net areas, and contributions to total net and gross building area.

**Circulation Analysis** — Study of movement patterns and required connections between spaces to inform adjacency and layout decisions.
