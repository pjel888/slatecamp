## Learning Goal

Identify the three catalysts of Neoclassicism — archaeological discoveries (Pompeii, Herculaneum), Grand Tour travel, and Enlightenment rationalism — and recognize key buildings (La Madeleine, Brandenburg Gate, US Capitol) by their revival of Greek and Roman forms.
## Core Concept

**Neoclassicism** emerged from Enlightenment values: reason, order, moral clarity, and a romantic (though often romanticized) view of Classical Antiquity as an ideal society. After Baroque drama and Rococo frivolity, architects consciously rejected both styles as decadent and looked backward—but with *archaeological rigor* and modern ambitions.

The style crystallizes through the mid-18th century with key catalysts:
- **Archaeological expeditions** to Pompeii and Herculaneum (beginning 1748) revealed actual Roman buildings and interiors
- **James Stuart and Nicholas Revett's *Antiquities of Athens*** (Volume I, 1762; continued through 1816) provided measured drawings of Greek temples, spurring Greek Revival
- **Enlightenment philosophy** valorizing reason, democracy, and virtue associated with Classical republics (Athens, Rome)
- **Rejection of Rococo** by intellectuals and reformers as morally corrupt and aesthetically frivolous

**Neoclassicism divides into two strands:**
- **Roman Classicism** (classical refinement, symmetry, proportional restraint, often earlier)
- **Greek Revival** (emphasis on Greek orders and temples, often later and more archaeologically driven)

**Jacques-Germain Soufflot** (1713–1780) designed the **Panthéon** (designed 1755, construction begun 1758, completed 1790, Paris), the quintessential early Neoclassical church. Soufflot reinterpreted the Baroque church as a rational Classical temple: a portico with Corinthian columns, a dome modeled on the Panthéon in Rome, and a barrel-vaulted interior organized by rational proportion rather than theatrical effect. The design consciously merges Classical temple form (portico, dome) with modern engineering and Enlightenment spirituality—the "Temple of Great Men" rather than a Counter-Reformation spectacle.

**Brandenburg Gate** (**Carl Gotthard Langhans**, 1788–1791, Berlin) is a paradigmatic Greek Revival monument. Modeled on the Propylaea of the Athenian Acropolis, it uses Doric columns, a pediment, and symmetrical proportions. The gate became Berlin's first Greek Revival building and an icon of Neoclassical civic monumentality.

**Thomas Jefferson** (1743–1826), influenced by Greek Revival and Palladio, designed **Monticello** (1768–1809, Charlottesville, Virginia)—a dome-crowned villa with Ionic portico, Classical proportions, and humanistic rationality. Jefferson's *Notes on the State of Virginia* championed the Classical aesthetic as aligned with American democratic ideals.

**Neoclassical principles:**
- **Rational geometry**: Straight lines, rectangles, circles—no Baroque undulation
- **Archaeological accuracy**: Based on measured drawings and excavated remains (not imagination)
- **Moral clarity**: Simplicity and austerity as virtues; decoration reduced and functional
- **Proportional harmony**: Classical orders and ratios govern design (derived from Renaissance and antiquity)
- **Intellectual restraint**: Ornament appears only where structurally justified
- **Public monumentality**: Civic buildings (government, culture, religion) assert Enlightenment values
- **Domestic refinement**: Interiors use simpler versions of Classical orders and motifs

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Note" status="preserved"]

**Neoclassical aesthetics contrast sharply with predecessors:**
- vs. Baroque: rational instead of emotional, straight instead of curved, restrained instead of exuberant
- vs. Rococo: symmetrical instead of asymmetrical, public instead of intimate, intellectual instead of playful
- vs. Medieval and Renaissance: more *archaeologically grounded* in actual antique sources

**Regional development:**
- **France**: Soufflot, Gabriel, Peyre—academic, proportionally refined, government-sponsored
- **Britain**: Stuart, Revett, Soane—Greek Revival emphasis, interior innovation (John Soane's Museum shows Neoclassical spatial complexity)
- **America**: Thomas Jefferson, Charles Bulfinch—democratic ideals, temple-form civic buildings, plantation villas
- **Germany/Prussia**: Karl Friedrich Schinkel—refined, proportionally exquisite, influential on 19th-century architecture
- **Iberia**: Late Baroque transitioning slowly; Neoclassicism arrives later and sometimes overlaps with local Baroque traditions

## Key Facts and Testable Items

**Key Architects and Their Contributions:**
- **Jacques-Germain Soufflot (1713–1780)**: Synthesized French Classical tradition with Greek temple forms; Panthéon as breakthrough
- **James Stuart (1713–1788) & Nicholas Revett**: Archaeological measurement of Greek temples; *Antiquities of Athens* (Vol. I, 1762–continuing) as authoritative source
- **Carl Gotthard Langhans (1732–1808)**: Brandenburg Gate as Greek Revival paradigm
- **Thomas Jefferson (1743–1826)**: Introduced Greek Revival and Palladianism to America; democratic ideals in Classical form
- **Karl Friedrich Schinkel (1781–1841)**: Prussian refinement; proportional exquisiteness; museum typology
- **John Soane (1753–1837)**: Interior spatial innovation within Neoclassical vocabulary; personal aesthetic laboratory

## Key Terms

- **Neoclassicism**: An architectural and design movement (late 18th–early 19th centuries) based on Enlightenment values and archaeological revival of Classical Antiquity
- **Greek Revival**: A strand of Neoclassicism emphasizing Greek temple orders and forms; emerged in the 1790s, based on Stuart and Revett's measured drawings
- **Roman Classicism**: A strand of Neoclassicism emphasizing Roman grandeur, domes, and imperial scale
- **Doric order**: The simplest Greek column order; sturdy and tapered
- **Ionic order**: A Greek column order with scroll-like capitals; more graceful than Doric
- **Corinthian order**: The most ornate Classical column order; used in grand Neoclassical buildings
- **Portico**: A covered entrance with columns; common in Neoclassical temples and civic buildings
- **Rotunda**: A circular building or central circular space, often covered by a dome
- **Entablature**: The horizontal structure above columns (architrave, frieze, cornice)
- **Archaeological accuracy**: Design based on measured drawings and excavated remains, not imagination or previous stylistic conventions
