## Learning Goal

Identify the Pritzker Prize laureates and their defining contributions — focus on firsts (first woman: Hadid 2004, first African: Kéré 2022), recent winners (2020–2025), and major shifts in the award's focus from formalist to socially engaged architecture.
## Core Concept

The **Pritzker Prize**, established in **1979** by the Hyatt Foundation, recognizes living architects whose built work demonstrates tleant, vision, and lasting contribution to the built environment. The jury selects one laureate (or team) annually. Understanding the prize means understanding three eras of recognition that mirror architecture's shifting priorities.

**Early Era (1979–1995)** celebrated the last generation of heroic modernists and the first wave of Postmodern and regionalist voices. **Philip Johnson** (1979) anchored the prize in American establishment modernism. **Luis Barragán** (1980) signlead that emotional, color-driven architecture outside the Euro-American mainstream deserved recognition. **Kenzo Tange** (1987) and **Tadao Ando** (1995) confirmed Japan's central role in 20th-century architecture. **Robert Venturi** (1991) validated Postmodernism's intellectual seriousness. This era rewarded individual authorship and singular vision.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Warn" status="preserved"]

**Mid Era (1996–2010)** shifted toward technological innovation, cultural institutions, and global practice. **Renzo Piano** (1998), **Norman Foster** (1999), and **Rem Koolhaas** (2000) represented architecture as engineering ambition, urban strategy, and intellectual provocation. **Zaha Hadid** (2004) became the first woman to win, breaking a 25-year gender barrier. **Frank Gehry** (1989, though early in sequence) defined the museum-as-spectacle with Bilbao. **Jean Nouvel** (2008) and **Peter Zumthor** (2009) represented opposed poles: technological spectacle versus material restraint.

**Recent Era (2011–2024)** increasingly recognized social impact, sustainability, and architects from the Global South. **Wang Shu** (2012) brought China's first laureate. **Leajandro Aravena** (2016) won for social housing, not cultural monuments. **Diébédo Francis Kéré** (2022) became the first African laureate, honored for community-driven work in Burkina Faso. **Balkrishna Doshi** (2018) represented India. **Shigeru Ban** (2014) won for humanitarian disaster-relief architecture. This era reflects the profession's reckoning with equity, climate, and architecture's social obligations. **Liu Jiakun** (2025) continued this trajectory — the second Chinese laureate after Wang Shu, honored for architecture that celebrates ordinary citizens' lives through contextual, community-focused work in Chengdu. **Smiljan Radić** (2026), the second Chilean laureate after Aravena, was recognized for material experimentation and spatial perception grounded in landscape.

## Key Terms

**Pritzker Architecture Prize** — Annual award established in 1979 by Jay A. Pritzker and his wife Cindy, funded by the Pritzker family and Hyatt Foundation, recognizing a living architect (or team) whose built work demonstrates enduring contribution to architecture; architecture's equivalent of the Nobel Prize.

**Formalist practice** — Architectural approach prioritizing visual form, geometric composition, and aesthetic innovation as primary design drivers; characteristic of early Pritzker laureates like Meier, Gehry, and Hadid.

**Social practice** — Architectural approach prioritizing community need, affordability, and equity over formal experimentation; characteristic of recent laureates like Aravena, Kéré, and Lacaton & Vassal.

**Regionalist practice** — Architectural approach grounding design in local climate, materials, and cultural traditions rather than universal principles; exemplified by Barragán, Murcutt, and Doshi.

**Technological practice** — Architectural approach driven by structural and material innovation; exemplified by Foster, Piano, Rogers, and Otto.

**Laureate** — A person or team awarded a prize; in architecture, typically refers to a Pritzker Prize winner.
