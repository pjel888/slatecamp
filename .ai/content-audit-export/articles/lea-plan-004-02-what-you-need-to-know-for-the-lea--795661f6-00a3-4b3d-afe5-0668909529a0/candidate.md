## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal
Analyze ecological site conditions — watershed hydrology, biodiversity, geological hazards, vegetation — and apply findings to site planning decisions, identify PD 1067 water body easements, and integrate ecological constraints with development programming.

Ecological considerations in site planning address water, biodiversity, environmental hazards, and the living systems that surround and integrate with human settlement. This is not environmental law (that belongs to other articles)—this is what a site planner needs to understand about the natural conditions on a site and how those conditions constrain or enable development.

### Watershed and Hydrology

Every site sits within a watershed—the land area that drains to a particular stream, river, or coastal area. Understanding the watershed tells you where water comes from and where it goes.

**Key Concepts**

1. **Drainage patterns** — Water flows downslope along predictable paths. On a site with contours, you can see drainage direction: water flows perpendicular to contour lines, downslope.

2. **Groundwater and aquifers** — Underground water stored in soil and rock layers. The water table is the depth where groundwater begins. Sites with high water tables require special foundation design.

3. **Stormwater runoff** — When rain falls, it either infiltrates soil, flows overland, or evaporates. Impervious surfaces (pavement, roofs) increase runoff and reduce infiltration. Steep slopes increase runoff velocity.

4. **Water balance** — Inputs (rainfall, groundwater inflow) minus outputs (evapotranspiration, runoff, deep percolation) determine whether a site is wet or dry seasonally.

**Site Planning Implications**
- Sites in upper watershed areas have minimal inflow and depend on rainfall
- Mid-watershed sites collect runoff from upslope and generate runoff to downslope neighbors
- Lower watershed sites (near rivers or coast) receive upstream flows and must plan for flooding

### Flood Zones and Flood Risk

Flooding occurs when water exceeds the channel capacity of rivers, esteros (streams), or the drainage system.

**Flood-Prone Areas in the Philippines**

Metro Manila and coastal cities frequently flood because:
- High water tables and sea level proximity
- Inadequate estero and drainage infrastructure
- Upstream watershed degradation increases runoff
- Typhoons bring intense rainfall (300+ mm in 24 hours)
- Storm surge combines with high tides in coastal areas

**What Site Planners Document**

1. **Historical flood elevation** — Using flood records or witness accounts, determine how high water has reached during past typhoons or heavy rains
2. **Flood return period** — 5-year, 10-year, 25-year flood elevations help assess risk. A site flooded in a 5-year event is riskier than one flooded only in a 25-year event
3. **Flood source** — Is flooding from river overflow, storm surge, inadequate drainage, or groundwater rise?
4. **Site elevation relative to historical flood marks** — If the site is below historical flood levels, development requires flood-proofing strategies

**Design Responses**
- Elevate sensitive uses (housing, offices) above the 25-year flood level
- Use ground floors for parking or flood-resilient storage (not housing)
- Install flood barriers or closures
- Maintain or restore wetlands and detention areas for natural flood absorption

### Wetlands and Water Bodies

Wetlands are permanently or seasonally inundated areas—mangroves, marshes, fishponds, rice paddies. They are among the most productive ecosystems.

**Why Wetlands Matter for Site Planning**

1. **Flood buffering** — Wetlands absorb and slowly release floodwater, reducing downstream flooding
2. **Biodiversity** — Fish, crustaceans, birds, and plants depend on wetlands for survival
3. **Water purification** — Wetlands filter sediment and absorb excess nutrients
4. **Livelihoods** — Wetland-dependent communities (fishers, rice farmers) depend on these areas for survival

**Water Body Easements (Referenced, Not Fully Taught Here)**

PD 1067 (Water Code of the Philippines) establishes mandatory easements from water bodies:
- **3 meters** from rivers/streams less than 3 meters wide
- **20 meters** from rivers/streams 3-20 meters wide
- **40 meters** from rivers/streams over 20 meters wide or navigable, and from lake shores/seas

These easements are no-build zones for permanent structures. Full treatment of PD 1067 and its application belongs to LEA-PLAN-005-03.

### Biodiversity and Habitat

A site may support native species—birds, fish, insects, plants. Development often fragments or destroys habitat.

**What Site Planners Assess**

1. **Vegetation types** — Are there native trees, secondary growth, or introduced/invasive species? What ecological role do they play?
2. **Fauna** — What birds, fish, mammals, or insects use the site? Are any threatened or endemic?
3. **Habitat connectivity** — Is the site isolated or does it connect to larger protected areas or corridors?
4. **Protected species** — The site or adjacent areas may be home to species protected by law (e.g., Philippine eagle, tarsier)

**Design Considerations**
- Preserve native vegetation where possible
- Create green corridors and stepping stones for wildlife movement
- Restore degraded areas with native species
- Manage exotic invasive species (water hyacinth, calamansi that escapes)

### Slope Stability and Landslide Risk

Steep, unstable slopes are hazardous.

**Indicators of Slope Instability**

1. **Soil composition** — Clay-rich soils are less stable than well-compacted sands; volcanic soils can collapse if saturated
2. **Slope angle** — Slopes steeper than 35–45° (depending on soil) are prone to slides
3. **Groundwater seepage** — Water emerging from a hillside indicates saturation; wet slopes are unstable
4. **Scarps and evidence of past movement** — Curved tree trunks, tilted utilities, bare soil patches, or tension cracks indicate previous slides
5. **Vegetation loss** — Areas recently deforested are highly prone to slides

**Site Planning Response**
- Do not build on slopes showing active instability indicators
- If development occurs, install slope stabilization (terracing, retaining walls, drainage)
- Maintain forest cover to bind soil
- Avoid removing vegetation from saturated slopes

### Soil and Geotechnical Conditions

Soil type, depth, and compaction affect buildability and foundation costs.

**Soil Classification**
- **Clay** — Fine particles; low permeability; compressible if soft; stable if properly compacted
- **Silt** — Between clay and sand; variable stability
- **Sand** — Larger particles; permeable; stable when compacted; liquefiable if saturated and shaken
- **Gravel** — Large particles; stable; good bearing capacity

**Geotechnical Risk in Philippine Sites**

1. **Soft clay in Metro Manila** — Deep soft clay layers require piles or special foundations; settlement risk is high
2. **Volcanic soils in Luzon** — Pumice and ash layers can be unstable when saturated; caves and voids are common
3. **Liquefaction near coasts** — Saturated sand near the water table can lose strength during earthquakes
4. **Buried obstacles** — Old foundations, debris, utilities, or unexploded ordnance (in WWII sites) may complicate excavation

**Site Planning Implications**
- A geotechnical investigation (test boring, soil lab analysis) is essential before design
- Poor soils increase foundation costs; consider site suitability before zoning or approving projects
- Avoid development on sites with unresolved geotechnical hazards

### Air Quality and Microclimate

Urban sites experience different air quality and temperature than rural areas.

**Urban Heat Island**
- Pavement and buildings absorb heat; vegetation is reduced
- Urban areas are 2–5°C warmer than surrounding rural areas
- Air stagnates over cities, trapping pollution

**Site Planning Mitigation**
- Increase tree canopy and vegetation
- Use lighter-colored pavement and roofing to reduce heat absorption
- Maintain ventilation corridors (gaps between buildings) for air flow
- Avoid blocking prevailing winds with dense built form

### Seismic and Geological Hazards

The Philippines sits on the Ring of Fire and experiences frequent earthquakes. Some sites are at higher risk than others.

**Fault Lines and PHIVOLCS Setbacks**

Active fault traces require setbacks. Structures must be at least **10 meters** from both sides of active fault traces (per PHIVOLCS/NBCP guidelines). Sites straddling a fault are unsuitable for habitation.

**Liquefaction Hazard**
- Saturated sand and silt near the water table may liquefy in earthquakes
- Coastal and low-lying sites are at higher risk
- Liquefaction reduces bearing capacity and causes differential settlement
- Design response: deeper piles, improved soil, or avoidance

**Tsunami and Storm Surge Risk**

Coastal sites are vulnerable to tsunami and typhoon storm surge.

**Post-Typhoon Yolanda Framework**: A **40-meter no-build zone** from mean high tide was established in select high-risk coastal municipalities (NDRP post-Yolanda, 2014). This restricts development in the immediate coastal strip, prioritizing evacuation space and reducing casualty risk.

### Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) and ECC

Sites classified as Environmentally Critical Projects (ECPs) or located in Environmentally Critical Areas (ECAs) require an **Environmental Compliance Certificate (ECC)** issued by DENR (Department of Environment and Natural Resources, not DHSUD).

**Reference Note**: Full treatment of PD 1586 (Philippine Environmental Impact Statement System) and ECC requirements belongs to LEA-PLAN-005-03. In this article, the planner notes: _"If the site is in an ECA or the project is an ECP, an EIA and ECC will be required."_ That is the extent of ecological site planning's role.

**Ecological Site Planning Does Not**
- Conduct the full EIA (that is DENR's role)
- Determine ECC requirements (that is DENR's determination)
- Prepare environmental compliance documents (that belongs to environmental specialists)

**What Site Planning Does**
- Identify if the site is in or near sensitive ecological areas
- Note whether ecological constraints exist
- Flag that further environmental assessment is needed

### Ecological Site Planning Checklist

<Tip>When analyzing a site's ecological conditions:

1. Map the watershed and identify drainage patterns
2. Check historical flood records and assess flood risk
3. Document existing wetlands, water bodies, and vegetation
4. Identify any protected or vulnerable species
5. Assess slope stability indicators and landslide risk
6. Review soil conditions and geotechnical constraints
7. Note proximity to active fault lines (PHIVOLCS)
8. Check if site is in an ECA or if project is an ECP (triggers EIA/ECC need)
9. Evaluate air quality and heat island effects
10. Document all constraints and inform preliminary site planning strategy

These inform feasibility; management and design choices come later.</Tip>
