# Le Corbusier

## LEA Point

For LEA history questions, Le Corbusier is most often tested through the Five Points of a New Architecture and Villa Savoye. The safe sequence is: 1927 Five Points, reinforced-concrete frame logic, then Villa Savoye at Poissy as the clearest built exam anchor from 1928-1931.

## Source Anchors

- The Five Points: pilotis, roof garden, free plan, ribbon or banded windows, and free facade.
- Villa Savoye: Poissy, France, Le Corbusier and Pierre Jeanneret, 1928-1931, a manifesto-like house that applies the Five Points.
- Reinforced concrete is the technical enabler for the free plan/free facade relationship because structure can move to columns instead of load-bearing walls.
- Modulor, Unite d'Habitation, Chandigarh, and Ronchamp are important Le Corbusier anchors, but they should not replace Villa Savoye when the question asks for the Five Points.

## Exam Pattern

If the stem says pilotis, roof garden, free plan, ribbon windows, free facade, or Villa Savoye, answer Le Corbusier. If it asks for the year of the Five Points, use 1927. If it asks for Villa Savoye's source-safe date range, use 1928-1931, not the older shorthand that starts at 1929.

## Guardrails

Do not say Le Corbusier personally invented every modern device associated with the International Style. Do not say every Le Corbusier building demonstrates all Five Points. Do not treat Ronchamp as a Five Points diagram; it belongs to a later, more sculptural and spiritual phase.

## Real World Scenario

For a compact urban clinic, a source-safe Corbusian precedent study would use pilotis for ground-level circulation, a free plan for flexible clinical rooms, ribbon windows for daylight, a roof garden for recovery space, and a free facade to separate structure from envelope expression.
