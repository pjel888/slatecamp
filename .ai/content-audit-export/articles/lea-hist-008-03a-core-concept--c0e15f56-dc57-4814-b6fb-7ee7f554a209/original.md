## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal

Identify Le Corbusier's Five Points of Architecture — pilotis, roof garden, free plan, horizontal window, free facade — and explain how each point enabled a new relationship between structure, enclosure, and interior space, exemplified by Villa Savoye.
## Core Concept
**Le Corbusier** (Charles-Édouard Jeanneret, 1887–1965) was the 20th century's most influential architect and theorist. In 1926, he formulated the **Five Points of Architecture**—a set of principles that became the DNA of modernism. These five points directly respond to new structural possibilities (reinforced concrete) and modernist ideology (functionalism, openness, light).

<Tip>
**1926 Five Points = Modernism's formula.** Pilotis, Free Plan, Free Facade, Ribbon Windows, Roof Garden. These five points are not aesthetic preferences—they are logical consequences of concrete construction. Master these five and you understand modern architecture's philosophical and structural foundation.
</Tip>

The **Five Points** are:

**1. Pilotis (Free-Standing Posts/Columns)**
Traditional buildings rest on solid masonry walls. Le Corbusier lifted buildings off the ground using slender reinforced concrete posts, creating an open space beneath. This simple move did three things: it freed the ground plane (permitting gardens, circulation, flexibility), it revealed the structural system (honest expression of how the building stands), and it created visual lightness despite massive walls above. Pilotis are not merely aesthetic; they are a structural and philosophical statement.

**2. Free Plan (Flexible Interior Layout)**
In traditional buildings, load-bearing walls dictate room sizes and positions. With a concrete skeleton supporting the roof, walls become non-structural screens, movable at will. This gave architects complete freedom to organize interior space according to function, not structural constraint. Rooms could be any size and shape. Walls could be added, removed, or repositioned without affecting the building's stability. The free plan revolutionized how humans inhabit space.

**3. Free Facade (Independent Exterior)**
Just as interior walls lost their load-bearing function, so did the exterior facade. With the frame carrying all loads, the facade became an independent skin. It could be glass, curtain wall, or solid material—its appearance need not correspond to interior organization. This allowed pure geometric facades uncompromised by structural necessity.

**4. Ribbon Windows (Continuous Horizontal Glazing)**
Traditional windows are discrete, small openings constrained by structural piers. The free facade enabled continuous horizontal strips of glass running along the entire wall. These ribbon windows flood interiors with light, dissolve the boundary between interior and exterior, and express the horizontal geometry of the modern building.

**5. Roof Garden**
The flat roof, made possible by concrete construction, was no longer dead space. It became an extension of habitable area—a garden, terrace, or recreation space. This reclaimed area that pitched roofs had wasted, responding to density in cities.

These five points were not mere formal games. Each emerged from structural logic and responded to modernist values: honesty of materials, rational planning, light and air, efficient use of urban space. Le Corbusier demonstrated all five in his masterwork, **Villa Savoye** (designed 1928, constructed 1929–1931, Poissy, France), which remains the clearest expression of these principles.

<Note>
Villa Savoye is the canonical example. When an exam asks which building most clearly demonstrates all Five Points, the answer is Villa Savoye. His later work (Notre-Dame du Haut) departs from the systematic doctrine. Know the difference between his theoretical purity and his later sculptural experimentation.
</Note>

Le Corbusier's other major works include the **Unité d'Habitation** (1947–1952, Marseille, France), a massive residential megastructure that applied the Five Points to housing; **Notre-Dame du Haut** (1950–1955, Ronchamp, France), a sculptural chapel that softened his rationalism; and the city of **Chandigarh** (designed 1950s, constructed through 1960s, India), a modernist capital designed from scratch.

<Warn>
Do not assume all Le Corbusier buildings follow the Five Points doctrine strictly. Notre-Dame du Haut is deliberately sculptural and emotionally driven—a departure from the rational Five Points system. His later work shows evolution away from rigid geometric principles toward expressionism. Understand the chronological shift in his philosophy.
</Warn>

## How It's Tested on the LEA

<ExamTip>**Pattern 1: Point Identification & Definition**
You may see: "Which of the Five Points of Architecture refers to the use of structural columns that allow ground-level circulation and open space beneath the building?" Answer: Pilotis. Expect straightforward definition questions.
</ExamTip>
<ExamTip>**Pattern 2: Building Analysis**
You may see an image of Villa Savoye: "Identify the Five Points of Architecture present in this building." Answer: All five (pilotis supporting the volume, free interior plan visible through continuous ribbon windows, free facade independent of the frame, flat roof with garden access). You must be able to visually recognize each point.
</ExamTip>
<ExamTip>**Pattern 3: Point and Implication**
You may see: "The free plan became possible with reinforced concrete construction because [A] walls no longer needed to be load-bearing [B] interior dimensions were reduced [C] zoning laws required open interiors [D] cost of partitions increased." Answer: A. The exam tests your understanding of *why* each point matters structurally and functionally.
</ExamTip>
<ExamTip>**Pattern 4: Architect-Theory Association**
You may see: "Which architect formulated the Five Points of Architecture in 1926?" Answer: Le Corbusier. Direct association questions are common.</ExamTip>

<Callout>Le Corbusier's Five Points of Architecture form the theoretical foundation for modern architecture and are heavily tested on the board exam. You must understand each point, recognize them in buildings, and explain why they represent a break from traditional construction and design.</Callout>

## Key Terms

**Pilotis**: Free-standing columns or posts that lift the main structure above ground, creating open space beneath; one of the Five Points; allows ground circulation and expresses structural honesty.

**Free Plan**: Interior layout determined by functional requirements, not structural walls; enabled by concrete frame construction; allows spatial flexibility and human-centered design.

**Free Facade**: Exterior skin independent of structural frame; can be designed for light, views, and aesthetics rather than structural necessity; allows geometric freedom.

**Ribbon Windows**: Continuous horizontal strips of glazing along the facade; floods interiors with light; dissolves interior-exterior boundary; enabled by free facade.

**Roof Garden**: Flat roof converted into habitable, landscaped space; extends usable area in dense urban contexts; expresses modernist reclamation of wasted space.

**Reinforced Concrete**: Structural material combining concrete's compression strength with steel rebar's tension strength; enables long spans and slender profiles; foundational technology for the Five Points.

**Megastructure**: Large-scale structural frame at urban or multi-building scale; Unité d'Habitation exemplifies this; can house multiple functions within a single massive system.

**Modulor**: Le Corbusier's proportional system based on human measurements; used to ensure all building elements relate to human scale; represents his belief in scientific design.
