# Institutional Human Dimensions

## Learning Goal

Apply human-dimension thinking to institutional spaces without mixing not-checked occupancy claims into the article: identify the user's activity, the psychological/social space requirement, and the accessible BP 344 dimensions that must be checked in institutional public areas.

## Source-Audited Scope

The Salvan design-theory source states that objectives for a satisfactory home environment are not necessarily the same as those for an office or factory, and that different subsystems have different needs. It also describes physical space, personal space, proxemic distances, and human-architectural interfaces that should be considered during conceptualization.

The TOA 3 source defines proxemics as the symbolic and communicative role of spatial separation, and defines personal space as the variable and subjective distance at which a person feels comfortable talking to another person.

The BP 344 2024 source supplies the checked accessible dimensions used here. Older claims about PD 1096 occupancy groups, school airspace, and US IBC terminology are not repeated because those primary code locators were not selected for this Batch 053 article.

## Human-Dimension Checklist for Institutional Design

1. Separate physical space from personal space. Physical space is the environment people see and use; personal space is the felt zone around a person that changes with activity, relationship, and culture.
2. Match distance to the institutional act. A reception conversation, dental appointment, library inquiry, classroom consultation, and waiting-area interaction need different social distances and different furniture/barrier arrangements.
3. Watch barriers. Salvan's doctor example shows that a desk can create distance and formality; removing the desk barrier can make interaction feel more personal and less intimidating.
4. Design for human values, not only minimum dimensions. Salvan explicitly links physical environment, values, offices, restaurants, schools, and homes. Institutional space can support or harm user comfort depending on materials, furnishings, sound, color, and control over place.
5. Check accessible entrances. BP 344 requires accessible entrances from arrival/departure points to the interior lobby and requires vestibules to provide at least 1800 mm by 1800 mm level area.
6. Check doors and routes. Doors require 900 mm minimum clear width. Sidewalks, walkways, and pathways require 1500 mm minimum width; 1800 mm is required where two wheelchair users must pass.
7. Check toilets. Accessible toilet provisions include a 450 mm toilet-seat height, transfer/turning space, reachable accessories, emergency call buttons at 400 mm to 600 mm, and strobe alarms where required.
8. Check elevators and refuge. Accessible elevator cars require 1400 mm depth by 1200 mm width and at least 900 mm clear door opening. A refuge area for one wheelchair user is at least 900 mm by 1200 mm, with reachable emergency communication at 700 mm to 900 mm.
9. Check counters and workstations. Accessible counters/workstations need at least 900 mm width, 700 mm vertical legroom, and 480 mm counter depth.

## Exam Traps

- Do not use a hospital, school, library, or office label as a shortcut for human fit. Identify the activity and the interface.
- Do not import US occupancy terms unless the question explicitly uses a US code source.
- Do not confuse personal distance with a fixed legal dimension. It is context-dependent; BP 344 dimensions are legal accessibility minima for the built interface.
- Do not erase psychological comfort from institutional design. The checked sources tie institutional environments to behavior, values, and comfort.
