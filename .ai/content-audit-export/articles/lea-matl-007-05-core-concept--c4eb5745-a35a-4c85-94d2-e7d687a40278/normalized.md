## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal

Identify the purpose and structure of a finish schedule as a specification document that coordinates floor, wall, and ceiling finishes across all rooms, and create a sample finish schedule entry applying correct material codes, locations, and installation notes.
## Core Concept

A finish schedule is a matrix table in construction documents that lists each room/space, identifies wall, floor, ceiling, and sometimes door and window finishes, and cross-references them to larger detail drawings. This document prevents field interpretation and rework. Three interrelated schedules exist: room finish schedules, door schedules, and window schedules.

Room finish schedules typically organize by room number and list finishes for walls (north, south, east, west), floor, ceiling, base, and trim. Format follows CSI (Construction Specifications Institute) standards, with specification callouts like "2.0.5 Semi-gloss Enamel, Boysen Permacoat, Color: Safety Orange per Pantone 151C."

Door schedules list door type (code: A, B, C, etc.), frame material, width×height, swing direction, hardware group, and finish. Example: "Door Type A: 900×2100mm, wood composite, left-hand swing, Hardware Set 1, finish: pre-finished maple stain per 2.0.8."

Window schedules specify type (casement, sliding, fixed), frame material (aluminum, UPVC, wood), glass type (clear, tinted, tempered), size, and finish. These cross-reference structural sections and ensure consistency across the building.

Philippine practice per RA 9266 (Architecture Act of 2004) and DPWH standards emphasizes clarity and traceability. Finish schedules must reference specification sections (e.g., Section 2.0 "Interior Finishes") and product standards (e.g., ASTM C373 for floor tile). Vague finishes like "paint TBD" or "ceramic tile—to be determined" are construction non-starters and cause delays.

Key information in schedules includes paint color codes (matched to approved color chips or Pantone numbers), floor material and size (e.g., "Mariwasa porcelain tile 600×600mm, Pattern #X-101, grout color: light gray"), ceiling type with acoustic rating if applicable, and baseboard/trim material with height and finish.

## Philippine Construction Context

Philippine architects and engineers follow CSI MasterFormat conventions, aligned with international practice but adapted to local materials. DPWH (Department of Public Works and Highways) standard specification compliance is mandatory for government infrastructure projects. Private construction follows the specifications agreed upon in the contract documents, though DPWH specs often serve as the baseline reference.

Local brands (Boysen, Davies, Mariwasa, Niro Granite) are named explicitly in schedules, with color codes referenced to approved color chips or Pantone numbers. Generic references like "paint—light color" cause rework when 20 similar products exist on-site. Finish schedules in Philippine projects often include a key/legend sheet that displays color samples and material samples, reducing field ambiguity.

For high-rise residential projects (dominant in Metro Manila), finish schedules are critical for unit standardization. Developer contracts typically specify standard finishes for all units (e.g., "All residential units: walls—Boysen Permacoat, Color #B150; floor—Mariwasa porcelain 600×600, Pattern #S-02; ceiling—gypsum + latex paint, White). Custom finishes require a change order.

Accessibility compliance (BP 344) is documented in schedules where applicable. Slip-resistant finishes on ramps and level paths are called out separately. Finish schedules serve as compliance documentation for inspectors.

## How It's Tested

**Question Type 1 (Schedule Interpretation):** A finish schedule shows "Room 201: North Wall—Paint, Boysen Permacoat, Color #B150." What additional information is unclear that might prevent accurate execution? Answer: DFT (dry film thickness), primer type, sheen level (matte, satin, gloss), and surface prep standard should be cross-referenced to the paint specification section. The color code is clear but other details are implicit, risking substitution.

**Question Type 2 (Schedule Creation):** A lobby requires marble veneer walls, porcelain floor tile, suspended ceiling with NRC 0.80, and wood trim. Create a finish schedule entry. Answer: format should list: walls (marble type, size, finish), floor (porcelain tile, size, grout color), ceiling (suspended tile, NRC 0.80, grid type), and base (wood trim, height, stain color), each cross-referenced to specification sections.

**Question Type 3 (Cross-Reference):** A door schedule shows "Door Type A: wood composite, finish—per 2.0.10." What does "per 2.0.10" refer to? Answer: Specification Section 2.0.10, which details door finish standards (primer, topcoat, sanding sequence, hardness test). The schedule abbreviation directs builders to detailed specifications.

**Question Type 4 (Consistency):** A multi-story residential project specifies different paint colors for Units 201 and 301 in the same location. A finish schedule shows Unit 201: Boysen Permacoat #B150, but Unit 301 shows only "light beige paint." What is the issue? Answer: Lack of consistency and product specification prevents bulk purchasing and creates quality variance. All standard units should use identical finishes; deviations require explicit custom-finish callout and cost tracking.
