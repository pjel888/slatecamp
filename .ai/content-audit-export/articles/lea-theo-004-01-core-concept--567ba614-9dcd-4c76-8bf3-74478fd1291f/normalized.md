The Philippine archipelago presents a unique climate challenge for architects: a tropical maritime environment characterized by high temperatures, persistent humidity, and extreme seasonal rainfall variation. Understanding this climate requires three parallel frameworks: geographic position, climate classification, and monsoon dynamics.

**Geographic Context and Latitude**

The Philippines spans from 5°N to 20°N latitude, positioning it squarely within the tropical zone. This near-equatorial location ensures consistent solar radiation and minimal seasonal temperature variation—typically 26–28°C annual average, with May being the hottest month (28.3°C) and January the coolest (25.5°C). However, latitude alone does not determine climate; maritime influence moderates these temperatures while creating relentless humidity. Average relative humidity remains 75–85% year-round, with some regions experiencing 90%+ during monsoon peaks.

**Coronas Classification and Regional Application**

The Modified Coronas Climate Classification, developed by Fr. Coronas in 1920 and refined for Philippine application, sorts the archipelago into four types based on seasonal rainfall distribution. This system is foundational for LEA preparation because it directly predicts which design strategies apply to specific regions:

**Type I (Pronounced Dry Season):** Found in Western Luzon and Western Visayas. Dry season November–April, wet season May–October with peak rainfall June–September. Annual rainfall moderate. Design implication: cross-ventilation can be maximized in dry season; monsoon protection essential May–October.

**Type II (No Dry Season, High Winter Rain):** Eastern Luzon, Eastern Visayas, Eastern Mindanao. No month entirely dry; December–February shows peak rainfall (exceeding 300mm/month in many areas). This perpetual dampness requires year-round moisture control, vapor barriers, and mold-resistant materials. Natural drying is slower; mechanical dehumidification often necessary.

**Type III (Short Dry Season, Interior Plateaus):** Batanes, Western Mindanao, interior highland areas. 1–3 month dry season; relatively dry November–April, wet May–October. These areas often experience orographic effects (windward/leeward variation). Elevation modifies base tropical patterns.

**Type IV (Even Rainfall Distribution):** Central Luzon, Central Visayas, Northeastern Mindanao. Rainfall distributed year-round with no pronounced drought. Consistent high humidity year-round; ventilation strategies must operate continuously.

**Monsoon Dynamics: Habagat and Amihan**

The Southwest Monsoon (habagat) dominates May–October, bringing moisture-laden winds from the Pacific. These westerly flows ascend over mountain ranges, producing heavy orographic rainfall (some regions exceed 5,000mm annually). Wind speeds increase significantly during peak monsoon (July–September); structures must resist sustained 15–25 knot winds with gust factors to 40+ knots during tropical storms.

The Northeast Monsoon (amihan), November–April, reverses flow direction. Originating from mainland Asia, amihan brings drier, cooler air that suppresses humidity to 65–70% in some areas. Temperatures drop slightly (22–24°C possible). Amihan creates the "dry season," though even Type II and Type IV zones receive measurable rainfall during this period.

**Typhoon Belt Exposure**

The Philippines sits within the Pacific Typhoon Belt (10–40°N), with the buckle centered on Northern Luzon. The archipelago experiences average 20 typhoons annually; 5–6 typically reach destructive strength (wind speeds exceeding 150 kph, gusts 200+ kph). These are not short-duration events—a single typhoon may stall over the region for 18–48 hours, producing sustained winds and rainfall exceeding 400mm per day.

The frequency and intensity create a hard constraint: buildings must survive typhoon pressures (external positive/negative) and absorb extraordinary precipitation without structural failure or interior flooding. Ground-level design must account for storm surge and flash flooding in coastal and low-lying areas.

**Thermal and Humidity Characteristics**

The tropical maritime setting suppresses diurnal temperature range. Nights remain warm (22–25°C even in January), preventing the heat-dump effect that makes thermal mass effective in arid climates. Humidity stays high because ocean temperature moderates cooling. This is the critical distinction: the Philippines cannot employ heavy thermal-mass strategies successfully because nighttime temperatures are too high for heat to escape from building mass.

**Design Implications Summary**

Architects must interrogate the Coronas type and monsoon exposure before selecting structural systems, material durability classes, or ventilation strategies. A Type I building can employ permanent openings and rely on dry-season ventilation peaks; a Type II building requires operable enclosures, moisture-resistant materials, and ventilation that operates in perpetually humid conditions. Typhoon exposure mandates structural redundancy, impact-resistant glazing, and aerodynamic forms that shed wind rather than trap it.

## Key Terms

**Coronas Climate Classification** — System sorting the Philippines into four rainfall-pattern zones (Types I–IV), developed by Fr. Coronas in 1920 and used to predict regional design requirements for ventilation, moisture management, and structural resilience.

**Habagat (Southwest Monsoon)** — May–October wind pattern bringing moisture-laden air masses from the Pacific; produces heavy rainfall and strong sustained winds; peak humidity and precipitation.

**Amihan (Northeast Monsoon)** — November–April wind pattern originating from mainland Asia; brings drier, cooler air and forms the "dry season"; creates regional rain-shadow effects on leeward slopes.

**Tropical Maritime Climate** — Oceanic moderating influence (high humidity, warm nights) combined with tropical latitude (high noon solar radiation); minimal seasonal temperature range but persistent high humidity (75–85%).

**Diurnal Temperature Range** — Daily high-to-low temperature difference; suppressed in maritime zones (typically 6°C or less) compared to arid climates (15–20°C). Suppressed range limits thermal-mass effectiveness.

**Typhoon Belt** — Latitudinal zone (10–40°N) where tropical cyclones form; the Pacific Typhoon Belt's buckle centers on Northern Luzon, making the Philippines highly exposed (average 20 typhoons/year).

**Orographic Rainfall** — Enhanced precipitation caused by moisture-laden air ascending over mountain ranges; explains why windward coasts receive 3,000–5,000mm while leeward interior valleys receive 1,000mm.

**Type II Climate (No Dry Season)** — Coronas classification for Eastern Luzon, Eastern Visayas, and Eastern Mindanao; no month with less than 100mm rainfall; peak December–February; requires year-round moisture management.

**Wind-Load Multiplier** — Safety factor applied to basic wind pressure (typically 1.3 kPa for tropical regions) to account for gust effects and sustained typhoon exposure; Philippines typically 1.75–2.0 in high-typhoon zones.
