## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal

Identify the core principles of tropical passive design — prioritize ventilation over thermal mass, shading over orientation, lightweight construction — and explain how these principles respond to the specific climatic challenges of the Philippines.
## Core Concept

Passive design in tropical climates operates on fundamentally different physics than temperate or arid zones. While cold-climate passive design stores heat in thermal mass and hot-arid design alternates between daytime solar gain and nighttime radiative cooling, tropical design must dissipate continuous heat and humidity in an environment where nighttime temperatures remain high (22–25°C) and diurnal range is suppressed (typically 6°C or less).

**Ventilation Hierarchy: The Core Strategy**

In tropical climates, ventilation is the primary cooling mechanism—not shade alone, not mass, not orientation. The hierarchy is:

1. **Cross-ventilation (primary):** Wind-driven flow through opposing apertures creates pressure differential that moves air regardless of indoor temperature gradient.

2. **Stack effect (secondary):** Warm indoor air rises and exhausts through high openings (monitors, roof vents, clerestories); cooler air enters at low level (windows, doors, louvers).

3. **Venturi effect (tertiary):** Wind accelerating around building geometry sucks air through openings on leeward side.

These three work in concert. A well-designed tropical building operates all three simultaneously; failure to integrate any one reduces cooling by 20–30%.

<Tip>The 15-meter rule for cross-ventilation is testable: buildings wider than 15–18 meters (perpendicular to wind) cannot ventilate interiors effectively without courtyards or atria. This is a hard constraint on tropical floor plate design and appears frequently on LEA questions about deep-plan buildings.</Tip>

<Note>Stack effect dominates in early morning (5–9 am) and evening (6–9 pm), not during midday when outdoor temperatures are high. Relying on stack effect alone for cooling during peak occupancy (8 am–6 pm) is a design error. Cross-ventilation is the only mechanism strong enough to provide daytime cooling in perpetually hot Type II/IV climates.</Note>

**Cross-Ventilation Design Principles**

Effective cross-ventilation requires:

- **Opposing apertures:** Windows on at least two perpendicular walls (ideally leeward and windward). Single-sided ventilation achieves only 5–10% of cross-flow effectiveness.

- **Deep plan challenge:** Buildings wider than 15–18 meters struggle to ventilate interior zones; interior courtyards or atria become necessary. The 15m rule is testable on the LEA.

- **Wind speed amplification:** Aperture sizing is counterintuitive. Smaller inlet openings (6–10% of floor area) create velocity; larger outlets (20–30% of floor area) maximize volume. Asymmetric aperture sizing is more effective than equal-sized openings.

- **Approach wind direction:** Prevailing monsoon direction (habagat from southwest May–Oct, amihan from northeast Nov–Apr) determines facade planning. A building must function in both directions or have seasonal operable screens.

- **Obstruction avoidance:** Vegetation, fences, or adjacent buildings within 2–3 building heights of the prevailing wind direction block natural ventilation by 40–60%.

**Stack Effect and Vertical Strategies**

Stack effect drives buoyancy-driven air movement when wind is absent (common during early morning, calm evening hours). Effective stack design requires:

- **Vertical air path:** Open stairwells, atriums, and light wells connecting low to high provide the thermal "chimney." Enclosed corridors block stack flow.

- **Temperature differential activation:** Indoor-outdoor temperature difference (ΔT) of 3–4°C minimum drives stack. In tropics, this is achievable early morning (outdoor 23°C, indoor cooled to 26°C by overnight ventilation) but fails by midday.

- **High exhaust openings:** Clerestories, roof monitors, or vented soffits 3+ meters above occupied zones maximize stack velocity. Skylights alone are insufficient; they must connect to vertical voids.

- **Low inlet openings:** Ground-level windows or louvers feed stack air; the larger the elevation difference (stack height), the greater the velocity.

The bahay kubo achieves stack effect through open floor plans where air rises from ground-level windows and exits via roof vents; no mechanical ducts required.

**Building Orientation in Tropical Context**

In tropical zones, orientation strategy differs from temperate climates. The sun's altitude at noon is nearly perpendicular year-round (70–85° depending on latitude and season), meaning:

- **Vertical surfaces receive less noon radiation** than horizontal roofs. East and west facades are the primary solar heat sources (morning and afternoon low-angle sun).

- **East-West elongation is preferred:** A building stretched east-west presents narrow faces to morning and afternoon sun. North-south elongation maximizes wall exposure to sun. The 2–3:1 length ratio (east-west : north-south) is typical for optimal tropical orientation.

- **Latitude effect:** The Philippines spans 5–20°N. At 15°N latitude, solar noon altitude ranges 48–75°. South-facing facades in Northern Hemisphere benefit from overhang shading; in Philippines, south faces are minor heat sources compared to east-west.

- **Azimuth shading:** East facades require 2–3 meter overhangs or brise-soleil to block 6–10am sun (summer). West facades need 3–4 meter elements to block 3–6pm sun. North facades (lower sun angle) need minimal shading in Philippines context.

**Deep Overhang and Horizontal Shading**

Overhangs are load-bearing design elements, not ornament:

- **Overhang depth calculation:** Required projection = (Wall height / tan(sun angle at critical hour)). For east wall at 8am in Manila (10°N): overhang depth ≈ 3 × wall height. For 4m sill height, overhang minimum 2.5–3m.

- **Overhang thermal benefit:** A 3m overhang reduces east-wall solar gain by 60–70%, which directly reduces interior cooling load. In high-humidity zones, reducing wall surface moisture absorption is equally important.

- **Soffit treatment:** Painted or reflective soffits (light color, high albedo >0.6) reflect residual solar radiation downward and outward, reducing radiation into open spaces below. Dark soffits absorb and re-radiate heat into occupied zones.

- **Ventilated overhang:** Permeable soffits (slatted, open grid) allow hot air to escape upward rather than trapping it. The "Venturi skirt" design—a 30–45 degree louver beneath overhangs—accelerates wind flow and increases cooling.

**Light Shelves and Daylighting Without Heat Gain**

Light shelves redirect high-angle noon sun deep into building interiors while preventing glare and heat gain:

- **Design principle:** A horizontal reflective element (light shelf) at desk height (0.75–1m above sill) intercepts direct sunlight and bounces it to ceiling/upper wall. The ceiling diffuses light throughout the space without concentrated heat at sill level.

- **Effectiveness:** Light shelves reduce window-area heat gain by 30–40% while achieving 2–3× greater interior illumination than unshaded windows of the same area.

- **Material selection:** Specular (mirror-like) aluminum shelves outperform matte finishes; however, diffuse ceilings (not mirrors) must receive the redirected light to prevent secondary glare.

- **Clerestory integration:** Light shelves work best paired with clerestory windows (upper facade) and interior partition glass (allowing light to penetrate to core zones).

**Thermal Mass Debate: Lightweight vs. Heavyweight in Tropics**

This is the critical contested terrain. Arid-climate doctrine advocates thermal mass; tropical performance research shows lightweight construction is superior.

<Warn>This is the LEA's highest-scoring mistake. Students cite "thermal mass reduces cooling load by 6–8°C" (true for arid climates with 15–20°C diurnal range). FALSE in tropics where nighttime temps remain 22–25°C. Concrete heated to 28°C during day will NOT cool below 25°C at night—it re-radiates heat into occupied spaces, degrading comfort. Default to lightweight in Type I–II climates; defend mass use only if nighttime temps provably drop below 20°C.</Warn>

**Lightweight case (supported by research):**
- High nighttime temperatures (22–25°C) prevent thermal mass from radiating stored heat. Concrete or masonry mass heated to 28°C by day will not cool below 25°C at night in a Type II climate.
- Lightweight timber or metal framing responds quickly to cooled air from night ventilation or early-morning stack effect; interior temperature drops 2–3°C faster than heavyweight buildings.
- Studies in Caribbean and Southeast Asian climates show lightweight houses are 2–3°C cooler throughout the day during occupied hours (8am–6pm) versus identical heavyweight designs.
- Nighttime cooling techniques (operable windows, night ventilation fans) reduce peak daytime cooling loads 35.9% more effectively in lightweight structures.

**Heavyweight case (situational):**
- In Type I zones with pronounced dry season and significant diurnal range (temperature dropping to 20–22°C at night), thermal mass can absorb daytime heat and discharge it during cooler nights.
- Mass moderates fluctuation in buildings with intermittent occupancy (schools, offices closed nights).

**LEA consensus:** Lightweight construction (timber frame, metal stud, bamboo, hollow clay tile) is the default tropical strategy. Heavyweight mass is justified only in Type III/Type IV regions with measurable dry seasons AND if nighttime temperatures reliably drop below 20°C.

**Courtyard Cooling and Atrium Strategy**

Courtyards create passive cooling through:

- **Evaporative cooling:** Courtyard water features (fountains, pools, wet surfaces) increase relative humidity locally and reduce air temperature by 1–2°C through evaporation.

- **Stack ventilation:** Atria open to sky and vented at roofline create powerful stack effect. Hot air rising through atrium vents cooler air in perimeter zones.

- **Thermal buffering:** Courtyard acts as thermal break between opposing facades; interior-facing walls remain cooler than exterior.

- **Night cooling:** Open courtyards allow nighttime radiation and convection to cool interior thermal mass (if mass is used) or to pre-cool incoming ventilation air.

- **Limitation:** Courtyards increase wall area (higher exterior surface = higher solar load) and can trap stlea air if poorly oriented relative to prevailing wind.

**Roof Design and Insulation**

Roofs receive 5–6× the solar radiation of vertical walls at tropical latitudes. Strategy:

- **Reflective surfaces (high albedo > 0.7):** White or light-colored finishes reduce roof surface temperature from 60–65°C (dark) to 35–40°C (light), reducing heat conduction into interior by 40%.

- **Ventilated roofs:** Air space between structural deck and finish roofing (minimum 150–200mm) allows convective cooling. Soffit vents (low) and ridge vents (high) create stack flow.

- **Roof insulation effectiveness:** In tropics, insulation prevents heat *ingress*, not heat loss (unlike cold climates). Fiberglass, mineral wool, or cork boards (50–75mm) reduce interior heat gain by 25–30%.

- **Polyurethane spray foam:** Higher R-value per inch; however, releases CFCs if not eco-formulated. BERDE-compliant projects must specify zero-ODP foams.

**Vernacular Exemplar: The Bahay Kubo**

The traditional Philippine bahay kubo embodies passive tropical design:

- **Elevation on stilts (1–2m):** Allows air to circulate beneath structure, cooling the floor and providing flood resilience. Eliminates ground moisture absorption.

- **Wide overhangs (2–3m):** Shade walls and allow wind penetration beneath eave level while protecting openings from rain.

- **Open floor plan:** No interior partitions; entire interior is one thermal zone. Wind penetrates unobstructed.

- **Bamboo slat flooring:** Permeable; allows stack effect (air rising through floor gaps) and prevents moisture trapping. Thermal conductivity is low (unlike concrete or tile).

- **Ventanillas (operable louvers):** Located high on walls; opened for stack ventilation, closed to exclude rain and wind. Adjustable shading.

- **Large windows on all sides:** Minimum 20–25% of wall area as openings; oriented to prevailing wind.

- **Natural materials:** Bamboo, wood, thatch (historically). Low embodied carbon; responsive to moisture.

The bahay kubo survives typhoons (flexible framing absorbs wind load), remains naturally cool without air conditioning (passive ventilation), and adapts seasonally (operable openings for habagat/amihan variation). Modern neo-vernacular architecture (Archdaily Philippines examples) replicates these principles in contemporary materials: metal stud frames, permeable wood/aluminum screens, raised platforms, light roofing.

<Tip>When analyzing vernacular models on LEA, extract the *principle*, not the material. "Use bamboo" is not a principle; "provide permeable floor surfaces enabling air circulation beneath structure" is. This distinction allows you to apply vernacular logic to contemporary materials (bamboo slat floor → perforated metal floor → hollow-tile ventilated floor).</Tip>

<Note>The bahay kubo's operable ventanillas are not decorative or optional—they are the primary cooling control in a building with few mechanical systems. Closing ventanillas during strong monsoons protects from wind/rain while maintaining some stack-effect ventilation. This dual functionality (protection + ventilation) is lost in modern buildings with fixed selead windows; operable coverage is essential in Philippine design.</Note>

## How It's Tested on the LEA

<ExamTip>1. **Cross-Ventilation Sizing:** "An office building plan is 18m (N-S) × 25m (E-W) in Cebu (Type IV climate). Prevailing wind: southwest (habagat, May–Oct). Specify aperture sizing for cross-ventilation and justify your inlet/outlet ratio."
   - **Answer:** E-W width exceeds 15m effective ventilation depth. Solution: interior courtyard or atrium (6m×6m) to create two 9.5m zones on each side. SW-facing facades: inlet 6% floor area (1.5m² for 25m²), NE-facing outlet 25% (6.25m²). Asymmetry creates velocity.</ExamTip>

<ExamTip>2. **Thermal Mass Rejection:** "A developer proposes thick concrete walls in an Eastern Visayas project (Type II, no dry season, nighttime temp 24°C). Critique this choice and propose an alternative."
   - **Answer:** Type II climate prevents nighttime thermal discharge (insufficient ΔT between wall [25°C] and ambient [24°C]). Concrete mass will trap daytime heat and re-radiate at night, degrading comfort. Alternative: 150mm lightweight block + 50mm EPS insulation; open floor plan with 20% window area + operable louvers for continuous ventilation. Stack effect via atrium for core cooling.</ExamTip>

<ExamTip>3. **Overhang Design Calculation:** "A south-facing facade in Manila (latitude 14°N) has a 4m tall wall. Design an overhang for the 8am winter-sun position (altitude 35°). Show calculation and final projection depth."
   - **Answer:** Overhang depth = Wall height / tan(altitude) = 4m / tan(35°) ≈ 4m / 0.7 ≈ 5.7m. However, this is winter minimum; at 8am summer (altitude ~60°), same depth overhangs 1.4m shadow. A compromise 3.5–4m overhang is typical, accepting some summer morning sun in exchange for winter protection.</ExamTip>

<ExamTip>4. **Bahay Kubo Retrofit:** "A heritage bahay kubo (open floor, slat floors, large overhangs) must accommodate typhoon-resistant sliding doors and insect screens (required for upgraded residential standard). Identify three design moves that preserve passive ventilation."
   - **Answer:** (a) Specify aluminum-frame sliding doors with ventilation louvers; when closed, louvers maintain 15% aperture area for stack flow. (b) Replace traditional fabric insect screens with perforated metal (aluminum 50% open area); reduces airflow resistance ~20% vs. fine mesh. (c) Maintain elevated slat floor by raising door sills 300–500mm, preserving under-structure ventilation.</ExamTip>

<ExamTip>5. **Light Shelf Integration:** "An administrative office in Quezon City (Type IV) has south-facing windows (1m sill height, 1.8m tall). Design a light shelf strategy including material, reflectance, and integration with ceiling."
   - **Answer:** Specular aluminum light shelf at 1.5m height (0.5m above sill). Reflectance minimum 0.8 (mirror finish). Redirect sunlight to suspended acoustic ceiling (white, diffuse, albedo >0.6) located 0.3m above shelf. Result: 30–40% reduction in sill-level heat while achieving 300–500 lux interior illumination at 3–4m depth.</ExamTip>

<Callout>Tropical passive design prioritizes ventilation over thermal mass, shading over orientation, and lightweight construction over heavyweight enclosure. The bahay kubo exemplifies all these principles: elevated on stilts, open floor plans with cross-ventilation, large overhangs, permeable bamboo flooring, and ventanillas (operable louvers) for stack effect. Effective design harnesses wind, sun path, and material thermal properties without mechanical air conditioning.</Callout>

## Key Terms

**Cross-Ventilation** — Wind-driven flow through opposing apertures; primary cooling mechanism in tropical climates, requiring asymmetric inlet/outlet sizing for maximum velocity.

**Stack Effect** — Buoyancy-driven vertical air movement caused by temperature differential (minimum 3–4°C) between interior and exterior; operates most effectively early morning and evening in tropics.

**Venturi Effect** — Wind acceleration around building geometry that reduces pressure on leeward side, drawing air through downwind openings; contributes 10–20% to total ventilation in well-designed forms.

**Light Shelf** — Horizontal reflective element (typically 0.75–1m above sill) that intercepts and redirects high-altitude sun to ceiling for diffuse daylighting without direct solar gain.

**Thermal Mass** — Heat-absorbing building material (concrete, masonry, water); ineffective in tropical climates where nighttime temperatures remain high (suppress radiative cooling); lightweight construction preferred.

**Diurnal Range** — Daily temperature swing (high-to-low); suppressed in maritime tropics (6°C or less), preventing thermal-mass cooling strategy that depends on 15–20°C range.

**Albedo** — Solar reflectance of surface (0–1 scale); white/light surfaces (albedo 0.7+) reduce roof/wall surface temperatures 25–30°C compared to dark finishes.

**Ventanilla** — Traditional Philippine operable louvre window, located high on walls; adjustable for stack ventilation, rain protection, and wind control; key bahay kubo feature.

**Overhang Projection Depth** — Horizontal extension of roof beyond wall face; calculated via sun-angle geometry to block low-altitude morning/afternoon sun while allowing high-altitude noon penetration (winter benefit in temperate zones; less critical in tropics).

**Bahay Kubo** — Traditional Philippine elevated wooden dwelling exemplifying passive tropical design: stilts, wide overhangs, open floor plan, permeable flooring, large windows, operable louvers, natural materials.

**Embodied Carbon** — CO₂ released during material extraction, manufacturing, transport, and installation; relevant to LEA because BERDE penalizes excess insulation and favors low-impact tropical materials (bamboo, wood, lightweight block).
