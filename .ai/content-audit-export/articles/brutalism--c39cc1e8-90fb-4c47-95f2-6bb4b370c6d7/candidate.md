# Brutalism

## Board Exam Quick Check

Identify Brutalism through mass, exposed reinforced concrete, and rough surface character. The checked sources link the term to Le Corbusier's later raw-concrete direction and to massive modern architecture with rough concrete surfaces left exposed.

## Source-Grounded Core

The local HOA reviewer defines Brutalism as inspired by the raw concrete used by Le Corbusier in his later buildings. It describes the style as massive modern architecture built of reinforced concrete, with rough and abrasive concrete surfaces left exposed.

A broader history source gives the same diagnostic language from another angle: Brutalism is associated with blank concrete surfaces and with Le Corbusier's rough-textured sculptural concrete. It also notes heavy walls, exaggerated roofs or parapets, slit or porthole-like windows, and fortress-like solidity in some 1970s office buildings.

## LEA Visual Anchors

- Rough exposed concrete is the strongest board-review clue.
- Massive forms matter more than applied decoration.
- Paul Rudolph's Art and Architecture Building at Yale appears in the local review source as the sample Brutalist anchor.
- Le Corbusier's later work is relevant because the source connects Brutalism to raw concrete in his later buildings.

## LEA Answer Pattern

Choose Brutalism when the prompt emphasizes raw or rough exposed concrete, weight, massive reinforced-concrete form, and sculptural or fortress-like presence. Reject answers that focus on glassy steel minimalism, historical ornament, or smooth white International Style abstraction.

## Common Exam Traps

- Not every concrete building is Brutalist. The source-backed cues are exposed rough concrete, mass, and strong plastic form.
- Do not confuse Brutalism with Miesian steel-and-glass minimalism. Mies is tied to refined steel and glass; Brutalism is tied to concrete mass and texture.
- Do not classify Brutalism by ornament. The board clue is material and form, not decorative reference.
