# Ludwig Mies van der Rohe

## LEA Point

Mies van der Rohe is tested through disciplined reduction: "less is more," universal space, precise proportions, and steel/glass/stone detail. The exam anchors are the Barcelona Pavilion, Farnsworth House, and Seagram Building.

## Source Anchors

- Barcelona Pavilion: designed by Mies van der Rohe and Lilly Reich for the 1929 Barcelona International Exposition; built from glass, steel, and fine stones; disassembled in 1930 and reconstructed on the original site in 1986.
- Farnsworth House: designed by Mies in 1945 and constructed in 1950-1951; a clear-span glass house raised in relation to the Fox River floodplain.
- Seagram Building: Mies van der Rohe with Philip Johnson, 1956-1958/1958, a Park Avenue office tower sheathed in glass and bronze with a disciplined modular curtain wall.
- The safe vocabulary is less-is-more, universal space, open plan, steel frame, glass enclosure, proportion, and refined material detail.

## Exam Pattern

If the stem says Barcelona Pavilion, free-flowing planes, glass/steel/stone, or 1929 German Pavilion, answer Mies. If it says glass house, Fox River, raised steel frame, or Farnsworth, answer Mies. If it says Seagram, bronze-and-glass curtain wall, Park Avenue plaza, or corporate International Style, answer Mies with Philip Johnson.

## Guardrails

Do not confuse Mies with Brutalism; his high-yield language is steel, glass, proportion, and reduction, not rough massive concrete. Do not say Seagram's bronze exterior I-beams are the actual load-bearing frame; use them as source-safe expression of rhythm and detail on a curtain-wall system. Do not date Farnsworth only as 1946-1951 when the source-safe wording is designed 1945, constructed 1950-1951.

## Real World Scenario

For a small gallery pavilion, a source-safe Miesian approach would use a clear structural bay, minimal partitions, large glass planes, carefully chosen stone or metal, and proportioned joints so the space feels open without relying on applied ornament.
