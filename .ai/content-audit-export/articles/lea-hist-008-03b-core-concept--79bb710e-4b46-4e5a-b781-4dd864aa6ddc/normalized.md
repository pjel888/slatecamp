## Learning Goal

Identify Mies van der Rohe's architectural philosophy — 'less is more,' universal space, honest expression of materials — and recognize his major works (Barcelona Pavilion, Farnsworth House, Seagram Building) by their structural clarity and minimalist precision.
## Core Concept
**Ludwig Mies van der Rohe** (1886–1969) stands alongside Le Corbusier as one of the two most influential modernists. But where Le Corbusier pursued functionalism and complexity, Mies pursued reduction and essence. His axiom—"**less is more**"—became the philosophy of minimalism.

Mies believed that architecture's job was to create pure space, divested of ornament, symbolism, and unnecessary form. This wasn't poverty or severity; it was refinement through elimination. Every element had to justify its presence through structural honesty, perfect proportion, and material beauty. Steel, glass, and concrete were not merely functional; they were material essences to be celebrated. A steel column is *just* a steel column—unadorned, perfect, beautiful because of what it is.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Note" status="preserved"]

The **Barcelona Pavilion** (1929, demolished 1930, reconstructed 1986, Barcelona, Spain) is his masterpiece and the clearest expression of this philosophy. The pavilion has no walls in the traditional sense—only free-standing steel columns supporting a flat roof, with glass and stone planes floating between them. There is no hierarchy, no clear distinction between inside and outside. The structure is exposed, elegant, the proportions exquisite. There are no ornaments, no applied decoration, yet the building is profoundly beautiful. This beauty emerges from pure form, material, and proportion.

The **Farnsworth House** (1949–1951, Plano, Illinois, USA) radicalizes this approach further. The house is a glass box elevated on steel columns. The walls are entirely transparent; the interior is an open plan with no fixed divisions except a central service core. The structural system—eight steel columns, a steel frame, and glass walls—is completely honest; nothing masks the structural logic. The proportions are refined to geometric perfection. The result is simultaneously austere and luxurious: a dwelling reduced to essentials, yet visually stunning.

The **Seagram Building** (1954–1958, **Mies van der Rohe** and **Philip Johnson**, New York) brought Mies's minimalism to the corporate skyscraper. The facade is a gridded curtain wall of bronze and glass; the structural columns are expressed as vertical bronze I-beams, visible on the facade. The plaza before the building is open, unencumbered—space itself is the primary design element. Mies famously said he wanted to create "universal space," a neutrally beautiful container that could accommodate any activity without imposing a predetermined program.

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0004 component="Warn" status="preserved"]

Mies's design method was the opposite of Le Corbusier's programmatic rationalism. He didn't list functions and design accordingly; he stripped away all program to reveal the essence of building itself. His buildings are meditations on space, structure, and material. For this reason, they are often perceived as cold or austere by those unfamiliar with his work. In fact, they are deeply humanistic—they trust that pure spatial and material beauty will move people.

## Key Terms

**Less Is More**: Mies van der Rohe's foundational principle that maximum spatial and material beauty emerges from minimum, essential elements; rejection of ornament and excess in favor of refined reduction.

**Universal Space**: Mies's concept of a neutral, geometrically perfect spatial container that can accommodate any activity without imposing programmatic constraints; priority over functional specificity.

**Structural Honesty**: Design principle that exposes the building's structural system; columns, beams, and connections are visible, celebrated, unadorned; structural logic and aesthetic form are unified.

**Curtain Wall**: A non-load-bearing facade of glass and aluminum/steel that hangs from the building's structural frame; allows complete design freedom for the exterior skin independent of structure.

**I-Beam**: An I-shaped steel structural member used in Miesian architecture; symbolizes honest expression of structural logic through visible, refined metalwork.

**Material Essence**: Design approach that celebrates the intrinsic properties of materials (steel's strength, glass's transparency, concrete's mass) without applied ornament or disguise.

**Transparency**: Architectural concept using glass to dissolve boundaries between interior and exterior; creates visual continuity and democratic access to space.

**Minimalism**: Design philosophy emphasizing essential elements, geometric purity, reduction to abstraction; Mies is a foundational figure in architectural minimalism.
