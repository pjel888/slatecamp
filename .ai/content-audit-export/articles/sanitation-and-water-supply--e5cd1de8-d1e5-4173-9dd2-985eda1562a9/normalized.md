## The Real-World Hook

The building official stands at the entrance of a newly completed four-storey commercial building in Quezon City, certificate of completion in hand, logbook signed, as-built plans submitted. By all accounts, the structural engineer is satisfied, the electrical permit has been cleared, and the fire marshal's inspection is scheduled for the following week. The owner is already fielding inquiries from tenants. There is just one problem: the building has no wastewater disposal system. The plumbing drains terminate in a holding pit with no connection to the city sewer main, no septic tank, and no absorption field. The storm drainage pipes discharge directly into the sanitary drainage line. And the ground floor restaurant space has hollow-block walls with no rat-proofing.

The building official has no choice. Under PD 1096, Rule IX, Section 901, every building erected for human habitation must be provided with — among other things — adequate and potable water supply, a suitable wastewater treatment or disposal system, storm water drainage, and pest and vermin control. These are not optional features. They are pre-conditions for occupancy. Without them, no Certificate of Occupancy can be issued under Rule III, Section 309. And if the building is occupied without a CO, the building official can initiate dangerous building proceedings under Rule II, Section 215.

The sanitation requirements of Rule IX are among the most frequently overlooked by architects who are focused on design aesthetics or structural compliance. But they are also among the most consequential: failures in water supply, wastewater, or storm drainage are the proximate causes of outbreaks of cholera, typhoid, and leptospirosis — all endemic to Philippine urban informal settlements. Understanding Rule IX is not bureaucratic box-ticking. It is a matter of public health survival in a country of over 100 million people living in a tropical, seismically active, typhoon-prone archipelago.

## Core Concepts

### Principle 1 — Section 901 Is a Pre-Occupancy Checklist, Not a Wish List

Section 901 of PD 1096 / 2004 Revised IRR states that all buildings erected, altered, remodeled, relocated, or repaired for human habitation shall be provided with the following:

1. **Adequate and potable water supply**
2. **Plumbing installation** (governed by the Revised National Plumbing Code of the Philippines)
3. **Suitable wastewater treatment or disposal system**
4. **Storm water drainage system**
5. **Pest and vermin control**
6. **Noise abatement device** (sound insulation requirements)
7. **Other measures for health protection**

The operative word is "shall." There is no discretionary language, no exemption for small buildings or low-cost housing, and no "where practicable" qualifier. The seven requirements of Section 901 are mandatory for every building intended for human habitation, regardless of size, cost, or location.

**The critical connection:** Section 309 of Rule III requires submission of a Certificate of Completion plus as-built plans plus a Building Inspection Sheet before the Building Official may issue a Certificate of Occupancy. The final inspection undertaken by the Office of the Building Official specifically verifies compliance with all requirements of the Code, including Rule IX. A building that fails any element of Section 901 will fail the CO inspection.

**Scope of "human habitation":** Section 901 applies not only to new construction but also to alterations, remodeling, relocation, and repair. This means that a landlord who subdivides an existing building into rental units, or who converts a warehouse into a dormitory, triggers full Rule IX compliance for the modified structure — including the installation of appropriate water supply, wastewater, storm drainage, and pest control systems appropriate to the new use.

### Principle 2 — Water Supply System: Source Hierarchy and Quality Standards (Sec. 902)

Section 902 establishes a clear hierarchy for potable water sources and subjects all sources to enforceable quality standards:

**Source Priority:**
1. **Municipal/city waterworks system** — the preferred and primary source. When a municipal or city waterworks connection is available, buildings shall connect to it. This is not a preference; the code prioritizes public water systems because they are regulated and regularly tested.
2. **Meteoric water (rainwater harvesting)** — permitted as a supplemental or secondary source in areas without municipal supply, subject to quality standards.
3. **Surface water** (rivers, lakes) — permitted subject to National Standards for Drinking Water under the Philippine Clean Water Act (RA 9275).
4. **Underground sources (deepwells)** — governed by the Water Code of the Philippines (PD 1067). Design, construction, and operation of deepwells must comply with PD 1067 requirements, including registration and water rights appropriation.

**Water Quality Standard:** All drinking water from non-municipal sources must meet the **National Standards for Drinking Water**, which implement WHO guidelines in the Philippine context. These standards govern physical, chemical, and microbiological parameters.

**Plumbing Connection:** All water piping installations within the building must comply with the **Revised National Plumbing Code of the Philippines (RNPCP)**. The RNPCP is the sister code to PD 1096 for plumbing systems — it governs pipe sizing, fixture unit calculations, pressure requirements, cross-connection control, and materials.

**Independent Waterwork Systems:** Private subdivisions and industrial estates that operate their own waterworks systems are governed by existing waterworks laws, not just PD 1096. This reflects the reality of many gated communities and economic zones in the Philippines that operate private water utilities.

**Design Implication:** For the LEA candidate, the critical awareness is that **water supply is not merely a plumbing detail** — it is a site selection and regulatory due diligence question. Before a building is designed, the architect must confirm whether municipal water service is available at the site, what the service pressure is, and whether the building's demand (particularly for mid-rise and high-rise buildings requiring booster pump systems) can be met by the available supply.

### Principle 3 — Wastewater Disposal: Sewer First, Septic Second (Sec. 903)

Section 903 establishes the same priority hierarchy for wastewater disposal as Section 902 does for water supply — public system first, private treatment second.

**Priority 1 — Municipal/City Sanitary Sewer:**
Sanitary sewage must be discharged to the nearest street sanitary sewer main when one is available, in accordance with the **Code on Sanitation (PD 856)** and **DENR criteria**. Connection to the public sewer is mandatory if the sewer main is within reach. Building officials verify sewer connection during CO inspection.

**Priority 2 — On-Site Treatment:**
Where no public sewer system is available, wastewater must be treated by one of the following methods:
- **Imhoff tank** — a two-compartment sedimentation-digestion tank, typically used for small communities
- **Septic tank with subsurface absorption field** — the most common private wastewater solution for Philippine residential and commercial buildings not served by public sewers
- **Suitable treatment plant** — any other treatment system meeting DENR effluent standards

The specification of the Imhoff tank and septic tank reflects the reality of Philippine sanitation infrastructure: as of 2020, the Philippine Statistics Authority estimated that less than 15% of the Philippine population is connected to a wastewater treatment facility. The vast majority of buildings rely on septic tanks — which is why the Plumbing Code's septic tank sizing and construction standards are critically important.

**All plumbing installations** — both for sanitary sewage and industrial wastewater — must comply with the **Revised National Plumbing Code**.

**Industrial Wastewater:** Section 903 explicitly covers industrial plumbing. Factories and industrial buildings must treat effluent to DENR standards before discharge, even when connected to a public sewer system. This creates a pre-treatment obligation for industrial occupancies that residential buildings do not share.

### Principle 4 — Storm Drainage Must Be Separate From Sanitary Sewer (Sec. 904)

Section 904 contains one of the most important negative prohibitions in Rule IX:

> Rainwater drainage shall **NOT** discharge to the sanitary sewer system.

This prohibition against **combined sewers** (mixing storm and sanitary drainage) is one of the most universally violated provisions in Philippine construction practice, and one of the most dangerous. When storm water enters a sanitary sewer, it causes two cascading problems:
1. **Hydraulic overload:** storm events can generate 10–100× the flow of normal sanitary sewage, overwhelming treatment plants and causing untreated sewage to bypass treatment (combined sewer overflow — CSO).
2. **Contamination:** storm water carries surface pollutants — oils, heavy metals, pesticides, and pathogens — into the sewage stream, complicating treatment.

**Positive Requirements of Section 904:**
- Buildings must make **adequate provision to drain rainwater from low areas** — particularly basement levels, light wells, and depressed courtyards.
- All storm drainage pipe installations must comply with the **Revised National Plumbing Code**.

**Design Implication — Philippine Context:**

In Metro Manila and other flood-prone Philippine urban areas, storm drainage design is a matter of life safety. Typhoons regularly deliver 100–300 mm of rainfall in 24 hours. A building that discharges its storm water into the sanitary sewer — or worse, that has inadequate storm drainage and allows ponding in basements — creates conditions for electrocution, leptospirosis (water-borne in flood conditions), and structural undermining. The prohibition in Sec. 904 is not merely about sewage treatment — it is about preventing buildings from becoming flood traps.

### Principle 5 — Pest and Vermin Control: A Mandatory Architectural Provision (Sec. 905)

Section 905 imposes three specific pest control requirements:

**Requirement 1 — Rat-Proofing:**
All buildings with **hollow and/or wood construction** shall be provided with rat-proofing. This requirement is particularly relevant in the Philippine context where:
- Concrete hollow blocks (CHB) are the most common wall material in residential construction
- CHB walls, when improperly constructed, leave open joints and cavities that become rat highways
- Rat-proofing typically means continuous mortar fill at wall bases, rodent-proof door seals, and mesh screens over openings

Rat-proofing is not optional for these construction types — it is a mandatory provision triggered by the building's construction system.

**Requirement 2 — Garbage Enclosures:**
Garbage bins and enclosures must be provided with **positive protection against pests**. "Positive protection" means physical barriers — tight-fitting lids, sealed containers, or enclosed garbage rooms — not just regular collection schedules. This requirement connects directly to the building's waste management design, including the location and design of garbage rooms in multi-family and commercial buildings.

**Requirement 3 — Screened Dining Areas:**
Dining rooms for **public use** (restaurants, cafeterias, canteens) that are **without artificial ventilation** shall be properly screened. This means fly screens on all openings facing outside — windows, doors, and ventilation openings. The public dining context triggers this requirement because food preparation and consumption in unscreened, naturally ventilated rooms in a tropical climate creates conditions for fly-borne contamination.

**Intersection with Dangerous Buildings (Sec. 215):**
Section 215 of Rule II lists "inadequate sanitation/plumbing" as one of the six categories of defects that render a building dangerous or ruinous. Persistent pest infestation — particularly rats in food preparation or storage areas — falls within this category. A building official can initiate abatement proceedings against a building with demonstrated pest infestation resulting from non-compliance with Sec. 905. This gives pest control requirements teeth beyond the CO stage.

### Principle 6 — Noise Pollution Control and Pipe Materials (Secs. 906 and 907)

**Section 906 — Noise Pollution Control:**

Buildings and structures shall be provided with **adequate noise abatement devices**. The code establishes the requirement without specifying numerical noise levels — those are governed by the **Presidential Decree 984 (Pollution Control Law)** and **DENR Administrative Orders** on ambient noise standards.

In architectural practice, noise abatement for buildings includes:
- **Sound insulation** in walls, floors, and ceilings (mass-law compliance, decoupling, absorption)
- **Mechanical equipment isolation** — vibration isolators for HVAC, pumps, and generators
- **Building siting and orientation** relative to noise sources (traffic, industrial, airports)
- **Buffer zones and landscaping** as supplemental noise barriers

Noise abatement is especially important for:
- **Mixed-use buildings** where residential units are above commercial or industrial tenants
- **Buildings near NAIA, Clark, or Davao International Airport** (aircraft noise)
- **Buildings adjacent to EDSA or other major arterials** (traffic noise)

The Metric Handbook (Littlefield) provides recommended noise criteria (NC) ratings by room type — for example, NC 25-35 for bedrooms, NC 35-45 for offices, NC 45-55 for restaurants — which serve as design targets even where the Philippine code does not specify numerical limits.

**Section 907 — Pipe Materials:**

All pipe materials shall conform to the **standard specifications of the Bureau of Product Standards (BPS) of the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI)**. This provision connects building code compliance to product certification — meaning that a plumber who installs non-BPS-certified pipes in a building is creating a code violation, not merely a quality concern.

BPS certification covers:
- PVC pipes (water supply, drainage)
- uPVC pipes (drain, waste, vent)
- GI pipes (water supply, fire suppression)
- CPVC and PPR pipes (hot water systems)
- Cast iron pipes (heavy drainage)

**For the LEA candidate:** Section 907 is rarely a standalone exam question, but it frequently appears as a rationale element in questions about material specifications. When a question asks why a particular pipe material must be used in a building, the answer chain is: BPS certification (Sec. 907) → Revised National Plumbing Code → PD 1096, Rule IX.

## PH vs. USA Comparative Analysis

| Dimension | Philippines (PD 1096 / 2004 IRR, PD 856) | USA (IBC 2021 / IPC 2021 / Federal Standards) | Key Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Water Supply — Governing Code** | PD 1096 Sec. 902 + Revised National Plumbing Code of the Philippines | IBC Chapter 29 (references IPC); EPA Safe Drinking Water Act | PH: NBC sets mandate; RNPCP sets technical requirements. USA: IBC mandates; IPC sets details; EPA sets water quality nationally |
| **Water Source Priority** | Municipal waterworks first; then surface/underground sources | Same priority — public water supply preferred; private wells where no public supply | Same principle; both codes prefer connection to public regulated supply |
| **Drinking Water Quality Standard** | National Standards for Drinking Water (PH) — WHO-aligned | EPA National Primary Drinking Water Regulations (NPDWRs) | Both are WHO-aligned; EPA standards are more detailed and have legal enforcement via the Safe Drinking Water Act |
| **Deepwell / Private Well Regulation** | PD 1067 (Water Code of the Philippines) — requires water rights appropriation | State-level regulation varies; EPA Underground Injection Control (UIC) for deep injection | PH requires national-level water rights; US regulation is primarily at state level with federal overlay for injection wells |
| **Wastewater — Primary Disposal** | Discharge to nearest sanitary sewer main (per Code on Sanitation, PD 856, and DENR criteria) | IPC and local sewer authority — connection to public sewer mandatory when available | Same principle; PH cites Code on Sanitation and DENR; USA cites IPC and local sewer ordinance |
| **Wastewater — Where No Public Sewer** | Imhoff tank, OR septic tank + absorption field, OR treatment plant | IRC R905 / IPC 701: septic system (on-site sewage facility) per state health department standards; USEPA guidelines | Same technologies; US relies on state health departments for design standards; PH relies on Plumbing Code and DENR |
| **Septic Tank Standards** | Revised National Plumbing Code + Code on Sanitation (PD 856) | State-specific regulations (no single national standard); USEPA guidance documents | USA: no single federal septic standard — highly fragmented by state. PH: nationally unified under RNPCP |
| **Storm Water — Separation** | Rainwater shall NOT discharge to sanitary sewer (Sec. 904) | IBC 1503 / IPC Chapter 11: storm drainage separate from sanitary drainage; same prohibition | Same principle — both codes require separate storm and sanitary systems |
| **Storm Water — Quality** | PD 1096 Sec. 904 focuses on drainage only; water quality governed by RA 9275 (Clean Water Act) | Clean Water Act Section 402 (NPDES) — stormwater discharge permits required for construction sites > 1 acre | USA has a more developed stormwater quality regulatory framework (NPDES permits). PH RA 9275 is the equivalent but less developed in practice |
| **Pest Control — Rat-Proofing** | Mandatory for all hollow/wood construction (Sec. 905) | IBC 1503.4 (no explicit rat-proofing mandate); local health codes govern; IPC pipe sleeves at penetrations prevent pest entry | PH explicitly mandates rat-proofing based on construction type. USA relies more on building envelope continuity and local health codes |
| **Pest Control — Screened Dining Rooms** | Required for public dining rooms without artificial ventilation (Sec. 905) | No explicit IBC provision; FDA Food Code governs food service sanitation including screening | PH integrates food safety screening into the building code. USA uses FDA Food Code as a separate regulatory instrument |
| **Noise Abatement** | Buildings shall have adequate noise abatement devices (Sec. 906) — no numerical limits in PD 1096 | IBC 1207 (Sound Transmission): STC ratings for party walls/floors in residential buildings (STC 50 for walls, STC 50 for floors in hotels and multi-family) | USA IBC specifies minimum STC ratings for specific assemblies. PH NBC requires noise abatement generally without specifying STC ratings — more prescriptive in USA |
| **Noise — Mechanical Equipment** | Sec. 906 general requirement | ASHRAE noise criteria (NC ratings) by occupancy type; local ordinances specify dB limits | USA has more developed professional standards (ASHRAE NC ratings); PH relies on DENR ambient noise standards |
| **Pipe Materials** | Must conform to BPS/DTI standard specifications (Sec. 907) | IPC Table 702.1 (drainage) and Table 605.3 (supply) — specific ASTM, AWWA, NSF/ANSI standards | USA specifies exact ASTM/NSF standards by material. PH references BPS certification — same function but administered differently |
| **Plumbing Code Reference** | Revised National Plumbing Code of the Philippines (RNPCP) | International Plumbing Code (IPC) 2021 or Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) depending on jurisdiction | PH has one national plumbing code; USA uses either IPC or UPC — code adoption varies by state |
| **CO Prerequisite** | All Sec. 901-907 requirements verified before CO issued per Rule III, Sec. 309 | IBC Sec. 111: CO issued after final inspection confirming all code compliance including plumbing | Same mechanism — both require plumbing and sanitation compliance before CO |
| **Dangerous Buildings** | Inadequate sanitation/plumbing = 1 of 6 dangerous building categories (Rule II, Sec. 215) | IPMC Sec. 108 (Unsafe Structures); Sec. 602 (plumbing deficiencies) — code official may declare unsafe | Same concept; PH uses PD 1096 Sec. 215; USA uses IPMC as a separate property maintenance code |
| **Primary Philippine Health Standards** | PD 856 (Code on Sanitation), RA 9275 (Clean Water Act), DENR criteria | EPA Safe Drinking Water Act, Clean Water Act, FDA Food Code | USA standards are more disaggregated across agencies; PH consolidates under fewer instruments with DENR as lead |

**Key Takeaway for LEA:** The Philippine approach to sanitation in PD 1096 is intentionally holistic — Sections 901-907 treat water supply, wastewater, storm drainage, pests, and noise as an integrated package of health protection requirements. The USA achieves similar outcomes but through a more disaggregated system (IBC + IPC + EPA + FDA Food Code + ASHRAE). For board exam purposes, remember that PH Rule IX is comprehensive and that each section name maps to a specific health threat.

## Cross-Referencing

### Connection to Rule III, Sec. 309 — Certificate of Occupancy

This is the most critical cross-reference in Rule IX. Section 309 of Rule III requires that a building pass final inspection before the Building Official issues a Certificate of Occupancy. The final inspection specifically checks compliance with all applicable provisions of PD 1096, including Rule IX. A building with any of the following will fail CO inspection:

- No connection to municipal water or a compliant private water source
- No wastewater disposal system (sewer connection, septic tank, or treatment plant)
- Storm drainage connected to sanitary sewer
- Hollow or wood construction without rat-proofing
- Public restaurant without screens and without mechanical ventilation
- Pipe materials not conforming to BPS/DTI standards

The Sec. 309 CO process also involves the **City/Municipal Fire Marshal** issuing a Fire Safety Inspection Certificate. While fire safety is governed by RA 9514 (Fire Code), the integration of fire and sanitation inspections into a single CO process reflects the code's intent that all health-and-safety systems be verified together before occupancy.

**Partial CO:** Section 309 also allows the Building Official to issue a **Partial Certificate of Occupancy** for portions of a building that are complete and compliant while other portions are still under construction. This is relevant for sanitation: the portions receiving Partial CO must have fully functional water supply, wastewater, and storm drainage systems serving that portion, not just a promise that systems will be connected when the rest of the building is complete.

### Connection to Rule II, Sec. 215 — Dangerous and Ruinous Buildings

Section 215 of Rule II lists **five categories of inadequate sanitation/plumbing** as grounds for declaring a building dangerous or ruinous. While the research data notes this as one of the six major categories without listing all sub-items, the connection is clear: a building that was once compliant can be declared dangerous after the fact if its sanitation systems fail, are removed, or are never properly installed. This means Rule IX compliance is not a one-time CO checkpoint — it is a continuing obligation of building ownership.

The abatement procedure under Section 215 and Rule II gives the Building Official the authority to order repair, vacation, or demolition depending on the degree of danger. For sanitation failures, the typical response is an order to install or repair the deficient system within a specified time period, with escalating penalties for non-compliance leading ultimately to a vacation or demolition order.

### Connection to Rule VII, Sec. 708 — Group A Dwellings

Section 708(c) of Rule VII explicitly requires that every Group A dwelling be provided with "at least one sanitary toilet and adequate washing and drainage facilities." This is a cross-reference trigger that links the occupancy classification requirements of Rule VII directly to the sanitation provisions of Rule IX. A single-family dwelling that meets all architectural requirements but lacks sanitary toilet facilities fails Section 708(c) and therefore Rule IX, Section 901.

### Connection to Rule VIII, Sec. 811 — Artificial Ventilation

Section 905(3) (public dining rooms without artificial ventilation must be screened) creates a design decision point: the designer must either specify mechanical ventilation for the dining room (triggering Rule VIII, Sec. 811 compliance for the mechanical system) or specify screens on all openings (triggering Rule IX, Sec. 905 compliance for pest control). These two rules work in parallel — satisfying one does not automatically satisfy the other because their objectives differ (Sec. 811 governs air quality; Sec. 905 governs pest exclusion).

### Connection to PD 856 — Code on Sanitation of the Philippines

PD 1096 repeatedly cross-references PD 856 for detailed sanitation standards. While PD 1096 sets the mandate that buildings shall have appropriate sanitation systems, PD 856 specifies the technical standards for those systems — including latrine standards, food establishment sanitation, housing sanitation standards, and institutional sanitation. For LEA purposes, PD 856 is a sister law to PD 1096, not a subordinate regulation.

### Connection to PD 1067 — Water Code of the Philippines

Section 902(3) specifically cites PD 1067 for the design, construction, and operation of deepwells. PD 1067 governs water rights appropriation in the Philippines — meaning that a building owner who sinks a deepwell without appropriate water rights under PD 1067 is in violation of both PD 1067 and PD 1096. For LEA candidates: the intersection of water rights law and building code is a distinctly Philippine issue that rarely appears in comparable form in US practice.

### Connection to RA 9275 — Philippine Clean Water Act

The storm drainage prohibition of Section 904 — that rainwater shall not discharge to the sanitary sewer — is reinforced by RA 9275 (Philippine Clean Water Act), which prohibits the discharge of pollutants to water bodies without treatment. Buildings near rivers, estuaries, or coastal areas that discharge untreated storm water into these bodies may trigger RA 9275 liability in addition to PD 1096 violations.

### Connection to RA 9514 — Fire Code and Building Systems

Noise abatement under Section 906 intersects with fire protection system design under RA 9514. Fire alarm systems generate noise levels (typically 75 dB at 10 feet per NFPA 72); emergency generator rooms require both noise insulation and adequate ventilation. The architect must balance Sec. 906 noise abatement requirements against the life-safety requirements of the fire code when designing these building systems.

## Mnemonic Devices

### Mnemonic 1 — "WARDEN" for Section 901 Sanitation Requirements

**W-A-R-D-E-N:**

- **W** → **Water supply** — potable, adequate (Sec. 902)
- **A** → **Abatement of pests** — rat-proofing, screens, garbage protection (Sec. 905)
- **R** → **Rainwater drainage** — storm water system, separate from sanitary sewer (Sec. 904)
- **D** → **Disposal of wastewater** — sewer, septic tank, or treatment plant (Sec. 903)
- **E** → **Equipment pipe materials** — must conform to BPS/DTI standards (Sec. 907)
- **N** → **Noise abatement** — sound insulation devices (Sec. 906)

The **WARDEN** mnemonic turns the six-component sanitation package into a single memorable word — fitting for a set of requirements designed to "guard" occupant health.

### Mnemonic 2 — "SS≠" for the Storm Drainage Prohibition

**Storm Sewer ≠ Sanitary Sewer**

For the Section 904 prohibition, remember: **"SS≠"** — Storm System does NOT equal Sanitary System. They are parallel, separate, never-mixing infrastructure systems. When in doubt: if it comes from the sky, it goes to the storm drain. If it comes from a fixture, it goes to the sanitary drain. Never cross the streams.

Expanded version for exam memory:
- **Sky water → Storm drain** (rain, roof runoff, area drains)
- **Sink water → Sanitary drain** (grey water from fixtures, black water from WCs)
- **Never the two shall meet** (Sec. 904 prohibition)

## Exam Trap Learts

**TRAP 1:**
> "Where no public sewer system is available, any private wastewater disposal method acceptable to the owner may be used, as long as waste is contained on the property."

**TRUTH:** **False.** Section 903(2) specifies three acceptable alternatives when no public sewer is available: (a) Imhoff tank, (b) septic tank with subsurface absorption field, or (c) suitable treatment plant. The selection and design must comply with the **Code on Sanitation (PD 856)** and **DENR criteria** — not merely the owner's preference. An unlined collection pit or a cesspool that does not meet these standards is a code violation, and the building official may refuse to issue a Certificate of Occupancy under Rule III, Sec. 309.

---

**TRAP 2:**
> "A newly constructed warehouse with hollow concrete block walls does not need rat-proofing because it is not intended for human habitation."

**TRUTH:** **Partially false — the logic is flawed.** Section 905(1) requires rat-proofing for **all buildings with hollow and/or wood construction** — the scope is not limited to "habitable" buildings. Moreover, if the warehouse contains food storage, connects to a food processing facility, or is later converted to residential use, the rat-proofing requirement becomes even more critical. The LEA exam may test the specific trigger: it is the **construction type** (hollow or wood) that mandates rat-proofing, not the occupancy classification. Furthermore, a warehouse storing food products is also subject to PD 856 (Code on Sanitation) requirements independent of PD 1096.

---

**TRAP 3:**
> "Since the building's plumbing contractor is licensed and reputable, pipe material compliance with BPS standards can be assumed and does not need to be separately verified during the CO inspection."

**TRUTH:** **False.** Section 907 requires that all pipe materials **conform to BPS/DTI standard specifications** — this is an objective, verifiable requirement, not an assumption based on contractor reputation. During the CO inspection under Rule III, Sec. 309, the Building Official (or the Inspector of Record) must verify that installed pipe materials carry appropriate BPS certification marks or are accompanied by product certification documentation. The architect of record bears professional responsibility for specifying BPS-compliant materials in the contract documents, and the contractor bears responsibility for installing only certified materials. Neither assumption nor reputation substitutes for material certification.

## Exam Tip

LEA Trap: Septic tank minimum = 2 chambers (1/3 and 2/3 of volume). Capacity = 0.2 m³ per bedroom minimum. Leaching field trench = 600 mm width minimum, 1.5 m minimum from property line. Well-to-septic separation = 15 m minimum (horizontal). The 15 m separation is the most tested distance.
