# The International Style

## LEA Point

The International Style is the exam label for codified early modern architecture, especially after MoMA's 1932 Modern Architecture exhibition and the Hitchcock/Johnson framing. The safest principles are volume over mass, regularity, and avoidance of applied ornament.

## Source Anchors

- MoMA anchors Modern Architecture: International Exhibition to 1932 and its exhibition/catalogue context.
- MoMA's International Style collection anchors the term to works such as Villa Savoye, Tugendhat House, Lever House, and the UN Secretariat facade.
- Local HOA/theory anchors test the style through glass curtain walls, steel or reinforced-concrete frames, open planning, flat roofs, structural clarity, and rejection of applied ornament.
- Canonical comparison works include Villa Savoye, Barcelona Pavilion, Lever House, Seagram Building, Farnsworth House, and Mies/Le Corbusier examples.

## Exam Pattern

If the stem says MoMA 1932, Hitchcock and Johnson, glass curtain wall, volume instead of mass, regularity, flat roof, open plan, and no applied ornament, answer International Style. If the stem contrasts Art Deco, choose the option that removes decorative luxury and emphasizes structural clarity.

## Guardrails

Do not say every International Style building is identical or perfectly climate-neutral. Do not confuse the style with Brutalism's raw concrete mass. Do not treat Art Deco geometric ornament as the same thing as International Style regularity.

## Real World Scenario

For a tropical office tower review, an International Style precedent is useful for frame-and-curtain-wall recognition, but the source-safe critique asks whether the universal glass box has been adapted for sun, heat, and local use.
