## Learning Goal

Identify the key markers of the International Style — glass curtain walls, structural frame expressed, rejection of ornament, flat roofs, asymmetrical composition — as codified by Hitchcock and Johnson (1932), and recognize its global dissemination through works like the Seagram Building.
## Core Concept
The **International Style** emerged from the 1932 Museum of Modern Art (MoMA) exhibition curated by **Henry Russell Hitchcock** and **Philip Johnson**. This movement codified early modernism's aesthetic principles: form follows function absolutely, ornament is eliminated entirely, and the beauty of a building lies in its proportions and material honesty rather than decoration.

The style's name reflects its ambition: it claimed to transcend national and regional preferences in favor of universal design principles grounded in industrial rationality. Load-bearing walls disappear in favor of steel or concrete skeleton frames with glass curtain walls stretched across them. Roofs flatten. Interior spaces flow openly, unobstructed by ornamental screens or applied decoration. The building becomes a **volume** defined by its essential form, not its mass.

Glass curtain walls constitute the movement's visual signature. Rather than treating the facade as a solid boundary, the International Style uses large expanses of glazing to express the frame within and dematerialize the external envelope. This transparency announces modern technology and efficiency. The frame itself—whether steel or concrete—becomes the ornament through its own logical expression.

The style rejected both 19th-century eclecticism and Art Deco's decorative impulses. Architects believed that functional design, rigorously pursued, would naturally produce beautiful buildings. This doctrine freed them from the burden of "styling" and demanded instead total integration of structure, materials, program, and form. Every element earned its presence through necessity.

The International Style dominated institutional and corporate architecture from the 1930s through the 1970s, reshaping downtowns and campuses worldwide. Its principles traveled easily because they claimed universality—a glass box with a steel frame looked equally legitimate in New York, São Paulo, or Tokyo. This globalization made it both the most influential and most critiqued modernist movement.

## Key Terms
- **Form follows function**: The principle that a building's shape should derive purely from its intended use, rejecting ornament as unnecessary.
- **Glass curtain wall**: A non-load-bearing exterior skin of glass and metal framing stretched across a structural skeleton.
- **Pilotis**: Columns or posts that elevate a building above ground level, freeing the floor beneath and creating an open plaza or garden.
- **Ribbon window**: A continuous horizontal band of glazing running across a facade, expressing the slab structure within.
- **Structural frame**: A steel or reinforced-concrete skeleton that carries all load, allowing freedom in facade design.
- **Dematerialization**: The visual effect of making a building appear weightless through transparency and minimal supports, negating the sense of mass.
- **Open plan**: Interior spaces flowing without fixed partitions, allowing flexible use and visual continuity.
- **Proportional grid**: A system of proportional modules controlling facade fenestration and structural bays.
- **Béton brut**: Raw concrete left exposed after formwork removal; used differently in Brutalism but pioneered in modernism.
- **Functionalism**: The doctrine that design should serve practical purpose above aesthetic tradition.
