# Inca Architecture Ashlar Masonry

## Source-Audited Scope

Batch 054 limits this topic to the checked South American and Inca evidence in George Salvan's local raw source family. The source supports distinctions between coastal adobe and highland stone, as well as smooth ashlar, polygonal masonry, precise fitting, Machu Picchu, the Gate of the Sun, and Sacsahuaman.

## Board Exam Quick Check

If an LEA question asks about Inca masonry, look for highland stonework, smooth ashlar, polygonal stones, bevelled or cushion-like faces, precise fitting, little external decoration, and strong site integration.

## Core Concepts

- Coastal work is described as adobe, while highland work uses rubble and dressed stone.
- Public buildings and fortifications used dressed stone in forms that include smooth ashlar and polygonal masonry of large irregular stones.
- Some stones have bevelled edges and cushion-like dressed faces.
- The checked source emphasizes precision in stone fitting and notes little rich external decoration in highland buildings.
- Machu Picchu is described as a late Inca town built of local stone with walling from coursed ashlar to roughly dressed rubble, niches, terraces, and stairs.
- The Gate of the Sun at Tiahuanaco is tied to decoration cut into a great andesite lintel.
- Sacsahuaman is described with terraces and high retaining walls of Yucay limestone, monoliths, polygonal stones, and precise fitting.

## LEA Use

Use ashlar, polygonal masonry, precision fitting, and site integration as the high-yield identifiers. If the question contrasts coast and highland, choose adobe for the coast and rubble or dressed stone for the highlands.

## Common Traps

- Do not state that the checked source proves mortar-bonded Inca ashlar for this article.
- Do not confuse Mesoamerican temple pyramids with Inca highland masonry.
- Do not treat every South American work as Inca; the checked source also names Tiahuanaco.
