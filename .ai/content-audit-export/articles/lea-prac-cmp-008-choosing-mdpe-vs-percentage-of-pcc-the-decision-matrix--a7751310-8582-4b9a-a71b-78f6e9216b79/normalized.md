## Choosing MDPE vs. Percentage of PCC — The Decision Matrix

## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- Compare MDPE and percentage-of-PCC as compensation methods.
- Describe when to use each method based on project characteristics.
- Use the decision matrix to select the appropriate fee method.
- Apply method selection criteria to exam scenarios.

---
### When Percentage-of-PCC Is Most Appropriate

Percentage-of-PCC works best when Project Construction Cost can be estimated reliably and the design scope aligns logically with construction cost. Traditional Design-Bid-Build projects with well-defined building types fit this pattern: standard office buildings, conventional apartment complexes, or routine institutional buildings where construction cost reasonably reflects design effort.

Projects with predictable scope, experienced clients accustomed to percentage-based fees, and conventional delivery methods benefit from percentage-of-PCC's simplicity and transparency. The method aligns fee with client value (the larger the building, the higher the fee), which many clients find intuitive. For projects with stable scope and reliable cost estimates established early, percentage-of-PCC provides simplicity and reduces administrative burden.

Percentage-of-PCC is also appropriate when the architectural value proposition connects directly to construction cost. A luxury residential or high-end commercial project where design sophistication drives construction cost justifies percentage-of-PCC because cost reflects design intent and complexity.

### When MDPE Is Most Appropriate

MDPE is preferable when Project Construction Cost is difficult to estimate reliably, when design complexity disproportionately exceeds construction cost, or when scope is uncertain. Programming-intensive projects where the architect's design work defines the program rather than responding to a preset program often justify MDPE. Master-planning projects where the architect defines land use, phasing, and infrastructure may have construction cost that underrepresents the architect's design contribution.

Projects with significant innovation, research, or development value justify MDPE because construction cost doesn't reflect the intellectual and developmental effort required. A cutting-edge research facility, a pioneering sustainable building, or a building incorporating advanced technological systems may have construction costs that don't justify percentage-based fees relative to design effort.

Renovation and historic preservation projects often justify MDPE because existing conditions create unpredictable scope and cost. Similarly, projects with phased or uncertain delivery, projects with substantial pre-design phases, and projects requiring extensive client coordination and redesign cycles may produce cost estimates that inadequately reflect architectural effort.

### Decision Matrix: Key Questions

To select between compensation methods, answer the following questions:

1. Can PCC be estimated within ±15% accuracy? If yes, percentage-of-PCC is viable. If uncertain or contingent on design development, MDPE is safer.

2. Does design complexity align with construction cost? If the building's construction cost reasonably reflects design effort, percentage-of-PCC works. If design complexity far exceeds construction cost (a small but highly sophisticated facility), MDPE is more appropriate.

3. Is project scope clearly defined? If yes, percentage-of-PCC provides simplicity. If scope is uncertain or client will refine scope during design, MDPE's hourly basis provides flexibility.

4. What is the delivery method? Design-Bid-Build projects suit percentage-of-PCC. Design-Build, CM at Risk, or projects with early contractor involvement may benefit from MDPE's flexibility.

5. Is there significant pre-design or planning work? If yes, MDPE accounts for this work fairly. Percentage-of-PCC may undervalue planning work not directly reflected in construction cost.

6. Does the project involve innovation or research? Yes indicates MDPE. No indicates percentage-of-PCC is appropriate.

7. Will the client likely approve scope changes or refinements? Yes indicates MDPE's flexibility is valuable. No indicates percentage-of-PCC's certainty is preferable.

### Quantitative Comparison Method

For projects where both methods are viable, calculate fees using both approaches and compare:

1. Estimate DPE based on anticipated project hours and team composition.
2. Apply appropriate MDPE multiplier (2.0–3.2 depending on complexity).
3. Calculate resulting MDPE fee.
4. Estimate PCC based on cost data or preliminary estimates.
5. Apply appropriate percentage-of-PCC (based on project group).
6. Calculate resulting percentage-of-PCC fee.
7. Compare results. Which method produces a fee that feels appropriate for the project?

Example: A modest renovation project might have DPE of 500,000 pesos with MDPE multiplier of 2.3 (resulting in 1.15 million peso fee). The estimated PCC is 15 million pesos. At 8% of PCC (Group 4 equivalent), the percentage-based fee would be 1.2 million pesos. Results are similar, suggesting either method is viable. The architect might choose MDPE because scope uncertainty makes hourly billing more appropriate, or choose percentage-of-PCC for client preference and simplicity.

### Hybrid Approaches

Some projects justify hybrid approaches combining elements of both methods. A base percentage-of-PCC fee with MDPE adjustment for pre-design phases recognizes that planning work precedes construction cost definition. A retainer covering estimated initial phases plus percentage-of-PCC for remaining design balances advance compensation with completion-based payments.

Design-Build projects sometimes use MDPE for design phases and a reduced percentage on construction cost, recognizing that contractor involvement during design reduces the architect's independent design effort and cost risk.

### Client Communication and Negotiation

When proposing either method, explain why it suits the project. For percentage-of-PCC: "This project's straightforward scope allows confident cost estimation, and a percentage-of-PCC fee aligns your investment with your facility's value." For MDPE: "This project's complex scope and uncertain program justify hourly-based fees to ensure fair compensation regardless of how construction costs ultimately develop."

Clients often have preferences based on prior experience. Some prefer percentage-of-PCC's cost certainty; others prefer MDPE's flexibility. The architect should be prepared to justify the chosen method and to negotiate if the client strongly prefers an alternative.

### Documentation in the Contract

The Owner-Architect Agreement must clearly specify which method applies, how it will be calculated, payment milestones, and contingency provisions. For percentage-of-PCC, the contract must specify what PCC amount will be used (Design Development estimate, final bid, or lowest responsive bid?). For MDPE, the contract must specify whether fees are estimated and capped or actual and variable, how hours will be tracked, and what hourly rates apply.
