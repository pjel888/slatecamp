### Solar Geometry Fundamentals

**Solar geometry** describes the apparent motion of the sun across the sky throughout the year, defined by **altitude** (angle above the horizon) and **azimuth** (compass direction). In architecture, these parameters determine when direct solar radiation enters a building—critical for thermal comfort in hot climates.

The **altitude angle** varies with season and latitude. At the equator (0°), the sun passes directly overhead twice yearly, creating extreme midday glare. At higher latitudes, the sun traces a lower arc. The Philippines, spanning 5°N (Mindanao) to 20°N (Batanes), experiences significant seasonal variation but never extreme low-angle winter sun like temperate zones.

The **azimuth angle** indicates the sun's compass direction: 0° = north, 90° = east, 180° = south, 270° = west. In the Philippines' low latitude, the sun rises slightly north of due east in winter and slightly south of due east in summer—a relatively narrow range compared to higher latitudes.

### Philippine Latitude and Solar Characteristics

For **Manila (14.6°N latitude)**:
- **Summer Solstice (June 21)**: Solar altitude at noon = 79.5°; sun rises ~62° from east, sets ~298° from north
- **Equinox (March/September 21)**: Solar altitude at noon = 69°; sun rises due east, sets due west
- **Winter Solstice (December 21)**: Solar altitude at noon = 59.5°; sun rises ~118° (SE), sets ~242° (SW)

These angles are substantially higher than temperate latitudes. At 40°N (New York), winter noon altitude ≈ 26°; at 14.6°N (Manila), winter noon altitude ≈ 59.5° (approximately 2.3 times higher). [Verify against NOAA Solar Position Calculator or peer-reviewed source for exact Manila values.] **This means Philippine buildings never receive true low-angle winter heat gain; heating is unnecessary, but cooling becomes year-round demand.**

For **Batanes (20°N latitude)**:
- Summer altitude = 73°; winter altitude = 53.5°
- Greater seasonal range than Manila but still fundamentally tropical

For **Davao (6.9°S to 8.8°N range)**:
- Near-equatorial sun angles; nearly vertical summer sun
- Minimal seasonal variation, but year-round intense radiation

### Building Orientation Strategy

The **optimal orientation** for tropical buildings minimizes east and west facade exposure while maximizes controlled north-south exposure. This principle contradicts temperate-zone guidance (south-facing for winter solar gain) and instead prioritizes summer cooling.

#### East-West Elongation

Elongating buildings on the **east-west axis** reduces the building's east and west exposures—the most problematic orientations in tropical climates. East-facing facades receive intense early morning sun (approximately 6:00–9:00 AM), while west-facing facades absorb afternoon heat (approximately 3:00–6:00 PM) when outdoor temperatures peak. [Specific times and altitudes should be verified using NOAA Solar Position Calculator or solar geometry software for Manila's latitude (14.6°N).] A building elongated east-west presents minimal east-west wall area and maximum north-south wall area.

**Energy Performance**: Buildings elongated east-west with appropriate shading reduce cooling loads by 20–30% compared to random orientations. In Coronas Type I climates (pronounced dry season, May–October), this reduction is especially significant during peak heat months.

#### North-South Exposure Benefits

North-facing facades in the Philippine tropics receive indirect, diffuse radiation. South-facing facades receive more direct sun but at high altitude angles (even in winter, >53°). At Manila's winter solstice (59.5° altitude), deep horizontal overhangs become impractical (>3 m); horizontal louvers angled 60–70° from horizontal are more effective for controlling high-altitude solar penetration. Neither requires extensive mechanical cooling beyond natural ventilation if properly shaded.

#### Deviation from True North

The **optimal deviation** from cardinal direction depends on latitude and climate zone:
- **Coronas Type I climates** (dry season 6 months): Align 10–15° east of south to capitalize on afternoon wind patterns during hot season
- **Coronas Type II/IV climates** (year-round rainfall): Align closer to true cardinal (0–5° deviation) to balance monsoon wind patterns
- **Latitude >17°N** (northern Philippines): Can accept 10–20° deviation without significant solar performance penalty

#### Case Study: Philippine Regional Adaptation

- **Luzon, Western (Coronas Type I)**: East-west elongation, 15° east deviation captures SW monsoon winds and minimizes afternoon heat
- **Visayas, Eastern (Coronas Type II)**: Cardinal orientation with deeper north-south shading; year-round rain requires consistent shadow control
- **Mindanao, Southern (Coronas IV)**: Flexible orientation; moisture distribution negates seasonal advantage

### Solar Path Diagrams and Design Analysis

A **solar path diagram** plots the sun's trajectory across the sky for a given latitude, with altitude (vertical axis) and azimuth (horizontal axis). Architects overlay building facade orientations, window openings, and shading device positions to identify solar penetration periods.

For **Manila (14.6°N)**:
- North facade: Direct sun only during equinox sunrise/sunset (minimal penetration)
- East facade: Direct sun 6:00 AM–9:00 AM year-round; winter deeper penetration
- South facade: Direct sun 3:00 PM–6:00 PM year-round; always high-angle (>53°)
- West facade: Direct sun 12:00 PM–6:00 PM year-round; most problematic for heat gain

**Design Response**:
- North windows can be large and unshaded (minimal solar heat gain)
- East windows require 1.5–2.0 meter deep overhangs to shade morning sun
- South windows require 0.8–1.2 meter deep overhangs (high-angle sun already reduces penetration depth)
- West windows require aggressive shading: 2.0–2.5 meter overhangs, exterior louvers, or vertical fins

### Shading Depth Calculation

The **required overhang depth** is calculated as:

**OverOverhang Depth (m) = Window Height × tan(solar altitude angle)

At **Manila, south facade, solar noon during winter solstice** (solar altitude ≈ 59.5°):
- Window height = 2.0 m
- Overhang depth = 2.0 × tan(59.5°) ≈ 2.0 × 1.664 ≈ **3.3 m required**

[Note: This calculation assumes the sun is directly perpendicular to the south-facing facade. Actual altitude angle varies with time of day and azimuth angle.] × 0.700 = **1.05 m minimum**

At **Manila, south facade, 2:00 PM during winter** (solar altitude ≈ 58°):
- Window height = 2.0 m
- Overhang depth = 2.0 × tan(58°) = 2.0 × 1.600 = **3.2 m required**

Wait—this reveals a critical Philippine reality: even at noon on the winter solstice, the sun altitude is so high (>59°) that a massive overhang becomes impractical. Instead, **horizontal louvers angled 60–70° are more effective** for south-facing windows.

### Integration with Philippine Climate Classification

The **Coronas climate system** (Philippine climate classification) determines how seasonal solar patterns interact with rainfall and wind. [Cite specific source: e.g., PAGASA climate classification or peer-reviewed reference.]

- **Type I (Western Luzon, Western Visayas)**: Dry Nov–Apr, wet May–Oct. Building orientation can accept greater east-west deviation to capture SW monsoon breezes during wet season. Solar shading is critical during dry season peak heat (Apr–May).

- **Type II (Eastern regions)**: No dry season, max rainfall Dec–Feb. Year-round cloud cover reduces solar intensity during peak cold months; summer sun remains intense. Shading devices must balance solar control with daylighting for perpetually cloudy months.

- **Type III & IV (Interior/distributed rainfall)**: Less pronounced seasonality. Building orientation prioritizes wind patterns over seasonal solar variation. Shading adjusts for year-round solar exposure.

### Natural Ventilation and Orientation Synergy

Building orientation influences cross-ventilation effectiveness through **wind exposure**. In Coronas Type I climates with pronounced SW monsoon winds (May–Sept), orienting the building with long facade perpendicular to monsoon flow maximizes ventilation. However, this may conflict with optimal solar orientation.

**Compromise solution**: Orientate primary facade (maximum window area, occupancy) toward prevailing monsoon; secondary facades toward solar-optimal direction. Mechanical ventilation assists during low-wind periods (dry season, calm afternoons).

## Key Terms

**Solar Altitude** — Angle of the sun above the horizon (0° = horizon, 90° = zenith). Determines overhang depth required to block direct radiation.

**Solar Azimuth** — Compass direction of the sun (0° = north, 180° = south). Determines which facade receives direct radiation at any time.

**Solstice** — Moment of extreme solar declination; summer solstice (~June 21) shows highest noon altitude; winter solstice (~Dec 21) shows lowest.

**Equinox** — Moment of zero solar declination; spring (~March 21) and autumn (~Sept 21) equinoxes show sun rising due east and setting due west.

**East-West Elongation** — Building footprint maximizes north-south facade length, minimizes east-west length; reduces combined east-west heat gain.

**Optimal Orientation Deviation** — Angular deviation from true cardinal direction (0°) to optimize both solar control and natural ventilation for a specific Coronas climate type.

**Overhang (Brise-Soleil)** — Fixed horizontal projection above a window; depth calculated via altitude angle and tangent function.

**Solar Path Diagram** — Two-dimensional graph plotting sun trajectory (altitude vs. azimuth) for a given latitude; used to analyze facade solar exposure.

**Direct Radiation** — Solar energy arriving in straight lines from the sun disk; high-altitude tropical sun creates shallow penetration angles.

**Diffuse Radiation** — Solar energy scattered by atmosphere and clouds; increases during wet monsoon season (Coronas Type II/IV).

**Coronas Climate Type Integration** — Orientation strategy modified for Type I (dry-season peak), Type II (year-round wet), or Type III/IV (moderate seasonality).
