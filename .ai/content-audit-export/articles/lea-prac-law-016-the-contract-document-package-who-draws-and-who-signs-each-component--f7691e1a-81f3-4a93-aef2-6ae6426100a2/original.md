## The Contract Document Package — Who Draws and Who Signs Each Component

## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- Identify all documents in the contract document package: drawings, specifications, bidding documents, contract forms.
- Distinguish who draws (architect/professional) vs. who signs (owner, architect, contractor).
- Explain the hierarchy of contract documents in case of conflict.
- Describe the architect's role in assembling and certifying the contract document package.

---
### The Complete Contract Document Package Structure

The contract documents package for construction comprises multiple integrated components: special conditions, general conditions, drawings, specifications, bill of materials, and other bid documents. Each component serves distinct functions in communicating design intent, establishing contractual obligations, defining construction requirements, and allocating risk between the owner, architect, contractor, and consultants. The documents are meant to be read together as an integrated whole, with each component informed by and referring to others.

Architectural drawings communicate the design through two-dimensional and three-dimensional representations. Structural drawings depict the building's framing system and load-bearing elements. Mechanical drawings show heating, cooling, and ventilation systems. Electrical drawings depict power distribution, lighting, and communications infrastructure. Sanitary or plumbing drawings show water supply and waste systems. Each specialist prepares drawings within their professional scope, and each must be signed and sealed by the appropriate licensed professional responsible for their accuracy and compliance with codes.

### Authorship and Preparation of Drawings

The architect prepares architectural drawings showing the building's configuration, layout, spatial relationships, and aesthetic design. These drawings become the foundation upon which specialists base their designs for structural, mechanical, electrical, and sanitary systems. The architect is responsible for communicating the overall design concept, building organization, circulation patterns, and visual intent through the architectural drawings. The architect's signature and seal on these drawings certify that the designs conform to applicable codes and that the building as designed can be safely constructed.

The structural engineer prepares structural and foundation drawings showing the building's support system, member sizes, connections, and load paths. These drawings must be coordinated with the architect's spatial design to ensure structural elements fit within proposed spaces and do not conflict with the architectural vision. The structural engineer's stamp on these drawings certifies structural adequacy and code compliance. Similarly, mechanical engineers prepare HVAC drawings, electrical engineers prepare electrical system drawings, and sanitary engineers or master plumbers prepare plumbing drawings, each responsible for accuracy within their professional discipline.

### The Signature and Seal Requirement

RA 9266 Section 23 provides that only a Registered Licensed Architect may sign and seal architectural drawings. Only a Registered Licensed Structural or Civil Engineer may sign and seal structural and foundation drawings. Only a Registered Licensed Mechanical Engineer may sign and seal mechanical system drawings. Only a Registered Licensed Electrical Engineer may sign and seal electrical system drawings. Only a Registered Licensed Sanitary Engineer or Master Plumber may sign and seal sanitary and plumbing drawings.

The signature and seal certify that the professional who applied them is responsible for the document's accuracy, code compliance, and professional adequacy. The signature constitutes a declaration that the professional has personally reviewed the document, verified its completeness and accuracy, and is willing to assume professional liability for its contents. The seal is a form of identification and certification that a licensed professional has reviewed and approved the work. The professional whose name and license number appear on the seal bears direct responsibility for the document's contents.

### Direct Supervision Requirement

A critical provision of RA 9266 Section 23 requires that architects may only sign and seal documents that they personally prepared or that were prepared under their direct supervision. Direct supervision means the architect was physically present, directly involved in the design process, and actively oversaw the work as it was being developed. Direct supervision does not mean merely receiving copies of completed documents for review or signing documents prepared by others months or years after their completion.

An architect who signs and seals documents prepared by junior staff without being directly involved in their development, or who signs documents prepared by another firm, violates this requirement and faces professional discipline. The architect's responsibility extends to ensuring the accuracy, code compliance, and professional adequacy of all documents bearing the architect's signature and seal. Delegation of drawing preparation to junior staff is permitted only when the architect maintains continuous direct involvement in the work and actively supervises the junior staff's design decisions and drawing development.

### Coordination and Integration of Multi-Disciplinary Documents

The various drawings and specifications must be coordinated to ensure consistency and prevent conflicts. The architect typically serves as the lead designer, and specialists coordinate their work with the architectural design. Architectural drawings establish the spatial parameters, floor heights, structural grid dimensions, and architectural requirements that the engineers must accommodate. Structural engineers then design the frame to fit within the architectural space and to support the loads the architect's design will impose.

Mechanical and electrical engineers must coordinate with both the architect and structural engineer to route their systems through available spaces without conflicting with the architectural or structural elements. A mechanical engineer who discovers that the architect's ceiling height is insufficient for the required ductwork, or that the structural system prevents required system routing, must communicate these conflicts during design development so that alternatives can be explored. The final drawings must represent a coordinated design where all systems integrate into a cohesive building that meets the architect's design intent.

### Specifications and Supporting Documents

Specifications complement the drawings by describing materials, finishes, installation methods, quality standards, and performance requirements that the drawings cannot fully communicate. The architect typically prepares or coordinates the general specification sections covering general conditions, contract procedures, and site management. Specialists prepare technical specifications within their respective disciplines describing equipment, materials, and installation standards for mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems.

The bill of materials, cost estimates, and other bid documents are prepared based on the drawings and specifications. The architect or estimator prepares these documents based on the scope of work shown in the architectural and engineer drawings and described in the specifications. These documents must accurately reflect the design intent and construction scope so that contractors can prepare accurate bids and understand the full scope of work required.

### Professional Liability and Document Responsibility

When a professional signs and seals a document, that professional assumes direct responsibility for its accuracy, completeness, code compliance, and professional adequacy. If the document contains errors, omissions, or non-compliance with building codes, the responsible professional is liable for any resulting construction defects, cost overruns, or safety issues. A structural engineer who stamps structural drawings later found to be inadequate for the loads imposed faces potential liability for structural failure, injury, or property damage.

This responsibility makes the direct supervision requirement essential. An architect cannot safely delegate signing and sealing to others or sign documents without thorough review and personal responsibility. The professional's insurance carrier (professional liability insurance) covers claims arising from negligence in the preparation of documents, but only if the professional exercised the care and diligence required of a competent professional. Documents signed without proper supervision or review, or documents containing obvious errors that reasonable review would have caught, may fall outside insurance coverage and expose the professional to uninsured personal liability.
