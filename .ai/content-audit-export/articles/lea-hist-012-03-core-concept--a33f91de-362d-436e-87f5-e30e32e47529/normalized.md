# North American Indigenous Architecture

## LEA Point

North American Indigenous architecture is regional. The LEA exam usually tests whether you can separate Southwestern Pueblo masonry/adobe, Haudenosaunee longhouses, Pacific Northwest plank longhouses, and Mississippian earthworks.

## Source Anchors

- Pueblo Bonito: NPS anchors the Chaco great-house pattern, including a large D-shaped plan and hundreds of rooms.
- Mesa Verde cliff dwellings: NPS anchors alcove settlement, masonry rooms, kivas, and water/shelter logic.
- Taos Pueblo: UNESCO anchors an enduring adobe, multi-story pueblo tradition.
- Haudenosaunee longhouse: Confederacy material anchors extended-family housing with compartments and a central corridor; keep gender/property claims source-safe and tied to matrilineal organization.
- Cahokia and Monks Mound: UNESCO anchors Cahokia as a major Mississippian center and Monks Mound as the largest prehistoric earthen structure in the New World, rising about 30 m.

## Exam Pattern

If the clue says viga, latilla, adobe, kiva, Pueblo Bonito, Mesa Verde, or Taos, think Pueblo/Southwest. If it says bark-covered extended-family longhouse with compartments, think Haudenosaunee. If it says platform mound, Cahokia, or Monks Mound, think Mississippian earthwork.

## Guardrails

- Do not turn every longhouse into the same form. Pacific Northwest plank longhouses and Haudenosaunee bark longhouses express different materials and social patterns.
- Do not use the old two-phase radiocarbon Monks Mound item as a core answer. The source-safe Batch 126 point is scale, earthwork construction, and Mississippian civic/ceremonial organization.
- Do not confuse adobe thermal mass with solid stone masonry.

## Real World Scenario

A preservation plan for Taos Pueblo focuses on earthen wall maintenance and living-community continuity. A Mesa Verde cliff dwelling plan focuses on alcove masonry, water access, and archaeological stabilization. A Cahokia mound plan focuses on earthwork erosion and site-scale management.
