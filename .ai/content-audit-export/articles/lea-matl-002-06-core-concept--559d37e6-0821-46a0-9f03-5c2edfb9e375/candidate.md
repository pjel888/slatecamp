## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal

Classify concrete admixtures (water reducers, retarders, accelerators, superplasticizers, air-entraining, pozzolans) by function and explain how each modifies fresh or hardened concrete properties to meet specific construction requirements.
## Core Concept

Admixtures allow engineers to fine-tune concrete performance for specific project requirements. A water reducer might lower water-cement ratio (increasing strength), while an accelerator speeds early strength for rapid form removal. Each admixture type has dosage limits and secondary effects; exceeding dosage can cause slump loss, segregation, or reduced durability.

The most commonly used admixtures in the Philippines are water reducers (to increase strength and reduce cost) and accelerators (to speed form removal in warm climates).

## ASTM C494 Chemical Admixtures for Concrete

ASTM C494 is the primary standard for admixture specifications and testing. It defines seven types (A through G) by primary and secondary effects:

## Type A: Water-Reducing Admixtures

Type A admixtures reduce the water required to achieve a target slump (workability), allowing engineers to either maintain the same water-cement ratio and increase strength, or reduce both water and cement while maintaining strength.

**Mechanism:** Water reducers contain surfactants (surface-active compounds) that coat cement particles, reducing friction between particles and improving particle packing. This allows concrete to flow with less free water.

**Typical dosage:** 0.3—1.0% by mass of cement.

**Effects:**
- Reduces water demand by 5—10% for the same slump
- Increases 28-day strength by 5—15% when water is reduced (holding cement constant)
- Minimal effect on set time
- Can increase shrinkage if dosage is excessive

**Applications:** General construction where moderate strength gain is desired without accelerating or retarding set time.

**Caution:** Exceeding dosage (>1.0%) can cause excessive slump loss, segregation, and bleeding.

## Type B: Retarding Admixtures

Type B admixtures delay the initial and final set times, slowing hydration. They are used in hot-weather concreting where rapid set would cause premature stiffening and difficulties with placement and finishing.

**Mechanism:** Retarders absorb onto cement particle surfaces, forming a barrier that slows water penetration and ion release, delaying the hydration process.

**Typical dosage:** 0.05—0.30% by mass of cement.

**Effects:**
- Delays initial set by 1—6 hours (variable by product and dosage)
- Delays final set proportionally
- May reduce early strength (1—3 days) due to delayed hydration
- At 7 and 28 days, strength is typically equivalent to non-retarded concrete because additional hydration time compensates
- Slump retention is improved in hot weather (workability extends)

**Applications:** Large pours, hot-weather concreting, slipform construction where extended workability is needed.

**Limit:** Excessive retardation (>0.50%) can prevent the concrete from ever fully setting; use with caution.

## Type C: Accelerating Admixtures

Type C admixtures speed hydration, accelerating set time and early strength development. Calcium chloride is the traditional accelerator (now often replaced due to chloride ion concerns in reinforced concrete).

**Mechanism:** Accelerators provide catalytic ions (typically Ca²⁺ or OH⁻) that increase hydration rate, or chemically combine with cement phases, advancing the reaction.

**Typical dosage:** 0.5—2.0% by mass of cement (for calcium chloride replacement). Modern chloride-free accelerators: 1—2% by mass.

**Effects:**
- Reduces initial set by 30—60% (from typical 6—8 hours to 3—4 hours)
- Increases 1-day strength by 50—100% (dramatic acceleration of early strength)
- 7 and 28-day strengths are typically equivalent to non-accelerated concrete
- Increases heat of hydration (warm feeling during curing); risk of thermal cracking if cooling is not controlled

**Applications:** Cold-weather concreting, accelerated form removal, repair of concrete damage where rapid strength is critical.

**Critical limitation:** Calcium chloride accelerators (>1.0% dosage) are NOT recommended for reinforced concrete because chloride ions migrate to rebar and cause corrosion. Non-chloride accelerators (sodium formate, other compounds) are preferred or required by NSCP 2015 in reinforced structures.

<Note>NSCP 2015 Section 1903 restricts chloride-based accelerators in reinforced concrete. Always specify chloride-free accelerators or document that reinforcement is absent.</Note>

## Type D: Water Reducing + Retarding

Type D combines the benefits of water reduction and retardation. Used in hot-weather concreting where strength gain is desired (water reduction benefit) but rapid set must be avoided (retardation benefit).

**Typical dosage:** 0.5—1.2% by mass of cement.

**Effects:**
- Reduces water by 5—10% (Type A benefit)
- Delays set by 1—4 hours (Type B benefit)
- Net effect: Improved workability and strength gain in hot conditions

**Applications:** Hot-weather large pours, complex shapes requiring extended working time with strength enhancement.

## Type E: Water Reducing + Accelerating

Type E combines water reduction with acceleration, used in cold-weather concreting where strength gain (water reduction) and rapid early strength (acceleration) are both desired.

**Typical dosage:** 0.7—1.5% by mass of cement.

**Effects:**
- Reduces water by 5—10%
- Accelerates early strength (1—3 days)
- At 28 days, strength is approximately 10—20% higher than baseline due to water reduction

**Applications:** Cold-weather concreting in structures requiring rapid form removal and early strength for construction sequencing.

## Type F and G: High-Range Water Reducers (Superplasticizers)

Superplasticizers (F and G) reduce water demand much more significantly than Types A—E, enabling self-consolidating concrete and flowing concrete without vibration.

**Mechanism:** Superplasticizers provide stronger surfactant action than standard water reducers, dispersing cement particles more effectively and allowing lower water-cement ratios (down to 0.30 or lower).

**Typical dosage:** 0.5—2.0% by mass of cement (higher than Type A due to greater potency per unit mass, but achieving larger water reductions).

**Effects:**
- Reduces water by 15—30% for the same slump
- Can achieve slump of 10+ inches without segregation (self-consolidating concrete)
- Early strength (1—3 days) may be reduced due to low water-cement ratio's sensitivity to curing
- 28-day strength can be 20—40% higher than baseline due to reduced water-cement ratio and improved particle packing
- May cause slump loss over time (increasing workability for 30—60 minutes, then decreasing)

**Type F (without retardation):** Used for flowing concrete in normal or cold weather.

**Type G (with retardation):** Used for flowing concrete in hot weather, extending slump retention.

**Applications:** Architectural concrete with complex forms, self-consolidating concrete (SCC) for efficient placement, high-strength concrete.

**Caution:** Excessive superplasticizer (>2.0%) can cause foam formation, excessive slump loss, and segregation. Compatibility with other admixtures and cement type varies; trial mixes are recommended.

## ASTM C260: Air-Entraining Agents

Air entrainment intentionally introduces microscopic air bubbles (typically 2—8% by volume, with bubble diameter 10—1000 micrometers) into concrete for freeze-thaw protection.

**Mechanism:** Air-entraining agents are surfactants that stabilize air bubbles during mixing. The bubbles remain distributed throughout the hardened concrete.

**Typical dosage:** 0.02—0.1% by mass of cement.

**Effects:**
- Each bubble creates a relief space for water expansion during freezing, preventing ice lens formation and spalling
- Freeze-thaw durability improves dramatically (300+ freeze-thaw cycles vs. &lt;50 for non-entrained)
- Compressive strength is reduced approximately 3—5% per 1% air (trade-off: durability for strength)
- Workability improves; less water is needed for the same slump
- Segregation and bleeding are reduced

**Applications:** Concrete exposed to freezing in wet conditions (northern climates, high-altitude regions). Less critical in tropical Philippines where freezing is rare, but may be specified near mountain areas or for long-term durability.

**Air entrainment is NOT recommended (and inappropriate) for concrete not exposed to freeze-thaw.** In the Philippines, unless a structure is exposed to cold mountain climate or freezing conditions, air entrainment is not specified and would waste the strength reduction.

## Dosage Ranges and Compound Effects

When multiple admixtures are combined (water reducer + accelerator, for example), their effects are not always linear. Incompatibilities can occur, reducing effectiveness or causing unintended consequences.

**Recommended practice:**
1. Specify only the admixtures necessary for the project
2. Always conduct trial mixes to verify performance
3. Maintain consistent dosage; variation in admixture amount changes concrete properties
4. Verify compatibility; some combinations are known to be incompatible (document in specifications)
5. Monitor concrete slump and air content on-site; adjust dosage if necessary

## How It's Tested

The LEA expects you to:

1. Identify admixture types by primary and secondary effects
2. Apply ASTM C494 classifications and dosage ranges
3. Determine appropriate admixture for specific project conditions (hot weather, cold weather, accelerated form removal)
4. Predict effects on slump, set time, strength development, and durability
5. Recognize incompatibilities and limitations (e.g., chloride accelerators in reinforced concrete)
6. Calculate admixture dosage based on cement content
7. Understand why excessive or insufficient dosage causes concrete defects

Typical questions present a concrete application (hot-weather pour, cold-weather placement, self-consolidating concrete requirement) and ask which admixture type is appropriate, or ask about effects on strength, set time, or durability.

<Tip>Always remember ASTM C494 types A—G. Type A = water reduction. Type B = retardation. Type C = acceleration. Types D and E combine two effects. Types F and G = superplasticizers. Each letter reflects both primary purpose and secondary effects, which are non-negotiable in the specification.</Tip>
