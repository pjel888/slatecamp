## What You Need to Know for the LEA

## Learning Goal
Read contour maps to determine slope steepness, calculate cut and fill volumes for building pads, design grading for positive drainage (2% minimum), identify erosion control measures, and apply grading constraints from environmental regulations.

Site grading is the process of rearranging earth material (cut and fill) to accommodate buildings and infrastructure, adapt to existing topography, and manage surface drainage. Grading analysis begins with contour map reading—closely spaced contours indicate steep terrain; widely spaced contours indicate flat terrain. Slope analysis determines buildability, erosion risk, and visibility.

## Core Concept

Grading refers to the modification of site terrain through excavation (cut) and placement of soil (fill) to create building pads, roads, drainage, and landscape features. Effective grading balances multiple objectives: minimize excavation volume (cost), accommodate building elevations, control stormwater runoff, stabilize slopes, and respect environmental constraints (no grading in water body easements per PD 1067, sensitive slopes per geotechnical study).

Slope analysis begins with contour line interpretation. Contour lines represent elevation at constant intervals (commonly 1 meter or 5 meters). Where contours are closely spaced, terrain is steep; where widely spaced, terrain is gentle. A slope's steepness is expressed as a percentage (rise ÷ run × 100) or as a ratio (1V:3H means for every 1 meter of vertical rise, 3 meters of horizontal distance). Slope determines cost and buildability: slopes > 25% are typically expensive to build on (require retaining walls, deep excavation); slopes > 40% may be unbuildable or require specialized foundations.

Cut-and-fill balance is a design goal: material excavated in one area (cut) is reused for fill elsewhere on the site, minimizing borrow and waste. However, cut and fill are not equal: soil compacts differently when placed as fill vs. in-situ; 100 cubic meters of excavated soil may compact to 85 cubic meters when used as fill.

## Philippine Context

Philippine sites are highly varied in topography: coastal plains in Metro Manila, hilly terrain around Lake Laguna, steep slopes in mountain provinces, and volcanic slopes near Tagaytay. Contour maps (typically from NAMRIA or municipal engineering offices) are starting points for site analysis. Geotechnical investigation is essential: Philippine soils include sensitive clay (Quezon area), volcanic ash (Laguna), and laterite (Davao). Cut-and-fill design must account for monsoon rains and landslide risk in steep areas. PD 1067 water body easements frequently intersect residential sites, restricting fill and structure placement. Slope stabilization (terracing, revetments, soil nails) is standard practice in hilly subdivisions.

## How It's Tested

**Pattern 1 — Contour interpretation:** "A topographic map shows contour lines spaced 2 meters apart horizontally and 1 meter apart vertically over a 50-meter distance. Is this terrain steep or gentle?" → 50m horizontal distance with 1m vertical rise = slope of 2% = gentle. (Closely spaced contours would show small horizontal distances for each vertical meter.)

**Pattern 2 — Slope calculation:** "A site rises 15 meters over a horizontal distance of 60 meters. What is the slope percentage?" → (15 ÷ 60) × 100 = 25% slope.

**Pattern 3 — Grading constraint:** "A site is adjacent to a PD 1067-protected water body. How does this constrain grading?" → No fill into the easement; no permanent structures; grading must respect setback.
