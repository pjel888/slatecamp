## What You Need to Know for the LEA
## Learning Goal

Classify sealants (silicone, polyurethane, polysulfide, acrylic, butyl) and caulks by movement capability, adhesion properties, weathering resistance, and application for different joint types and building substrates in the Philippine climate.
## Core Concept

Sealants differ from caulks by durability and movement: Sealants are elastomeric (flexible), lasting 10-20 years, with high tensile strength (1.5-3.0 MPa) and elongation (100-400%). Caulks are acrylic latex or silicone-based, lasting 3-7 years, lower cost, suitable for non-moving joints. Four primary sealant chemistries exist: (1) Silicone (ASTM C920, C1733) offers superior UV/temperature stability (-40 to +200°C), high elongation (300-400%), and non-staining (non-corrosive), but poor adhesion to some substrates and high cost (2× polyurethane). (2) Polyurethane (ASTM C920, C1619) provides excellent substrate adhesion, high tensile strength, rapid cure (24 hrs walkable vs 7 days silicone), and broad chemical compatibility, but requires careful moisture/cure management. (3) Polysulfide (ASTM C920) offers superior fuel/chemical resistance (fuel-resistant for aircraft/marine), high elongation, but slower cure and distinct odor. (4) Acrylic Latex (ASTM C834) is water-based, environmentally friendly, low odor, but lowest durability and unsuitable for wet/movement-heavy joints.

Joint design is critical: width-to-depth ratio of 2:1 (not 1:2) means for a 10 mm wide joint, sealant depth = 5 mm (joint width 10 mm ÷ 2 = 5 mm depth). Backer rod (foam or rope) behind sealant prevents three-sided bonding and allows proper stress distribution. For joints exceeding 1.5 inches (38 mm) wide or deeper than 0.5 inches (12.7 mm), consider compression seals or field-molded systems instead of gun-applied sealants. Movement capability is characterized by Class per ASTM C920: Class 100/50 provides 100% extension / 50% compression movement capability per ASTM C920; Class 50 = ±50%; Class 25 = ±25%; Class 12.5 = ±12.5%. For example, a 10 mm joint in Class 50 sealant can accommodate 5 mm expansion and 5 mm contraction (±50% movement capability).

Tropical cure considerations include high humidity (80-100% RH), which accelerates polyurethane but slows silicone and acrylic evaporative cure. Temperature (25-35°C ambient) activates polyurethane isocyanate chemistry but may cause "skin-over" (surface cure before deep cure is complete) in thick applications. Substrate moisture compatibility varies: polyurethane tolerates damp substrates; silicone and acrylic require dry surfaces.

## Philippine Construction Context

Philippine construction employs ASTM C920 silicone sealants for high-movement building joints, window/door perimeters, and interior expansion joints (Class 50 standard). Polyurethane sealants are increasingly specified for hybrid glass-metal curtain walls due to superior adhesion and rapid cure in tropical humidity. Acrylic latex (interior paintable caulk) is widely used for drywall/plaster joints and trim, despite short service life (3-5 years). Tropical challenges include (a) rapid wet/dry cycling causing joint widening/narrowing (±10% seasonal variation in concrete width), (b) salt-air corrosivity requiring silicone (non-corrosive) over polysulfide, (c) high UV exposure accelerating acrylic degradation (yellowing, loss of elasticity), and (d) high-humidity cure inhibition for silicone (requires active ventilation/dehumidification during cure). Backer rod installation is frequently omitted due to cost and labor unfamiliarity, resulting in excessive sealant depth (poor stress distribution, rapid failure).

## How It's Tested

**Pattern 1: Joint Design Verification** — "A building expansion joint is 12 mm wide. What is the required sealant depth per ASTM C920 (2:1 ratio), and what backer rod diameter is required?" (Answer: Depth = 6 mm (12 mm ÷ 2); backer rod ≈ 15-18 mm diameter (1.25-1.5× joint width).)

**Pattern 2: Sealant Type Selection** — "A fuel storage tank in a coastal area requires perimeter joint sealing. Temperature range is -5 to +50°C with frequent wet/dry cycling. Which sealant chemistry is most appropriate?" (Answer: Polysulfide per ASTM C920; fuel-resistant, high elongation, salt-air durable. Silicone is alternative but less fuel-resistant.)

**Pattern 3: Movement Class Specification** — "A concrete floor slab in a tropical building experiences ±30 mm seasonal width variation over a 10 meter span (0.3% strain). What minimum Class sealant is required?" (Answer: Class 50 (±50% or ±5 mm for 10 mm wide joint is conservative for 0.3% strain over entire span). Joint width designed for expected ±30 mm movement; Class 50 accommodates ±50% of nominal width (e.g., 60 mm joint handles ±30 mm), which exceeds practical gun-application limits; consider compression seal or staged joint design.)

**Pattern 4: Cure Compatibility** — "A polyurethane sealant is applied to a damp masonry wall (90% RH) in Manila at 30°C ambient. Will cure time be shorter or longer compared to dry conditions at 20°C?" (Answer: Cure time shorter; elevated temperature (30°C) and damp substrate accelerate isocyanate polymerization. Polyurethane is superior to silicone for this application.)
