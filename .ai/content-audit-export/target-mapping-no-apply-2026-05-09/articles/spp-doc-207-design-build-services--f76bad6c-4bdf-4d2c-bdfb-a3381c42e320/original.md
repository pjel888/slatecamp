## Overview

**SPP Document 207** governs **Design-Build Services (DBS)** — the project delivery method in which the Architect assumes professional responsibility and civil liability for **both the design and the construction** of a project. It replaces the **1979 UAP Doc. 207** and forms part of the Supplemental Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRR) of **Republic Act No. 9266** (The Architecture Act of 2004).

## Learning Goal

After reading this article, you should be able to:

Identify the architect's role, responsibilities, and compensation under SPP Doc 207 Design-Build Services; distinguish design-build (single contract with design-build entity) from traditional design-bid-build (owner-architect + owner-contractor contracts); explain the architect's ethical obligation to maintain professional independence even under a design-build delivery; and apply the percentage-of-construction-cost or lump-sum compensation basis.
---
SPP 207 is unique among the SPP documents because it fundamentally changes the Architect's legal position in the project: instead of acting as the Owner's agent who oversees a separate contractor, the Architect under DBS **directly assumes construction responsibilities** — managing resources, procuring materials, hiring personnel, and in certain modes, guaranteeing the project cost. This document also carries significant legal implications with respect to **Republic Act No. 4566** (the Contractors' License Law) and the need for the Architect to maintain a distinct professional identity even while performing construction management functions.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Design-Build rationale: Time, Cost, Quality

Every building project requires a balance of three competing objectives:

- **Time**: Delivering the project within the required schedule
- **Cost**: Executing the project within budget
- **Quality**: Achieving acceptable performance and design standards

In many projects — particularly those with tight delivery schedules, complex coordination requirements, or limited owner capacity — this balance **can best be achieved by the Architect performing Design-Build Services**. The building industry and the architectural profession have devised several project delivery methods with the ultimate goal of handling projects in the **shortest possible time, at the lowest possible cost, and at acceptable quality and performance**.

### 1.2 Why Architects Are Qualified for Design-Build

The Architect's qualification for Design-Build derives from three core competencies:

1. **Education and training**: Architects are trained to understand both the design and the technical/constructional aspects of buildings — unlike pure designers who are unfamiliar with construction logistics, or pure contractors who are unfamiliar with design intent
2. **Expertise across the full project lifecycle**: From conceptualization to implementation, the Architect has the knowledge base to manage all project phases
3. **Single-point accountability**: Design-Build simplifies the project structure by eliminating the gap between designer and constructor — the client deals with one responsible professional for both design quality and construction delivery

## 2. Scope of Design-Build Services

SPP 207 defines two distinct modes of Design-Build Services, each with different risk profiles, cost structures, and compensation arrangements.

### 2.1 Design-Build Services by Administration (DBS-A)

#### Scope Structure

The scope of Design-Build Services by Administration is based on the Architect's Regular Design Services (SPP 202) — it includes all five design phases:

- **2.1.1** Project Definition Phase
- **2.1.2** Schematic Design Phase
- **2.1.3** Design Development Phase
- **2.1.4** Contract Document Phase
- **2.1.5** Construction Phase

#### The Expanded Construction Phase

What distinguishes DBS-A from SPP 202 is what happens during the **Construction Phase**. Under SPP 202, the Architect makes periodic site visits to observe construction. Under SPP 207 DBS-A, the Construction Phase **goes far beyond periodic inspection** — the Architect takes on direct construction administration responsibilities:

**a. Preparation of schedule of work, program and estimates**
Includes detailed estimates of materials, labor, transportation, equipment, and services as reference for construction planning and cost control.

**b. Organization and hiring of construction personnel**
The Architect hires and designates duties and remunerations for construction workers, site staff, and supervisors — acting as the employer of record for the site workforce.

**c. Negotiation and contracting with piecework contractors**
Identifies, negotiates with, and enters into contracts with piecework (pakiao) contractors; evaluates and approves their work accomplishments for payment.

**d. Procurement of materials, plants and equipment, licenses and permits**
Purchases all building materials, rents or purchases construction equipment, and secures all construction-related licenses and permits from regulatory authorities.

**e. Authorizing and undertaking payments of accounts**
Reviews and approves all payment claims from workers, contractors, and suppliers; authorizes disbursement from the Owner's revolving fund.

**f. Keeping records and books of accounts**
Maintains complete financial records of all construction expenditures — materials, labor, equipment, overhead — for transparency and periodic Owner audit.

**g. Negotiation with Government and private agencies**
Represents the Owner in dealings with LGUs, DPWH, DENR, utilities, and any private agency having jurisdiction over the project (permits, clearances, right-of-way, utility connections).

**h. Management of all other business transactions**
Handles all other business activities related to the project's construction and implementation — a catch-all provision that gives the Architect full administrative authority over the project.

#### The Revolving Fund

A critical operational provision: **the Architect does NOT advance any money** for payment of construction expenditures. Instead:

- The Client **directly pays all costs** for labor and materials
- A **revolving fund** is given to the Architect beforehand as a working capital for day-to-day site expenditures
- The revolving fund is **accounted for and subject to periodic auditing** by the Client

This revolving fund mechanism is the operational basis for the DBS-A model — it ensures the Architect maintains financial accountability while the Owner retains ultimate control over project funds.

### 2.2 Design-Build Services with Guaranteed Maximum Project Construction Cost (DBS-GMPCC)

#### 2.2.1 Basic Structure

The DBS-GMPCC method is **essentially the same as DBS-A** in terms of service scope — the Architect performs all eight construction administration tasks — **except** that the Architect additionally **provides the Owner a guaranteed maximum project construction cost** for the construction. This adds a financial commitment layer on top of the administration role.

#### 2.2.2 The Savings-Sharing Mechanism

If, upon completion of the project, there is a **realized savings** from the estimated project construction cost:

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0003 component="Callout" status="preserved"]
Extracted summary:
- Props: variant="note"
- Text: *"The savings shall be divided equally between the Owner/Client and the Architect."*
[/SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK]


This 50/50 savings split is a powerful incentive for the Architect to find cost efficiencies, negotiate better material prices, and optimize construction methods — because the Architect directly benefits from every peso saved.

#### 2.2.3 The 10% Cost Overrun Rule

The project construction cost is guaranteed by the Architect **not to exceed 10% of the estimated project construction cost**. The liability rule is as follows:

- If the actual cost **≤ estimated + 10%**: Within acceptable tolerance — no liability for the Architect on overruns within this band
- If the actual cost **> estimated + 10%**: The Architect is **liable for the excess amount**, but **only up to the amount of his administration fee**

This liability cap is architecturally significant: the Architect's exposure is capped at the administration fee — the Architect cannot lose more than the fee earned for administering the project.

#### 2.2.4 Force Majeure and Legitimate Cost Escalations

Additional expenditures beyond the GMPCC that are **NOT attributable to the Architect's fault** shall be **borne by the Owner/Client**. Legitimate exceptions include:

- **Change/Variation Orders (CVOs)** — changes in scope instructed by the Owner
- **Extra Work Orders (EWOs)** — additional work not included in the original contract
- **Substantial price escalation** of materials or labor, evidenced by data certified by a nationally recognized agency such as **NEDA (National Economic Development Authority)**
- Other causes not attributable to the fault of the Architect

The NEDA certification requirement is important: informal market price increases alone are insufficient — the Architect must be able to demonstrate price escalation through an officially recognized data source to be relieved of liability for cost overruns.

## 3. Manner of Providing Services

### 3.1 Two Modes of Architect Involvement in Construction

The Architect may be involved in construction — including that of his own design — through either of two arrangements:

**3.1.1 Member of or Part of a Construction Entity**
The Architect works **in tandem with** an entity that will construct his design — acting as the design authority within a design-build firm or consortium. The Architect and the constructing entity collaborate, with the Architect having authorized the entity to construct his design.

**3.1.2 State-Licensed Contractor**
The Architect is **himself a State-licensed contractor** implementing his own design (or that of others). This requires the Architect to hold a valid contractor's license under **Republic Act No. 4566** (the Contractors' License Law) — a separate professional credential from the Architect's PRC registration.

### 3.2 Mandatory Professional Identity Safeguards

Regardless of which arrangement is adopted, the Architect must strictly adhere to two conditions:

#### 3.2.1 Retention of Professional Identity

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0005 component="Callout" status="preserved"]
Extracted summary:
- Props: variant="note"
- Text: *"The Architect must retain his separate/distinct professional identity, prerogatives and integrity as an Architect, and is therefore subject to the standards and tenets of the SPP, particularly Document 200 (Code of Ethical Conduct) and SPP Document 202."*
[/SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK]


This provision prevents the Architect from "becoming" a contractor in the legal sense. Even while managing construction under SPP 207, the Architect remains a registered professional subject to PRC oversight, not merely a business entity subject only to DTI or SEC regulation.

#### 3.2.2 Conflict of Interest Disclosure

The Architect must adhere to the Architect's Credo, with the pledge that he:

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0006 component="Callout" status="preserved"]
Extracted summary:
- Props: variant="note"
- Text: *"...shall disclose whenever required, any business investment or venture that may tend to create a conflict of interest, and ensure that such conflict neither compromises the legitimate interest of the Client nor interfere with his duty to render impartial judgment."*
[/SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK]


This conflict of interest provision is particularly important in DBS-GMPCC, where the Architect has a direct financial stake in project cost performance (through the savings-sharing mechanism). The Architect must be transparent about this financial interest and ensure it does not distort material selection, subcontractor evaluation, or construction quality decisions.

## 4. Method of Compensation

### 4.1 Payment Flow: The Owner Pays All Costs

The method of payment under SPP 207 fundamentally differs from SPP 202:

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0007 component="Callout" status="preserved"]
Extracted summary:
- Props: variant="note"
- Text: *"The manner of payment to the Architect follows the progress of construction. The Client directly pays all costs for labor and materials. The Architect does not advance any money for payment of expenditures connected with the work."*
[/SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK]


This is critical: the Architect is an **administrator**, not a financier. The Owner retains financial control; the Architect exercises administrative and professional authority over how the money is spent.

### 4.2 Permits, Licenses, and Incidentals

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0008 component="Callout" status="preserved"]
Extracted summary:
- Props: variant="note"
- Text: *"The Owner/Client shall pay for the cost of all permits, licenses and other incidentals to the work."*
[/SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK]


All government fees (Building Permit, Electrical Permit, Sanitary Permit, Fire Safety Inspection Certificate, etc.) are **Owner's cost**, not deducted from the Architect's administration fee.

### 4.3 Construction Superintendent and Support Staff

The Architect may appoint — subject to the Owner/Client's approval — a construction superintendent, purchasing agent, timekeeper, and property clerk.

- Props: variant="note"
- Text: *"Salaries of such persons are paid by the Owner/Client and NOT deductible from the Architect's Fee under this SPP."*

This means the Owner bears the cost of the site management team — it is separate from the Architect's administration fee. The Architect's 7% (or 10%) RPF covers professional services, not the salaries of site staff.

## 5. The SPP 207–RA 4566 Interface

One of the most legally nuanced aspects of SPP 207 practice is its relationship to **Republic Act No. 4566** — the Contractors' License Law.

### 5.1 Licensed Professional Practice vs. Contracting

R.A. 4566 requires a **contractor's license** for any person or entity undertaking construction work for compensation. However, the law also **exempts licensed professionals** acting within the scope of their professional practice.

The distinction is critical for architects under SPP 207:

[SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK:ui_0013 component="ComparisonTable" status="preserved"]
Extracted summary:
- Props: (none)
- Text: | Activity | Classification | License Required | |---|---|---| | Architect administering construction under DBS-A with Owner's revolving fund | **Licensed Professional Practice** — exempt from RA 4566 | PRC Architect's license only | | Architect or firm signing a construction contract committing to deliver a completed building for a fixed lump sum (turnkey) | **Contracting** — subject to RA 4566 | Requires PCAB Contractor's License |
[/SLATECAMP_UI_BLOCK]


Under SPP 207 DBS-A, the architect acts as the **Owner's agent** and **administrator** — the Owner pays all costs directly through the revolving fund. This is the structural mechanism that keeps DBS-A within professional practice rather than contracting.

## 7. Practical Notes for Architects and Clients

**For Architects:**

- The **revolving fund mechanism is the legal and operational backbone** of DBS-A. It must be properly documented with a formal revolving fund agreement, clear replenishment procedures, and periodic audit rights for the Owner. Without this structure, the arrangement may be re-characterized as unlicensed contracting under RA 4566.
- Under DBS-GMPCC, the **50/50 savings split** is an incentive but also a temptation. Architects must ensure that cost savings are achieved through genuine efficiencies — better procurement, smarter construction methods, value engineering — and never through reduction of design quality, material specification, or workmanship standards.
- The **10% liability cap on overruns** under GMPCC protects the Architect from catastrophic financial exposure, but means that fee risk and service quality are tightly linked. The GMPCC fee of 10% of PCC must be sufficient to absorb normal project contingencies and still leave a reasonable professional return.
- Remember: the **Regular Design Services fee under SPP 202 is always charged separately** — the 7% or 10% DBS administration fee covers only the construction phase administration, not the design phases.

**For Clients / Project Owners:**

- **DBS-A is best for owners who trust the architect's judgment and want simplicity**: one professional to deal with, full design and construction integration, and lower transaction costs from not having to manage a separate contractor relationship.
- **DBS-GMPCC is best for cost-sensitive owners**: the guaranteed maximum cost provides budget certainty, the 50/50 savings sharing creates a genuine incentive for the Architect to be cost-efficient, and the 10% tolerance band is a reasonable contingency for any project.
- Owners should always verify whether the Architect is engaging construction workers **as employees** (under the revolving fund model) or as an independent contractor (which may require PCAB licensing). The former is proper SPP 207 practice; the latter may constitute unlicensed contracting.
- The Owner retains the right to **audit the revolving fund** — this should be exercised regularly (monthly or quarterly) throughout construction to maintain cost visibility and catch any financial discrepancies early.
