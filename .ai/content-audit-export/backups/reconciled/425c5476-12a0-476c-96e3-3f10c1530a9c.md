## Reimbursable Expenses — What Is Reimbursable, What Is Absorbed, and How to Bill


## Learning Goal

After studying this article, you should be able to:

- Define reimbursable expenses and what qualifies.
- Distinguish reimbursable expenses (billable to client) from absorbed expenses (included in fee).
- Describe the documentation required for billing reimbursables.
- Apply reimbursement rules to practice expense scenarios.



### Definition of Reimbursable Expenses

Reimbursable expenses are costs the architect directly incurs for the specific project that are separate from the architect's professional services fee. These expenses are itemized and billed separately, with the architect recovering actual costs plus a reasonable markup (typically 10–15%) to cover administrative handling. Reimbursable expenses do not generate profit for the architect; they represent cost recovery.

Reimbursable expenses must be reasonable, documented, directly attributable to the project, and authorized by the client. The architect should not bill costs not previously disclosed and approved by the client. Transparency regarding what costs are reimbursable protects both parties and prevents surprises during invoicing.

### Categories of Reimbursable Expenses

Travel expenses for site visits, client meetings, or consultant coordination are typically reimbursable. This includes airfare, ground transportation (taxi, rental vehicle), accommodation, and meals while traveling for project purposes. Some firms limit reimbursable travel to trips exceeding a specified distance or cost threshold; the contract should specify whether all project travel is reimbursable or only travel for specific purposes.

Printing and copying costs are reimbursable, including cost of printing full sets of contract documents, specifications, and special drawings for distribution to bidders and other parties. Standard office copying (office supplies used in normal business operations) is not reimbursable; only project-specific printing and copying is reimbursable.

Consultant fees retained for specialized services are reimbursable if the architect retains these consultants on the client's behalf. Structural engineer fees, MEP consultant fees, cost estimation fees, sustainability certification fees, or specialized consultants for laboratory design, theater systems, or other specializations are typically reimbursable. However, if the client retains consultants directly (a separate agreement between client and consultant), these are not architectural reimbursable expenses; they are client direct costs.

Model making, rendering preparation, and visualization costs may be reimbursable if specifically authorized by the client. Some clients request physical or computer models for presentation or communication; the actual costs of producing these items are reimbursable.

Site investigation expenses (soil borings, geotechnical surveys, environmental assessments) are reimbursable if the architect arranges and pays for these studies. If the client directly engages soil investigators or environmental consultants, these are client costs; if the architect arranges these services, costs are reimbursable.

Permit and filing fees paid by the architect on the client's behalf are reimbursable. Building permit application fees, heritage conservation permits, environmental clearances, or other regulatory approvals cost money; the architect should be reimbursed for these amounts.

### Categories of Non-Reimbursable (Absorbed) Expenses

Standard office overhead is not reimbursable: office rent, utilities, insurance, administrative staff, office equipment, and general software licenses are absorbed by the architect's fee. These fixed costs of operating the firm are covered by the professional fee, not itemized as project expenses.

General office supplies used in normal business operations are absorbed: basic supplies, general technology costs, communications, routine office maintenance, and general professional memberships are covered by the firm's overhead and are not billed to individual projects.

Routine design work and project coordination are not separately billed; they are covered by the architect's professional fee. Meetings with clients, coordination with consultants, and design development—these are part of the contracted design services and should not be billed as additional expenses. The architect's time is compensated through the fee structure (percentage-of-PCC, MDPE, lump sum, or hourly).

Computer equipment, design software, and BIM licensing are absorbed by the architect's fee. While these technology costs are significant for architecture firms, they are fixed business costs covered by fees, not project-specific reimbursable expenses.

Professional development, training, and conferences are not project reimbursable expenses; they are firm-wide benefits absorbed by the architect's overall business model.

### Markup on Reimbursable Expenses

Many architects apply a markup (typically 10–15%) to reimbursable expenses to cover administrative handling, invoicing, and compliance costs. A consultant fee of 100,000 pesos is invoiced at 110,000–115,000 pesos to cover administrative costs. The markup should be disclosed to the client in the proposal or contract; most clients accept reasonable markups for administrative handling.

Some architects bill reimbursable expenses at actual cost without markup. This approach is simpler but provides no compensation for administrative work. The architect should establish a clear policy and communicate it to the client.

### Tracking and Documentation of Reimbursable Expenses

The architect must document all reimbursable expenses with receipts, invoices, or other evidence of cost. Travel expenses should be documented with airline tickets, hotel receipts, and meal receipts. Consultant fees should be documented with consultant invoices. Printing and copying should be documented with printer or vendor invoices. This documentation allows the architect to substantiate costs and protects against disputes regarding legitimacy of claimed expenses.

Monthly invoicing should itemize reimbursable expenses separately from the professional fee. A typical invoice might read: "Professional Services (Design Development Phase): 1,000,000 pesos; Reimbursable Expenses: 50,000 pesos (structural engineer fee 40,000 + printing 10,000); Total Due: 1,050,000 pesos." Itemizing reimbursable expenses separately from professional fees provides transparency and prevents confusion.

### Pre-Approval of Reimbursable Expenses

The contract should establish that significant reimbursable expenses require client pre-approval. The architect should not unilaterally engage a consultant or incur substantial travel costs without confirming the client will reimburse. A pre-approval mechanism might establish: "Consultant fees require written client approval; travel expenses require advance notification of location and estimated cost; printing costs are reimbursable if not exceeding 25,000 pesos per batch without prior approval."

This approach protects both parties: the client knows what costs are coming and has opportunity to object if they seem excessive, and the architect has documented authorization to proceed with reimbursable expenses.

### Reimbursable Expenses and Fee Comparisons

When comparing fee proposals from different architects, ensure they are structured identically regarding reimbursable expenses. An architect proposing lower professional fees but higher reimbursable expenses might cost more overall than an architect with higher fees but lower expenses. The contract should be clear about what is and is not reimbursable so comparisons are accurate.

Some architects include certain expenses in their professional fees that others treat as reimbursable. For example, small printing jobs are absorbed by one architect but reimbursed by another. The contract should establish clear boundaries to prevent disputes.
