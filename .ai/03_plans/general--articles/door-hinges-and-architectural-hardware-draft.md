# Door Hinges and Architectural Hardware

## Learning Goal

Identify the main types of door hinges and finish hardware, explain what each item does in a door assembly, and choose the correct hardware family for a board-exam or drawing/specification situation.

## Why This Matters on the LEA

Hardware questions look small, but they test whether you can read a door as a working assembly. A door is not only a leaf in an opening. It needs hardware to swing, close, latch, lock, protect adjacent finishes, resist weather, and allow safe use. The usual exam trap is to memorize names without knowing the action: a butt hinge turns a leaf from the jamb edge; a pivot hinge shifts the turning axis; panic hardware releases an exit door by pressure on a horizontal bar; a closer returns the leaf to the closed position.

For board review, sort hardware by function first:

- **Support and movement:** hinges, pivots, tracks, guides.
- **Security and latching:** locksets, latches, bolts, strikes, cylinders, dead bolts.
- **Closing and egress:** closers, panic hardware, coordinators.
- **Operation and protection:** pulls, push plates, kick plates, stops, holders, bumpers.
- **Sealing and threshold conditions:** thresholds and weatherstripping.

Source: George Salvan, *Architectural Building Materials*, hardware section, raw PDF p. 173 and hinge section, raw PDF pp. 177, 181–183. Source: Francis D.K. Ching, *Building Construction Illustrated*, 5th ed., Door Hardware 8.17, Door Hinges 8.18, Door Locksets 8.19, Panic Hardware & Closers 8.20.

## Finishing Hardware vs Rough Hardware

**Hardware** means metal products used in construction, including bolts, hinges, locks, and related fittings. The first useful distinction is between finishing hardware and rough hardware.

**Finishing hardware** is exposed and has both function and appearance. It includes the visible and touchable parts used with doors, windows, and cabinets: hinges, locks, catches, pulls, plates, and similar items. Because it is visible, it belongs in both technical coordination and architectural finish selection.

**Rough hardware** is normally concealed or constructional. Salvan identifies rough hardware as bolts, nails, screws, spikes, rods, and other metal fittings intended to be hidden in the work. These are not chosen for decorative effect. They are chosen for fastening, anchoring, or support.

Exam shortcut: if the user sees, touches, or operates it on the finished door, it is usually finishing hardware. If it is buried, concealed, or used mainly to fasten construction, it is usually rough hardware.

Source: Salvan, *Architectural Building Materials*, hardware section, raw PDF p. 173.

## The Door Hardware Set

A complete door hardware set is a coordinated group of parts, not a random list of accessories. Building Construction Illustrated lists finish door hardware as including locksets, hinges, closers, panic hardware, push and pull bars or plates, kick plates, stops, holders, bumpers, thresholds, weatherstripping, tracks, and guides.

When selecting hardware, BCI gives practical selection factors:

- Function and ease of operation.
- Whether the item is recessed or surface-mounted.
- Material, finish, texture, and color.
- Durability for the expected frequency of use.
- Exposure to weather or corrosive conditions.

That last point matters in Philippine practice. A stainless steel pull or hinge may be justified in a coastal school or resort where salt air attacks ordinary plated finishes. A residential interior door may not need the same exposure rating. The exam version is simpler: match the hardware to the door's function, use, exposure, and required operation.

Source: BCI, 5th ed., Door Hardware 8.17.

## Hinge Fundamentals

A **hinge** is a movable joint that attaches, supports, and turns a door about a pivot. Salvan describes it as two plates joined by a pin that supports the door and connects it to its frame, allowing the door to swing open or closed.

The basic anatomy is:

- **Leaf / plate:** the flat metal plate fastened to the door or frame.
- **Knuckle / barrel:** the rolled cylindrical portion along the hinge edge.
- **Pin:** the rod through the knuckles that creates the turning axis.
- **Mortise:** the recess cut into the door or jamb so the hinge leaf sits flush.
- **Jamb leaf and door leaf:** the two sides of the hinge, one on the frame and one on the door.

The board-exam skill is not to draw every screw. The skill is to know how the door is carried and where the turning axis is located.

Source: Salvan, hinge definition, raw PDF p. 177. Source: BCI, Door Hinges 8.18.

## Main Hinge Types

### Butt Hinge

A **butt hinge** is the standard door hinge. It has two rectangular metal leaves joined by a pin and is secured to the abutting surfaces of the door and jamb. In common door construction, each leaf is usually mortised so the door can close neatly with only the knuckle visible at the edge.

Use it when the door is a normal side-hinged swinging door and there is no special requirement for concealed action, double swing, full-length support, or center pivoting.

Board trap: do not confuse the hinge name with the door type. A flush door, panel door, and metal door may all use butt hinges depending on the frame and specification.

Source: Salvan, butt hinge, raw PDF p. 177. Source: BCI, Door Hinges 8.18.

### Fixed-Pin and Loose-Pin Hinges

A **fixed-pin hinge** has the pin fastened permanently in place. A **loose-pin hinge** has a removable pin that allows the two hinge leaves to be separated, so the door can be removed more easily.

The difference is not the door swing. The difference is whether the pin can be removed. In security-sensitive situations, removable pins may be inappropriate on the exposed side of a door unless the hinge has a security feature. BCI notes that self-locking pins that cannot be removed when the door is closed are also available.

Source: Salvan, fixed-pin and loose-pin hinge entries, raw PDF p. 177. Source: BCI, Door Hinges 8.18.

### Full-Mortise, Half-Mortise, Half-Surface, and Full-Surface Hinges

These terms describe **how the leaves are mounted**.

- **Full-mortise hinge:** both leaves are mortised into the door and jamb; only the knuckle is visible when the door is closed.
- **Half-mortise hinge:** one leaf is mortised into the edge of the door, while the other is surface-mounted to the frame.
- **Half-surface hinge:** one leaf is mortised into the frame, while the other is surface-mounted to the face of the door.
- **Full-surface hinge:** both leaves are surface-mounted to the adjacent faces of the door and frame.

Use this family of terms when the question asks about installation condition, not swing direction.

Source: BCI, Door Hinges 8.18. Source: Salvan, full-surface hinge, raw PDF p. 177.

### Loose-Joint Hinge

A **loose-joint hinge** allows the door to be lifted off the vertical pin without unscrewing the hinge. Salvan describes it as having two knuckles, one with a vertical pin that fits into a corresponding hole in the other.

This is useful when the door or panel must be removable for access, maintenance, or furniture-like construction. The exam trap is to confuse it with a loose-pin hinge. A loose-pin hinge removes the pin; a loose-joint hinge separates by lifting the leaf off the pin.

Source: Salvan, loose-joint hinge, raw PDF p. 177.

### Spring Hinge

A **spring hinge** contains one or more springs that help return the door after opening. Salvan identifies spring hinges as acting in one direction or in both directions. BCI describes spring hinges as having coiled springs in the barrels for closing a door automatically.

A **single-acting spring hinge** assists return in one swing direction. A **double-acting spring hinge** allows the door to swing in either direction and is commonly associated with restaurant, hospital, or kitchen doors where hands may be occupied and traffic moves both ways.

Board trap: a spring hinge is not the same as a full door closer. A door closer is usually a hydraulic or pneumatic device that controls closing action more deliberately. A spring hinge provides spring return but does not necessarily give the same controlled closing performance.

Source: Salvan, spring hinges, raw PDF p. 181. Source: BCI, Door Hinges 8.18.

### Double-Acting Hinge

A **double-acting hinge** permits a door to swing in both directions. BCI notes that double-acting hinges are usually fitted with springs to bring the door back to the closed position after opening.

Use it for doors that must be pushed open from either side, such as service doors between kitchen and dining areas. Do not use it as a generic answer for all high-traffic doors. Some high-traffic doors need closers, panic devices, automatic operators, or sliding systems instead.

Source: BCI, Door Hinges 8.18. Source: Salvan, spring hinges, raw PDF p. 181.

### Pivot Hinge and Floor Hinge

A **pivot hinge** turns the door about a pivot axis rather than relying on a conventional side-jamb hinge barrel. Salvan defines the pivot as the axle or pin about which a door or window rotates. A vertical spring pivot hinge may be mortised into the heel of the door, with the door fastened to the floor and door head with pivots.

BCI identifies **floor hinges** as hardware used with a mortise pivot at the door head to allow a door to swing in either direction; they may include a closer mechanism. This makes pivot/floor hinge assemblies common in heavier or more refined entrance doors, especially where the hardware is intended to be concealed or visually minimized.

Board trap: a pivot door can look like an ordinary swinging door in elevation. The key is the axis of rotation. If the rotation occurs around top and bottom pivots rather than a continuous side-jamb hinge line, it belongs to the pivot family.

Source: Salvan, pivot hinge and vertical spring pivot hinge, raw PDF pp. 181–183. Source: BCI, Door Hinges 8.18.

### Piano / Continuous Hinge

A **piano hinge**, also called a continuous hinge in many specifications, is a long, narrow hinge running the full length of the two joined surfaces. Instead of carrying the door on two or three separated hinges, the load is distributed continuously along the edge.

Use it for cabinet lids, access panels, long doors, or conditions where continuous support and alignment matter. For exam purposes, the word **piano** is the giveaway: long, narrow, full-length hinge.

Source: BCI, Door Hinges 8.18.

### Invisible / Concealed Hinge

An **invisible hinge** uses flat plates rotating about a central pin, with shoulders mortised into the door edge and frame so that the hinge is concealed when the door is closed.

Use it where the design intent is a clean, flush, hidden-joint appearance. In a board question, associate concealed hinges with visual integration, not with panic egress or self-closing behavior unless another hardware item provides that function.

Source: BCI, Door Hinges 8.18.

### Parliament and Olive-Knuckle Hinges

BCI identifies **parliament hinges** as having T-shaped leaves and a protruding knuckle so that a door can stand away from the wall when fully opened. It identifies **olive-knuckle hinges** as hinges with a single pivoting joint and an oval-shaped knuckle. Salvan also records the paumelle/olive-knuckle vocabulary.

These are less common exam answers than butt, spring, pivot, and piano hinges, but they are useful recognition terms. If the question describes a protruding knuckle that lets the door clear wall trim, think parliament hinge. If it describes a refined single oval knuckle, think olive-knuckle/paumelle vocabulary.

Source: BCI, Door Hinges 8.18. Source: Salvan, paumelle and olive-knuckle hinge entries, raw PDF p. 181.

## Other Door Hardware Families

### Locksets

A **lockset** is a complete manufactured lock assembly. Salvan describes it as a complete locking system with the locking mechanism and accessories such as knobs, escutcheons, and plates. BCI groups major lockset types into mortise locks, unit/integral locks, and cylinder locks.

- **Mortise lock:** housed in a mortise cut into the door edge; the mechanism is covered on both sides, with visible faceplate, knobs/levers, cylinder, and trim.
- **Unit / integral lock:** fitted into a prepared door edge condition and combines some security advantages of mortise locks with installation economy.
- **Cylinder lock:** housed within bored holes at right angles, one through the lock stile and one in the door edge.

Board trap: a lockset is not only the knob. It includes the locking mechanism and associated trim.

Source: Salvan, lockset vocabulary, raw PDF pp. 181–183. Source: BCI, Door Locksets 8.19.

### Latches, Bolts, Strikes, and Stops

A **latch** holds the door closed without necessarily locking it. A **dead bolt** projects a solid bolt for security. A **strike** is the receiving plate or keeper on the frame side where the latch or bolt engages. A **door stop** limits movement so the door, wall, or adjacent finish is not damaged.

These items are small, but they are frequently where specification coordination fails. A lock without a properly aligned strike will not latch. A heavy door without a stop may damage the wall. A bolt without correct receiving hardware does not secure anything.

Source: Salvan, door hardware vocabulary, raw PDF pp. 181–183.

### Panic Hardware

**Panic hardware** is a door-latching assembly that disengages when pressure is applied to a horizontal bar across the interior face of an emergency exit door. BCI notes that the push bar spans the interior face of the door at waist height and should extend across at least one-half of the width of the door leaf on which it is installed.

Use this term for egress doors where occupants must be able to release the latch quickly by pushing, especially under crowd or emergency conditions. Do not claim panic hardware is required for every door. BCI states that building codes require panic hardware on emergency egress doors in certain occupancies; the applicable building code controls the exact requirement.

Source: BCI, Panic Hardware & Closers 8.20.

### Door Closers and Coordinators

A **door closer** is a hydraulic or pneumatic device that automatically closes a door quickly but quietly. BCI explains that closers reduce the shock a large, heavy, or heavily used door would transmit to the frame, hardware, and surrounding wall.

Closers may be:

- Surface-mounted at the door head or top jamb.
- Concealed within the door head or frame.
- Mounted on the push side or pull side.
- Concealed within the floor construction for some glass entrance doors.

A **coordinator** is used on a pair of doors so the inactive leaf closes before the active leaf. This matters where the latching sequence must be controlled.

Source: BCI, Panic Hardware & Closers 8.20.

### Push Plates, Pulls, Kick Plates, Stops, Holders, Bumpers, Thresholds, and Weatherstripping

These items make the door usable and durable.

- **Push plate:** protects the face of a push-side door from hand wear.
- **Pull handle / pull bar:** gives the user a graspable element for pulling.
- **Kick plate:** protects the lower door surface from feet, carts, and wheelchair footrests.
- **Stop / holder / bumper:** controls the door's limit of travel or holds it open where appropriate.
- **Threshold:** covers or forms the transition at the bottom of the opening.
- **Weatherstripping:** helps seal against air, water, dust, or sound transmission, depending on the assembly.
- **Track and guide:** controls sliding or rolling door movement.

Board trap: these are not decorative afterthoughts. They are part of the door's performance.

Source: BCI, Door Hardware 8.17.

## How to Choose the Correct Hinge or Hardware in an Exam Question

Use this sequence:

1. **Name the door movement.** Is the leaf side-hinged, double-swinging, pivoting, sliding, folding, or rolling?
2. **Identify the user action.** Push, pull, latch, lock, release for egress, close automatically, hold open, or protect the leaf?
3. **Check visibility and installation.** Mortised, surface-mounted, concealed, full-length, or exposed?
4. **Check traffic and exposure.** High-use public door, residential interior door, coastal exterior opening, service/kitchen door, emergency exit, or cabinet/access panel?
5. **Choose the hardware family.** Hinge, pivot, closer, panic device, lockset, plate, stop, threshold, weatherstripping, track, or guide.

Do not jump from the word “door” to “butt hinge.” A door may need a hinge, pivot, track, closer, lockset, panic device, kick plate, threshold, and weatherstripping in the same opening.

## Real-World Application

Suppose an architect is reviewing the hardware schedule for a small restaurant renovation in Cebu City. The dining-room doors are ordinary side-hinged wood doors, the kitchen service door is pushed through constantly from both sides, the main glass entrance must close without slamming, and the rear exit is part of the emergency egress path.

The architect should not specify one generic hinge for all four conditions. The dining-room doors may use ordinary butt hinges and locksets suitable to their function. The kitchen service door suggests a double-acting spring hinge because staff may pass through while carrying trays or supplies. The glass entrance may need a closer or floor-concealed closer depending on the door system. The rear exit should be checked against the applicable building and fire code requirements before specifying panic hardware, because panic devices are occupancy- and egress-condition dependent.

The practical lesson is coordination. Door hardware is where architecture, security, durability, accessibility, and life safety meet. A beautiful door detail fails if it cannot close, latch, release, resist use, or be operated by the intended user.

## Common Exam Traps

### Trap 1 — Treating a hinge as any metal part on a door

A hinge specifically supports and turns the door. A lockset secures it. A closer returns it. A panic device releases it. A stop limits movement. Name the function before choosing the term.

### Trap 2 — Confusing loose-pin and loose-joint hinges

A loose-pin hinge separates by removing the pin. A loose-joint hinge separates by lifting one part off the vertical pin. Both can help with removal, but the mechanism is different.

### Trap 3 — Confusing spring hinges with door closers

Spring hinges provide spring return. Door closers are hydraulic or pneumatic devices that control closing more deliberately. If the question mentions reducing shock, quiet closing, surface-mounted/concealed closer mechanisms, or coordination of paired leaves, it is pointing to closers rather than ordinary spring hinges.

### Trap 4 — Assuming panic hardware is universal

Panic hardware is associated with emergency egress doors in certain occupancies and conditions. Do not specify it for every door unless the governing code condition requires it.

### Trap 5 — Thinking “finish” means decorative only

Finishing hardware is exposed and often contributes to appearance, but it still performs a technical function. Hinges, locks, pulls, closers, and plates are not ornaments; they make the door assembly work.
